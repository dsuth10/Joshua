The *K–6 Curriculum Planning Model* (K–6CPM) for Australian Curriculum V9 English models a tailored approach to curriculum organisation, supporting systematic and synchronous curriculum implementation in multilevel contexts across Prep to Year 6. This flexible model draws on level descriptions, text types and targeted achievement standard aspects to streamline content and contextualise units and assessments, integrating the strands. Its cyclic design helps sustain engagement and relevance across different context.

Unit 1 focuses on ‘adapting familiar stories’ as a common theme. Unit contexts across successive years may explore a range of literary texts, for example:

* narrative text topics such as family or life-like contexts, imaginary creatures, futuristic or fantasy worlds, social issues, historical or cultural contexts
* a selection of classic and contemporary narrative texts
* literature of wide-ranging Australian and world authors, including that of First Nations Australians and texts from and about Asia.

The K–6CPM includes supplementary resources to support implementation in a range of schools including small schools and larger schools with complex and/or multilevel contexts.

This assessment task is aligned to the aspects of the achievement standard being assessed. Individual school contexts will inform decisions about how the task is to be implemented and how students will engage with the task.

Assessment conventions and resources

Purpose: To read, view and comprehend an imaginative narrative text. To create a written and/or multimodal imaginative text experimenting with ideas from a familiar narrative.

Technique: Short response (Part A); Extended response (Part C).

Conditions:

* Individual work.
* Provide the task to students in a way that is age-appropriate.
* Students independently read a passage (unread) from an imaginative text (Part A).
* Suggested length for Part C: 200–400 words (informed by QCAA guidelines).
* Provide lesson time for drafting with access to teacher feedback and conferencing.
* May be completed over multiple lessons (Part B and Part C).

Implementation advice

* Adapt this assessment task to suit the diverse needs of the students, school and local context.
* When adapting the assessment, maintain alignment to the targeted achievement standard aspects and aligned curriculum elements and consider ways to make the assessment meaningful and accessible for students.
* Answer spaces have not been included to provide flexibility in how and where students will respond.
* Each part of the summative assessment may be conducted as a series of smaller tasks contributing to a collection of work to be used to gather evidence of student learning against the marking guide.
* Assessment offers tasks across varying language modes – listening, speaking, reading, viewing and writing. Teachers can adapt tasks to allow students to demonstrate the full extent of what they know, understand and can do in multiple ways. This approach ensures accessibility for varied learning needs and supports students’ unique ways of seeing, hearing, and/or processing language.

Part A: Reading and viewing

Students read, view and comprehend an imaginative text.

Instructions

* Select a written imaginative narrative text with a social, cultural or historical context that develops ideas in the story through characters, settings and events. This could be a novel or short story.
* Select an appropriate text that students have not read for them to read independently, for example: a chapter of a narrative or a complete short story. The selected text should support text comprehension and be long enough for students to demonstrate their reading ability. Ensure an appropriate level of text complexity.
* If engaging with a shared class novel, before administering the task, students read/engage with the narrative up to the selected text portion as part of teaching and learning in this unit of work and then independently read the selected portion.
* Provide students with individual copies of the text selection and a copy of the questions in Part A.
* Students independently read the selected text.
* After students read, they respond to the questions. Remind them to re-read and annotate relevant sections of the text to support their responses.

Part B: Speaking and listening (monitoring strategy)

Instructions

* Guide students to discuss the narrative and ideas for how they can experiment with figurative language, storylines, characters and settings, with a partner or small group.
* Guide students to tell their narrative ideas to support their written text.
* Observe student interaction skills including paraphrasing and questioning to clarify meaning and students’ sharing of a narrative experimenting with ideas from the original text.

Part C: Writing and creating

Students plan, create and edit a written imaginative narrative.

Instructions

* Guide students to plan their narrative, optionally using the **Sheet — Narrative adaption** planning table (optional). Their plan should consider:
* the original author’s audience and purpose
* ideas for a storyline using context, narrative point of view, characters and settings from the narrative.
* Before students begin writing, remind them to use appropriate text structures, language features and narrative literary devices.
* Students use their planning to create their narrative.
* Prompt students to re-read and edit their narrative.
* Guide students to add a multimodal element to their story using audio or digital tools, for example: image generator to create characters and settings for their story (optional).

Part 1: Reading and viewing

Read the text provided by your teacher and answer the questions. Remember to re-read and annotate sections of the text to support your responses.

Purpose and audience

1. What is the author’s purpose in writing the narrative?
2. Who is the audience for this narrative?

Context and settings

1. a. What is the context (social, historical, cultural) of the text? (For example, during which historical event does the narrative take place? What is the social or cultural context?)
2. How do you know the narrative is set in this context? Consider the author’s language choices (such as precise words, idiomatic language), relationships between characters, facts that establish context (such as historical events, traditions and customs) and descriptions of settings.

Characters and events

1. a. Who is a main character?
2. Summarise events and actions in the provided text and evaluate the importance of the events for the main character. Use evidence from the text to support your response.
3. a. From which character’s point of view is the text told?
4. How does the character’s point of view influence how the reader feels about that character and events in the text? In your response, use examples from the text and consider the context, actions of the character, and relationships with other characters.

Part B: Speaking and listening

* Discuss with a partner or small group how you could write your own imaginative narrative by experimenting with figurative language, storylines, characters and settings from the narrative.
* Tell your narrative.

Part C: Writing and creating

1. Plan, create and edit an adaptation of a familiar written imaginative narrative text. This might be a prequel, a sequel, an alternative ending, a short story or a different adventure for the same characters.

Record notes and details about the actions of characters, the setting and the plot for your text.

You might like to use the planning table to organise your ideas and assist your planning.

Remember to:

reflect on the original author’s audience (consider the group of people the author was writing for) and purpose

extend the storyline using the existing context, narrative point of view, characters and settings from the narrative.

1. Create your narrative.

Use your planning to guide your story development.

Use text structures to organise your ideas, for example:

paragraphs with logically sequenced ideas and supporting details

sentence openers that give prominence to the main idea in paragraphs

cohesive devices to guide the reader through the text.

Use language features to express and develop your ideas, for example:

a range of sentences, including simple, compound and complex sentences

consistent tense within and between sentences

topic-specific vocabulary and words, phrases and ideas suited to the context

a wide range of verbs and verb groups, noun groups/phrases and adverbials

punctuated dialogue.

Use narrative literary devices, for example:

characterisation and setting

imagery and figurative language such as simile, metaphor and personification

dialogue.

1. Review and edit your writing for:

text structure and meaning

accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation.

1. Publish your narrative (optional). You may include multimodal elements such as illustrations.
