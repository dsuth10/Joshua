| Assessment task 1 — Responding to literature☼ |

This assessment task is aligned to the aspects of the achievement standard being assessed. Individual school contexts will inform decisions about how the task is to be implemented and how students will engage with the task.

The assessment task gathers evidence of student learning for these aspects of the Year 6 English achievement standard:

Reading and viewing

Read, view and comprehend different texts created to inform, influence and/or engage audiences.

Identify similarities and differences in how ideas are presented and developed including through characters, settings and/or events, and how texts reflect contexts.

Speaking and listening

Interact with others, and listen to and create spoken and/or multimodal texts including literary texts.*

Writing and creating

Create written and/or multimodal texts, including literary texts, for particular purposes and audiences, developing, explaining and elaborating on relevant ideas from topics or texts.

Use text structures and vary paragraphs to organise, develop and link ideas.

Use and vary language features including sentence structures, topic-specific vocabulary and literary devices, and/or multimodal features.

*Denotes monitoring strategy within the task.

## Assessment conventions and resources

Purpose: To read, view, comprehend and compare imaginative narrative texts. To create a written and/or multimodal imaginative adaptation of a narrative.

Technique: Short response (Part A); Extended response (Part C).

Conditions:

- Individual work.

Provide the task to students in a way that is age-appropriate.

Students independently read a passage (unread) from an imaginative text (Part A).

Suggested length for Part C: 200–400 words (informed by QCAA guidelines).

Provide lesson time for drafting with access to teacher feedback and conferencing.

May be completed over multiple lessons (Part B and Part C).

## Implementation advice

## Part A: Reading and viewing

Students read, view, comprehend and compare imaginative texts.

Instructions

Select a written imaginative narrative text with a social, cultural or historical context that develops ideas in the story through characters, settings and events. This could be a novel or short story.

Select an appropriate text that students have not read to read independently, for example: a chapter of a narrative or a complete short story. The selected text should support text comprehension and be long enough for students to demonstrate their reading ability. Ensure an appropriate level of text complexity.

If engaging with a shared class novel, before administering the task, students read/engage with the narrative up to the selected text portion as part of teaching and learning in this unit of work and then independently read the selected portion.

Provide students with individual copies of the text selection and questions 1–5 in Part A.

Students independently read the selected text	 and answer questions 1–5.

Provide students with an alternate imaginative narrative text that reflects the same context to read or view (as whole class, in a small group or independently). This could be another print text or a digital text. If using a digital text, consider how students will access for repeated viewing.

After the students read and view both texts, they respond to question 6. Remind them to re-read and annotate relevant sections of the text(s) to support their responses.

## Part B: Speaking and listening (monitoring strategy)

Guide students to discuss the narrative and how they could adapt plot structure, characters, settings and/or ideas, with a partner or small group.

Guide students to tell their narrative ideas to support their written text.

Observe students’ interaction skills including paraphrasing, questioning, clarifying and interrogating ideas and students’ sharing of a narrative experimenting with ideas from the original text.

## Part C: Writing and creating

Students adapt the familiar written imaginative narrative text using the same characters and settings and reflecting author style.

Instructions

When students are planning their written adaptation, they need to consider:

author’s purpose (consider if the author had a message or idea they wanted to share) and style (consider the author’s distinctive language choices)

existing social, cultural and/or historical context, characters and settings

cohesive and innovative elements (for example: rising and falling action, plot tension, non-linear plot structure, shifts in time).

Before students begin writing, remind them to use appropriate text structures, language features and literary devices.

Students may also choose to include multimodal elements as an optional aspect.

Teachers may choose to provide students with any of the following optional materials to support planning:

Sheet — Narrative adaptation planning table (optional) to assist students in planning their narrative.

Sheet — Narrative adaptation plot profile (optional) to assist students in planning their narrative.

Students use their planning to create their narrative.

Prompt students to re-read and edit their narrative for precision using negotiated criteria.

Guide students to add a multimodal element to their story using audio or digital tools, for example: image generator to create characters and settings for their story (optional).

## Part A: Reading and viewing

Read the text provided by your teacher and answer questions 1–5.

Audience and purpose

Who is the audience for this narrative? Explain why you think this.

What is the author’s purpose/s in writing the narrative? (Consider if the story is just meant to entertain and tell a good story or if the author is trying to teach the audience something or persuade them on an issue.)

Context and setting

Describe the setting and context (social, historical, cultural) of the narrative using examples from the text. (For example, during which historical event does the narrative take place? What is the social or cultural context?)

Author style

What are some characteristics of the author’s individual style? (Consider: Does the author use vocabulary, narrative voice, patterns of language, mood, humour or idioms suited to the particular context?)

Characters and events

a.	Who is a main character in the narrative?

Summarise their actions during the events of the narrative text provided by your teacher.

Why has the character taken these actions?

How do their actions reflect the context and develop ideas of the narrative?

Read, view and comprehend a second imaginative narrative text and answer the questions comparing the two texts.

Similarities and differences

Compare the texts by identifying similarities and differences.

In your response, you could consider some or all of the following:

how the texts present and develop ideas about the same context through similar or different characters, settings or events

how the same historical event and/or cultural context is portrayed through the point of view of different characters

how the social relationships between characters are similar or different

how the two authors have similar or different styles of writing or presenting

how the authors’ styles, use of language, narrative structure and/or point of view (narrative voice) evoke a similar or different response from the reader/viewer regarding the character or events.

## Part B: Speaking and listening

Discuss with a partner or small group how you could write your own imaginative narrative by adapting plot structure, characters, settings and/or ideas and experimenting with literary devices from the narrative.

Tell your narrative.

## Part C: Writing and creating

Plan, create and edit an adaptation of a familiar written imaginative narrative text. This might be a prequel, a sequel, an alternative ending, a short story or a different adventure for the same characters.

- Plan an adaptation

Record notes and details about the actions of characters, the setting and plot for your adaptation. Also consider how you will engage the author’s original audience, support their purpose and reflect their style.

You might like to use the planning table or plot profile to organise your ideas and assist your planning.

Remember to:

use the existing context, characters and settings from the narrative, reflecting the original author’s style, purpose and audience

create a series of events in your adaptation that will lead to a new complication and/or resolution that addresses the author’s original purpose and is directed to the same audience

experiment with literary devices (for example: narrative structure, flashbacks and shifts in time, characterisation and setting, rhetorical devices, quoted/reported speech and figurative language)

consider also using ideas from other texts you have read or viewed.

- Draft the adaptation

Consider how you could use:

text structures, including:

paragraphs with supporting detail to logically sequence ideas

elaborated events such as flashbacks, flashforwards and shifts in time

cohesive devices such as repetition, synonyms and antonyms, and word associations to link ideas and to alert the reader about how the story is unfolding

language features, including:

a variety of sentence types, including complex sentences with embedded clauses

nouns, expanded noun groups, adjectives and adjective groups for fuller descriptions

expanded verb groups, adverbs and adverb group/phrases, and elaborated tenses

topic-specific and vivid vocabulary, words and phrases suited to the context

punctuated dialogue

literary devices, for example:

characterisation and setting

emotive vocabulary, rhetorical devices and figurative language such as metaphors, similes, personification, idioms, imagery and hyperbole.

- Review and edit your draft for precision, including:

text structure and meaning

accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation.

- Publish your narrative (optional). You may include multimodal elements such as illustrations.
