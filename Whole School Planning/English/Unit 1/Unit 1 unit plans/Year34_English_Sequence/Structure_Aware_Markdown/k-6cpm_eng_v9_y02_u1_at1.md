| Assessment task 1 — Responding to literature☼ |

This assessment task is aligned to the aspects of the achievement standard being assessed. Individual school contexts will inform decisions about how the task is to be implemented and how students will engage with the task.

The assessment task gathers evidence of student learning for these aspects of the Year 2 English achievement standard:

Reading and viewing

Use phonic and morphemic knowledge, and grammatical patterns to read unfamiliar words and most high-frequency words.*

Use punctuation for phrasing and fluency.*

Speaking and listening

Interact with others, and listen to and create spoken texts including stories.*

Share ideas, topic knowledge and appreciation of texts when they recount, inform or express an opinion, including details from learnt topics, topics of interest or texts.

Writing and creating

Create written and/or multimodal texts including stories to inform, express an opinion, adapt an idea or narrate for audiences.

Punctuate simple and compound sentences.

*Denotes monitoring strategy within the task.

The following materials can be used when implementing Unit 1: Examining and creating imaginative texts in the K–6CPM English Year level plan example.

See the English Unit 1 Curriculum and assessment alignment planner (CAAP) to guide unit planning when developing a teaching and learning sequence and implementing the assessment in the unit.

## Assessment conventions and resources

Purpose: To recount and share ideas using oral language and adapt an idea in a written and/or multimodal story.

Technique: Observed demonstration (Part B); Extended response (Part C).

Conditions:

Individual work.

Provide the task to students in a way that is age-appropriate.

Suggested length for Part B: Up to 1 minute (informed by QCAA guidelines).

Suggested length for Part C: Up to 100 words (informed by QCAA guidelines).

May be completed over multiple lessons (Part B and Part C).

## Implementation advice

## Part A: Reading and viewing (monitoring strategy)

Before administering the task, students engage with texts about the topic to build relevant background knowledge. Background knowledge texts can be read to the students.

Instructions

Select a decodable text that aligns with the explicitly taught phonics scope and sequence program and students’ phonic development. For example, consider texts available at Decodable texts on the Reading through the Australian Curriculum hub.

Ask students to:

independently read aloud the decodable text. Observe students:

using phonic and morphemic knowledge and grammatical patterns to read unfamiliar words and most high-frequency words (high-frequency words will depend on your context)

using punctuation for phrasing and fluency

monitor their own reading and, if needed, use learnt self-correction strategies.

Record observations, including prompts, using the Teacher recording sheet (optional) (provided) and the monitoring strategy.

## Part B: Speaking and listening

Students recount a familiar story and share their opinions and ideas for adapting the text.

Establish and communicate to students who the audience will be, for example: teacher, peers or younger students.

Instructions

Select an authentic familiar imaginative story to read to the class that includes a clear main character, setting and events.

Ask students to recount the events in order. Remind students to use words and phrases from the story to talk about characters and plot, including the beginning, how the problem is introduced and how the problem is solved. You might do this as a puppet show or role-play.

Provide students with informal opportunities to express their opinion about the text by sharing ideas with a partner or small group. Guide student with Sheet — Active listening (optional) (provided) and observe students’ interaction skills, for example: actively listening to others, expressing and responding to opinions, making statements and extending own ideas (monitoring strategy).

Ask students to share:

things they liked and disliked about the story, for example: ‘I liked how the author described the setting because …’

ideas about how they might adapt the story, for example: ‘What would you change?’; ‘What might happen next?’, to support development of their written text.

## Part C: Writing and creating

Students create a short imaginative text that adapts an idea from a story read to them.

Establish and communicate to students who the audience will be, for example: teacher, peers or younger students.

Instructions

Invite students to change the story read to them by adapting structures and/or language features from the story.

- Guide students to:

plan their ideas, optionally using the Sheet — Story planner (optional) (provided). Remind students to think about the characters, setting and events and what they will change, for example: inventing some speech, dialogue or behaviour for a favourite character or an alternative event or outcome.

Remind students to include the familiar text structure of a narrative, including:

orientation

complication

resolution.

Before students begin writing, remind them to include:

sequenced, simple and compound sentences

noun groups, verb groups and a range of prepositions

punctuation, including capital letters for the beginning of a sentence, a title and proper nouns, commas to separate items in a list and punctuation to indicate the end of a sentence.

Students use their planning to write their imaginative text.

Guide students to re-read to edit their sentences and make sure they make sense, add or delete words to improve meaning, and check spelling.

Guide students to add a multimodal element to their story using audio or digital tools, for example: image generator to create characters and settings for their story (optional).

## Part A: Reading and viewing

Read a text aloud to your teacher.

## Part B: Speaking and listening

Listen to a story read by your teacher. Your teacher will ask questions about the story.

Recount the story including details about the characters, setting and events in order.

Remember to talk about:

Who are the main characters?

What is special or unique about the characters?

Where does the story happen? How does the setting help to tell the story?

What is the problem the characters face?

How is the problem solved?

How does this story make you feel? What did you like or dislike about the story? Explain why.

Talk with a partner.

What do you think might happen next?

What part of the story could you change or adapt?

## Part C: Writing and creating

Create a short text that changes an idea from the story read to you. You might change the structure of the story and/or language features, for example:

a character

what the characters say to each other (dialogue)

a character’s behaviour

the setting

an event.

- Plan your ideas using a story planner.

What will you change in the story?

What is the problem?

How will you sequence the events?

How will the problem be resolved?

Create your story.

Use your planning to help you write the story.

Use simple and compound sentences, with conjunctions such as ‘and’, ‘but’ and ‘so’.

Use noun and verb groups to add details.

Write sentences, use sentence boundary punctuation including capital letters for titles, and use commas to separate items in lists.

Re-read and edit your work. Remember to:

read your sentences to check they make sense

think of any words you could add, remove or change to improve meaning, and check your spelling and punctuation.

Publish and illustrate (optional).

Bring your story to life by including an illustration (for example: using digital tools or drawing).
