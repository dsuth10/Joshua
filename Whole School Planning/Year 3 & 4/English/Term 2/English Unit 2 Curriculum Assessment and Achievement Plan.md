  - Column 1: Students engage with a variety of decodable and authentic texts, including non-fiction texts, through shared reading, viewing and storytelling. These texts include topics that reflect upon and expand their world and include literature from both Australian and world authors, including First Nations Australian authors. They apply their understanding by creating short informative texts to report on ideas or events and present their work to an audience.
  - Column 2: Students engage with a variety of decodable and authentic texts, including non-fiction texts, through shared reading, viewing and storytelling. These texts may include topics being studied in other learning areas, incorporate oral narrative traditions and literature of First Nations Australians, and introduce new content on familiar topics with supporting illustrations and diagrams. Through these texts, students examine how different types of informative texts, such as information reports, recounts of events, simple procedures and factual descriptions, are structured according to their purpose. They apply their understanding by creating short texts to report on events or experiences and present their work to an audience.
  - Column 3: Students engage with a variety of decodable and authentic texts, including non-fiction texts, through shared reading, viewing and storytelling. These texts may include Australian and world authors, including First Nations Australian authors, and introduce new content and connect topics being studied in other learning areas. Through these texts, students examine how different types of information texts, such as information reports, recounts of events, procedures and descriptions, are organised and how authors use language and visual features to suit their purpose. They apply their understanding by creating texts to report on a topic to present their work to an audience.
  - Column 4: Students engage with a variety of informative texts featuring increasingly complex and technical content on topics of interest or those studied in other learning areas. These texts, written by Australian, First Nations Australian and world authors, support and extend students’ independence in reading. Through these texts, students examine how different types of informative texts, such as information reports, descriptions, procedures and explanations, are typically structured and presented to suit their purpose. They explore how language and visual features enhance meaning. They apply their understanding by using these texts as models to create reports on a topic and present their work to an audience.
  - Column 5: Students engage with a variety of texts, including informative texts of increasing complexity and technicality. Written by Australian, First Nations Australian and world authors, these texts support students’ development and extend them as independent readers. Texts may explore topics of interest or connect to learning in other areas, incorporating text-specific language features, varied sentence structures and some unfamiliar vocabulary.  Through these texts, students examine how characteristic features and the organisation of language features serve the texts’ purposes. They apply their understanding by using these texts as models to create reports on a topic and to present their work to an audience.
  - Column 6: Students engage with a variety of informative texts, including reports, explanations, reviews and procedures written by Australian, First Nations Australian and world authors. Through these texts, students examine how structural features, such as chapters, headings and subheadings, tables of contents, indexes and glossaries, help readers navigate and understand information. They apply their understanding by using these texts as models to create a report and present their work to an audience.
  - Column 7: Students engage with a variety of informative texts, including reports, reviews, procedures, biographies and autobiographies, written by First Nations Australian, Australian and world authors. Through these texts, students examine content about a wide range of topics, including topics of personal interest and subjects from other curriculum areas. Students explore how text structures and features, such as headings, timelines and images, inform the readers and improve access to information. They apply their understanding by creating a report and present their work to an audience.

Targeted text selection plays an important role throughout the teaching and learning experiences across Prep to Year 6. The selection and use of texts in English reflects the cumulative and recursive nature of learning. Teachers consider where students have come from and are headed to, so that engagement with texts builds their knowledge of the world, concepts and language. Texts should be intentionally selected to teach an aspect of the curriculum or build students' knowledge. Practise through shared construction, setting out an information report, adding a title and subheadings (Y1/Y2), finding information, using topic-specific words and adding an appropriate image/s. Create different styles of reports based on student interest. Give examples of a spoken report and discuss presentation skills such as inclusion of a greeting, features of voice and prompts to stay on track.  Practise presentation skills.  Years 3–6  Introduction to informative texts: Review students’ prior knowledge of informative texts, including the different types, for example: factual recounts, factual descriptions, procedures, explanations, biographies, comparative and part-whole information reports, online texts, documentaries and news footage (Y3–6) (type of text discussed may vary depending on class composition).  Review audience, purpose and main ideas for informative texts, recognising that texts can be created for similar purposes but different audiences (Y3–6). Discuss what makes a source authoritative and consider things such as experience and qualifications of authors, website content, writers and publishers (Y5). Examine texts including media texts that represent ideas and events, and identify how they reflect the context in which they were created (Y6). Explore objective and subjective language (Y4–6) and bias (Y6) so that students can identify factual reporting in the media as well as the presentation of points of view and bias. Students should understand that points of view and bias can be expressed through both language choice and the selection and use of multimodal elements such as factual or emotive images, bright or sombre music, and bright or subdued colours and graphics (Y4–6). Explore and provide context for reading the texts or parts of the texts (Y5–6). Build students’ background knowledge to support comprehension of the selected texts. Explicitly teach technical and learning area–specific vocabulary from texts (see also Language features). Teach/review note-taking skills. Teach and/or review interaction protocols for discussing texts, for example: ‘think, pair, share’ and ‘turn and talk’ routines. Share the assessment task and learning intentions for the unit. Text structures and organisation: Describe the characteristic stages and phases of informative texts and how these relate to purpose (Y3–6). Discuss the purpose of layout features in print and digital texts and the words used for navigation (Y3). Discuss text navigation features of print and online texts that enhance readability, including table of contents, glossary, chapters, headings and subheadings, headlines, drop-down menus, links, graphics and layout (Y4–6). Use paragraphs with topic sentences to organise texts (Y3–6). Explore text cohesion through paragraphs (Y3–6) and text connectives to sequence and connect (Y4–6). Sentences: Understand that a clause is a unit of grammar usually containing a subject and a verb that need to agree (Y3). Explore how texts can be made cohesive by using the starting point of a sentence or paragraph to give prominence to the message and to guide the reader through the text (Y5). Recognise that sentence openers signal what a sentence will be about, and the rest of a sentence typically elaborates on the sentence opener by providing new information (Y5). Understand that the structure of a complex sentence includes a main clause and at least one dependent clause, and understand how writers can use this structure for effect (Y5). Recognise that a sequence of clauses may use different tenses but remain connected through a topic (Y5). Know that complex sentences make connections between ideas to provide a reason, state a purpose, express a condition, make a concession or link two ideas in terms of various time relations (Y5). Understand how embedded clauses can expand the variety of complex sentences to elaborate, extend and explain ideas (Y6). Parts of speech and punctuation: Use verbs to represent different processes for doing, feeling, thinking, saying and relating (Y3). Use relevant tense (Y3–6). Understand how noun groups can be expanded in a variety of ways to provide a fuller description of a person, place, thing or idea (Y5). Understand how ideas can be expanded and sharpened through careful choice of verbs, elaborated tenses and a range of adverb groups (Y6). Use punctuation including dialogue punctuation (Y5). Use commas for lists, to separate a dependent clause from an independent clause, and in dialogue (Y6). Language features: Explore language features that support meaning (for example: clause- and word-level grammar, vocabulary, punctuation, images) (Y5). Discuss how choices vary for the purpose, subject matter, audience (Y3–4) and mode or medium (Y5). Explore the objective language of factual reporting (Y3–6). Discuss and extend use of topic-specific and technical vocabulary (Y3). Explore a range of synonyms and antonyms of selected words, and morphological knowledge, to build understanding (Y4). Discuss how vocabulary is used to express greater precision of meaning, including through the use of specialist and technical terms (Y5). Visual features: Identify how images extend the meaning of a text (Y3). Explore the effect of choices when framing an image, the placement of elements in the image and the salience on composition of still and moving images in texts (Y4). Explain how the sequence of images in print, digital and film texts has an effect on meaning (Y5). Explore how sequential events can be represented visually by a series of images, including comic strips, timelines, photo stories, procedure diagrams and flowcharts, life cycle diagrams and the flow of images in picture books (Y5–6). Identify and explain how images, figures, tables, diagrams, maps and graphs contribute to meaning, observing how concepts, information and relationships can be represented visually (Y6). Comparing texts (Year 6): Identify similarities and differences in text structures of informative and imaginative texts and how they support the purpose of texts. Review the purpose of literary/imaginative texts (to engage, inform and/or persuade), and text structures of imaginative texts (for example: narrative structure including orientation, complication, resolution, characterisation; poetry structure; performance structure) so that students can make comparisons (Y6). Creating texts: Plan, create, edit and publish written and multimodal informative texts (Y3–6). Use visual features, appropriate form and layout with ideas grouped in simple paragraphs, mostly correct tense, and topic-specific vocabulary (Y3). Use visual features, relevant linked ideas, complex sentences, appropriate tense, synonyms and antonyms, spelling and simple punctuation (Y4). Use visual features, text structure appropriate to the topic and purpose, text connectives, expanded noun groups, specialist and technical vocabulary, and punctuation (Y5). Use paragraphs, a variety of complex sentences, expanded verb groups, tense, topic-specific and vivid vocabulary, punctuation, spelling and visual features (Y6). Presenting an informative text with visual and/or multimodal features: Explore visual elements that extend meaning (Y3 and Y4). Explore multimodal (including digital) elements that add to meaning (Y5 and Y6). Use model spoken texts to strengthen understanding of features of voice and how they can be used and varied to enhance students’ spoken text and engage the audience (Y3–6). Practise delivering short spoken texts, varying tone, pace, pitch and volume for effect (Y3–6).

| All about dogs Dogs are the most common domesticated pet in the world and have been human companions for thousands of years.  Scientific facts about dogs Dogs are mammals as they have a skeletal system, breathe with lungs, have a body covering of hair and give birth to young which they feed with milk. They belong to a group of animals called canines. They are closely related to wolves, foxes and coyotes. The scientific name for dogs is *Canis lupus **familiaris*. There are over 340 different dog breeds in the world, ranging from chihuahuas, which weigh an average of 2 kilograms, to Great Danes, which weigh about 75 kilograms. Keeping dogs healthy Just like humans, dogs need food, water, exercise and medical care to stay healthy. Here are some important things for pet dogs: Diet: Dogs eat special dog food with the right nutrients. Some foods, like chocolate and grapes, are toxic to dogs. Exercise: Dogs need daily walks and energetic play to stay fit. Grooming: Brushing and clipping their fur and trimming their nails helps keep them clean. Vet visits: Dogs need check-ups and vaccinations to prevent illnesses.     Regular grooming keeps dogs healthy. Human and dog relationships Dogs have been living with humans for over 15,000 years and have evolved to help people in many ways. They have bonded strongly with humans. Dogs understand complex human emotions and can sense when their owners are happy or sad. They communicate using barks, tail wags and body movements. Scientists believe that dogs bond so well with humans because, when people interact with their dogs, our bodies produce special chemicals that make us feel happy in their presence. Dr Andrea Jones, a leading Australian expert on canine cognition from ACIS University, said, ‘this 2025 scientific study of how dogs and humans interact helps us to understand why dogs are so important for our wellbeing’. Dogs have incredible senses that help them do important jobs. Their sense of smell is up to 100,000 times stronger than a human’s, which makes them able to smell things we can’t detect. They also have sharp hearing and can hear sounds that people cannot. Many dogs are pets and provide companionship. Others work as assistance dogs. These include therapy dogs, guide dogs and medical service dogs. Some can sense when their owners are about to become ill with conditions such as epilepsy and diabetes, and help them to stay safe. | Police dogs use their senses to find drugs, explosives and even missing people. Military dogs help soldiers by detecting dangers like landmines. Search and rescue dogs use their strong sense of smell and hearing to locate lost or trapped people, even under rubble or snow. Quarantine dogs work at airports and borders, sniffing out fruits, plants and even diseases to stop harmful items from spreading. Many dogs in Australia and around the world are farm dogs, working on farms by herding sheep and cattle or guarding livestock.    Fun facts about dogs 🐶	A dog’s nose print is unique, just like a human fingerprint! 🐕	The fastest dog breed is the greyhound, which can run at over <br>70 kilometres per hour. 🎵	Dogs can recognise their owner’s voice and even respond to music. 👅	Dogs don’t sweat like humans –** **they cool off by panting!  🐕	The world's oldest dog was an Australian cattle dog named Bluey <br>who lived to be 29 years and 5 months old. | Purpose The purpose of a printed informative text is to communicate information about a topic through the use of words and supporting images. This text communicates facts about dogs with images which extend the meaning of the words. The text uses mainly objective language, although the overall message of the text is a positive one about dogs reflecting both the context (modern Australian society) and a cultural and societal bias in favour of dogs. The supporting visuals are photographs and a diagram. The photographs are realistic but still frame dogs in a positive light. For example: The sequential images, while showing the effect of grooming on an unkempt dog, are also humorous with a small and friendly-looking dog. The dog is photographed looking at the reader and not displaying any aggressive behaviours. The photograph of the guide dogs is taken from a low and non-threatening level, and the puppy is framed prominently in the image. Most readers have a positive response to ‘cute’ puppies. While the two dogs may or may not be related, positioning an adult dog and a puppy together leads the reader to an interpretation of a familial or maternal bond which is culturally positive. The cattle dog herding sheep isn’t snarling or barking and the sheep do not look frightened or anxious. The photograph of the police and their canines, while factual, is also a positive image. The photograph is again taken from a low and non-threatening level, the officers are smiling and the dogs are relaxed. (Consider the impact on the reader if the dogs had been photographed working in ‘attack and take-down’ mode, attacking a perpetrator.) The photograph of Bluey the dog portrays her sitting in a chair and shaking hands in an example of anthropomorphic behaviour, which positions the reader positively. The diagram, while quite stark in its black line drawing and use of technical vocabulary, uses a ‘friendly’ appearing dog. Audience The audience is people with an upper primary level vocabulary who are interested in finding out about dogs generally, rather than a scientific audience of, for example, veterinarians.  Media text features While not specifically a media text, the text does have some features of one which can be examined with students. It is current (a 2025 study is mentioned). It reflects its context (a modern Australian audience). It has bias in its presentation of a positive view of dogs accompanied by positive images. |
