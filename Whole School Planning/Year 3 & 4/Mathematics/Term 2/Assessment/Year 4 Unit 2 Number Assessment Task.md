## Part C: Use mathematical modelling to plan a pizza party

In Unit 2, students use the process of mathematical modelling to solve financial problems when they:

understand the problem by asking questions, making statements and wonderings (predictions and assumptions) and identifying information needed to help find the solution

plan how to solve the problem by representing the situation to show what is known (and unknown) using materials, diagrams (for example: part-part-whole), bar models, arrays, comparison models, number sentences and basic facts. Students make choices about the operations and use of efficient calculation strategies and digital tools. They formulate the problem mathematically using number sentences

do (solve) the problem using chosen calculation strategies and digital tools (where appropriate)

consider findings by explaining choice of operations used to solve the problem. Students consider how digital tools were useful in planning and/or solving the problem.

communicate solutions with others (for example: adult, classmates). Students interpret and share results in terms of the situation.

## Implementing the mathematical modelling process

Understand the problem

Provide students with a ‘messy’ real-world financial problem. For example:

| Our class is planning an end-of-term pizza party. How much will a class pizza party cost for each person? |
| --- |

Consider your cohort and change the topic of the problem if needed. For example:

How much should we charge for lemonade at the sports carnival?

What can we sell at the tuckshop to make the most profit?

If our float for the fete stall is $50, what is the best combination of notes and coins?

As a class, discuss the problem (not assessed), including statements and wonderings (predictions and assumptions). Support students to brainstorm questions and/or statements about the problem. Example prompt questions and sentence starters may include:

What do you know about the problem? I know … I notice …

What do you wonder about the problem? I wonder …

What do you need to find out to solve the problem? If I knew …, then I could work out …

What maths can you use to solve this problem? What strategies can you use? I can use maths to …

How will you use digital tools?

Provide opportunities for students to choose digital tools to help plan and/or solve the problem. Opportunities to access and use digital tools may include: using a-word processing program, online poll or online drawing program; using virtual materials, for example: counters.

Plan how to solve the problem

Students plan how they will solve the problem, including making decisions, for example: what types of pizza they will get, how many pizzas they will need, what other items they will need for the party (for example: drinks, napkins, paper plates, decorations, dessert), how they will represent the problem, how they will use digital tools (where appropriate).

Provide students with resources to support their calculation strategies to help plan how to solve the problem. Resources may include physical or virtual fraction manipulatives, fraction strips, calculators, physical or virtual materials (for example: counters, blocks, pizza images), word-processing program, online drawing tools. If needed, the Sheet — Frank’s pizza and party place can be used as a stimulus for planning and solving the problem. Alternatively, provide students with appropriate local business websites or shopping catalogues to use.

Students develop their plan by representing the problem, for example: using materials, diagrams (for example: part-part-whole), bar models, arrays, comparison models, number sentences and basic facts.

Students choose which operations they will use to formulate the problem using number sentences.

Do (solve) the problem

Encourage students to use a range of efficient calculation strategies when solving the problem, showing all working.

After the assessment (not assessed)

When students have completed the assessment, extend the mathematical modelling process by having students share their solution with others.

Have a class discussion about the solutions the students arrived at and how mathematical modelling is a useful process for solving problems that have more than one answer. For example:

What do you notice about everyone’s solutions? Are there any similarities or differences?

How did your plan help you to work out your solution?

Is there another way you could have worked out your solution?

If you were planning another class party, what else would you include?

- 47 + 15 is odd/even because I know that odd + odd = even. When I add the ones together (7 + 5) it makes 12, which means the answer will have a two in the ones place, so will be even.

203 – 42 is odd/even because I know that odd – even = odd. When I subtract the ones (3 – 2) it makes 1, which means the answer will have a one in the ones place, so will be odd.

12 × 4 is odd/even because I know that even × even = even. When I multiply the ones together (2 × 4) it makes 8, which means the answer will have an eight in the ones place, so will be even.

Luca added three consecutive 3-digit numbers together and the answer was even. Luca’s number sentence was 301 + 302 + 303 = 906.

Can any three consecutive numbers be added together to make an even number? Explain using your knowledge of patterns when adding odd and even numbers. Hint: Use up to five number sentences to support your answer.

Answers will vary, for example:

Any three consecutive numbers added together will follow these patterns:

odd + even + odd = even, for example: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6

even + odd + even = odd, for example: 2 + 3 + 4 = 9

The first pattern has two odd numbers (1, 3) with an even number in between. The odd numbers added together (1 + 3) make an even number (4), and when added to the other number (2), which is even, the total of the consecutive numbers is even.

The second pattern has two even numbers (2, 4) with an odd number in between. The even numbers added together (2 + 4) make an even number (6), but when added to the other number (3), which is odd, the total of the consecutive numbers is odd.

Therefore, any three consecutive numbers cannot be added together to make an even number. Only consecutive numbers that follow the pattern odd + even + odd will make an even number.
