# Lesson 7 — Cyclone Structure & Convection Currents

## Lesson Objectives

1. Identify the key structural features of a tropical cyclone and explain the role of each.
2. Distinguish between a labelled and annotated diagram.
3. Explain how convection currents form and link this to cyclone formation.
4. Analyse the structural failure of buildings during historical cyclone events.

## Evidence of Learning

- Correctly label: eye, eye wall, rain bands, spiral arms
- Explain why warm air rises in a convection current
- Describe the difference between a simple label and a detailed annotation

---

## Learning Sequence (60 minutes)

### Phase 1: Recall Quiz — 5 questions (5 min)

Show on Slide 2. Students show A/B/C/D fingers:

1. Minimum sea surface temperature for cyclone formation? → **26.5°C**
2. Wind speed threshold for a cyclone? → **62 km/h**
3. What is a hurricane called in Australia? → **Cyclone**
4. Which direction do southern hemisphere cyclones spin? → **Clockwise**
5. What is the calm centre called? → **The eye**

---

### Phase 2: VIDEO — Cyclones Explained BTN (8 min)

`Resources/Video/Cyclones/Cyclones Explained - Behind The News.mp4`

- Students open Lesson_07_Handout Section A (Cyclone Structure Diagram — unlabelled)
- Task: While watching, mark where each feature belongs — eye, eye wall, rain bands, spiral arms, warm air rising, cool air sinking, rotation direction.
- After video: 2-min pair-check. Teacher confirms on slide.

---

### Phase 3: Labelled vs Annotated Diagrams (15 min)

**Direct instruction (Slide 6):**
- **Labelled diagram** = names only (arrows to parts)
- **Annotated diagram** = names + explanation of what each part does

Example shown on slide:
- *Labelled:* "Eye" → calm centre
- *Annotated:* "Eye — lowest air pressure zone. Skies appear clear. The most dangerous winds form in the eye wall surrounding it."

**Student Activity (Section B, 12 min):**
Students take their labelled diagram from Phase 2 and convert it into a highly detailed **annotated diagram**. They must add explanations for at least 6 features, describing how each part contributes to the storm's intensity.

**Discussion:** How does a cyclone diagram differ from the real event? (Size, 3D vs 2D, duration, temperature)

---

### Phase 4: Reading Task — Cyclone Tracy & Althea (12 min)

`Lesson_07_Reading_Tracy.docx`

Students read the combined Tracy/Althea extract. Answer Section C questions:

1. Why did buildings fail during Cyclone Althea?
2. What structural change came from Althea's destruction?
3. What was Operation Navy Help?
4. How did Cyclone Tracy change Australian building codes?
5. Identify two ways modern houses are safer than those built before 1974.

**Teaching point:** Science → community response: Althea's building failures directly caused the Cyclone Testing Station (JCU, 1972) to be established.

---

### Phase 5: VIDEO — Cyclone Debbie BTN (8 min)

`Resources/Video/Cyclones/Cyclone Debbie - Behind The News.mp4`

- Task: Compare Debbie (2017) with Tracy (1974). Section D: Write a detailed comparison of the similarities and differences in how these storms affected communities.
- **Teaching point:** Modern warning systems and building codes significantly reduce casualties even from intense cyclones.

---

### Phase 6: Introduce Convection Currents (10 min)

**Slide 7: What Are Convection Currents?**

Teacher draws on board or shows a digital animation:
1. Heat a fluid (air or water) → warm fluid is less dense → **rises**
2. Cool fluid is more dense → **sinks**
3. Circular movement = **convection current**
4. This occurs on a massive scale over warm tropical oceans — driving cyclone formation

*Link back:* "Look at your annotated cyclone diagram. Label where the convection current is happening within the storm."

---

### Phase 7: Link Convection → Cyclone Formation (2 min)

**Slide 9:**
> "Over a warm tropical ocean, the water heats the air above it. That warm air rises. This creates a low-pressure zone at the surface. Cooler air rushes in to fill the gap — and the Earth's rotation makes it spiral. This is how a cyclone begins."

Students complete Section E: *In your own words, describe how convection currents lead to cyclone formation.* (4–5 sentences)

## Learning Alerts

- Students who copy labels without understanding what each part does
- Confusing the heat source (warm ocean) with the cyclone itself — ocean is the fuel
