Field Study Udawalawe National Park, Sri Lanka

Asian Elephants are
Social Networkers

By Virginia Morell

For a long time, we thought we knew how elephant society worked. We believed they lived in rigid, family-based groups led by a wise matriarch. But new research is revealing a much more complex, fluid, and 'unfaithful' social world—one that looks surprisingly like our own social networks.

Why people were confused before

Most of what we knew about elephant social structure came from studying captive populations or groups in environments where they were forced together by limited resources. These early studies by researchers like Shermin de Silva suggested a very simple, fixed hierarchy. We missed the true complexity because we weren't looking at them in their natural, open habitats.

A place where elephants became easier to study

Udawalawe National Park in Sri Lanka provided the perfect 'open' environment to see how elephants actually choose to associate:

  • check_circle Scrub Forest: The vegetation allows for better visibility over long periods.
  • check_circle High Density: A large population in a relatively accessible area.
  • check_circle Year-round Access: Unlike seasonal parks, researchers could follow individuals through all cycles.

What the researchers did

By tracking 286 individual elephants over several years, the team used photographic identification and statistical network analysis to map every encounter and relationship, revealing patterns never seen before in non-primates.

How many friends do they have?

The research shattered the idea of small, isolated family units.

  • groups

    10-50 Regular Friends: Most elephants maintain a consistent network of 10 to 50 associates that they meet and interact with regularly.

  • diversity_2

    Hundreds of Acquaintances: Beyond their core circle, they recognize and interact with hundreds of other individuals over their lifetime.

  • psychology

    Cognitive Mapping: This requires immense brain power to track the history and status of so many different individuals.

Loyal elephants and 'unfaithful' elephants

The study found a fascinating paradox. While elephants have a core set of loyal friends they stick with for years, they are also socially 'unfaithful.' They frequently leave their primary group to mingle with others, trade information about food sources, or find mates, before returning to their core companions. This 'fission-fusion' society is remarkably similar to human social networking.

Acoustic Communication

They use low-frequency infrasonic rumbles that can travel through the ground for miles. This allows them to maintain 'contact' with friends and family even when they are miles apart, coordinating movements across the landscape.

science

Chemical Communication

Through pheromones in their urine and secretions from temporal glands, elephants leave chemical 'status updates' on trails. Other elephants can smell these to know who has passed by, their health, and their reproductive state.

A changing, flexible society

Ultimately, elephant society isn't a rigid hierarchy; it's a dynamic, ever-changing landscape of alliances. Their ability to manage these complex relationships suggests a level of social intelligence that rivaled only by humans and other higher primates. As environments change, their social flexibility may be their greatest tool for survival.
