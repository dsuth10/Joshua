# Year 6 Science — Energy and Electricity  
## Research and Ideation Notes for an Expanded Tinkercad, Circuits and Arduino Unit

**Purpose of this document:**  
This document collects the current research, curriculum links, design ideas and possible lesson directions for expanding the existing Year 6 *Energy and Electricity* unit into a richer, more practical unit using Tinkercad Circuits, physical circuit kits and Arduino-compatible microcontroller kits.

The aim is not yet to write the final unit plan, but to capture the best ideas so they can be developed into a full teaching sequence, student workbook, Tinkercad challenge cards and assessment materials.

---

## 1. Existing Unit Foundation

The current unit already has a strong conceptual structure. It focuses on:

- electrical circuits as systems for transferring and transforming electrical energy
- constructing and diagramming circuits
- identifying conductors and insulators
- applying electrical safety knowledge
- understanding how electricity is generated from different energy sources
- comparing fossil fuels, renewables and alternative energy sources
- considering sustainability and community decision-making
- communicating scientific ideas using multimodal texts

The uploaded unit summary organises the unit into two main parts:

1. **Electrical Circuits, Conductivity and Safety**
2. **Electricity Generation, Energy Sources and Sustainability**

This structure should be retained, but strengthened with a more explicit design, simulation, investigation and engineering process.

---

## 2. Recommended Unit Reframe

### Proposed Unit Title

**Electricity Engineers: Designing, Testing and Improving Circuits**

### Driving Question

**How can we design safe electrical systems that transfer and transform energy to solve real problems?**

### Core Learning Flow

The practical section of the unit can be redesigned around this cycle:

**Predict → Build in Tinkercad → Simulate → Explain → Build Physically → Troubleshoot → Improve → Communicate**

This makes Tinkercad more than a novelty tool. It becomes the place where students safely test ideas, make mistakes, diagnose problems and improve their understanding before handling real components.

---

## 3. Why Tinkercad Fits This Unit

Tinkercad Circuits allows students to build and simulate electrical circuits in a browser. It supports basic components such as batteries, LEDs, resistors, switches, buzzers and motors, as well as Arduino-based circuits.

This is useful for Year 6 because students can:

- build circuits safely before using physical equipment
- see whether a circuit works immediately
- test open and closed circuits
- reverse LEDs or wires and observe failure
- compare component layouts with schematic diagrams
- use simple coding blocks before moving to text-based Arduino code
- explore cause and effect in a low-risk environment

The most powerful classroom use is not simply having students copy circuits. The best use is having students predict, test, explain and troubleshoot.

## 4. Arduino-Compatible Kits

The linked kit is an ELEGOO UNO R3 Project Complete Starter Kit, compatible with the Arduino IDE. It is listed as including more than 200 components, a tutorial, UNO R3 controller board and USB cable.

For classroom use, kits like this are best treated as the **physical extension layer** rather than the starting point.

Recommended use:

1. Start with unplugged and teacher demonstration activities.
2. Move to Tinkercad virtual circuit building.
3. Use Tinkercad troubleshooting challenges.
4. Rebuild selected circuits physically using real components.
5. Extend some circuits using Arduino-compatible boards for sensing and control.

Important terminology note:  
An Arduino Uno is better described as a **microcontroller board**, not a full single-board computer. A microcontroller receives inputs, runs simple instructions and controls outputs. This distinction is useful for students because it helps them understand that Arduino projects are about controlling electrical systems, not general computing like a laptop.

## 5. Curriculum Fit

The existing unit aligns strongly with the Year 6 Science idea that electrical energy can be transferred and transformed in circuits and generated from a range of sources.

The expanded unit strengthens this by adding:

- practical circuit construction
- virtual simulation
- controlled investigations
- circuit diagramming
- troubleshooting
- evidence-based explanations
- engineering design thinking
- digital technologies links
- multimodal communication

The key curriculum ideas remain Science-focused. Coding is used only where it supports the Science concept of input, output, control, energy transfer and transformation.

## 7. Strong Expansion Ideas

### 7.1 Virtual Circuit Bootcamp

Students begin with short Tinkercad circuit challenges.

#### Suggested Challenges

| Challenge | Student Task | Key Science Learning |
|---|---|---|
| Make It Glow | Build a complete LED circuit | Complete circuit, polarity, electrical to light energy |
| Make It Stop | Add a switch to control the LED | Open and closed circuits |
| Make It Warn | Add a buzzer and LED | Electrical to sound and light energy |
| Make It Move | Add a motor | Electrical to movement energy |
| Make It Safer | Add a resistor correctly | Component protection and safe current levels |
| Make It Match | Build from a circuit diagram | Scientific representation |
| Explain It | Write the energy transfer and transformation | Scientific communication |

The focus should be on explanation, not just completion.

---

### 7.2 Circuit Detectives

Create deliberately faulty circuits in Tinkercad. Students diagnose and explain what is wrong.

#### Example Faults

- LED is connected backwards.
- Circuit is not a complete loop.
- Switch is not placed in a useful position.
- Resistor is missing.
- Wires are connected to the wrong breadboard row.
- Positive and negative rails are confused.
- Buzzer is connected with incorrect polarity.
- Motor is connected but not controlled by the switch.
- The circuit diagram does not match the physical layout.

#### Student Response Stem

> The circuit does not work because ____.  
> Electricity cannot flow because ____.  
> To fix the circuit, I would ____.  
> After the change, electrical energy will be transferred from ____ to ____ and transformed into ____.

This directly supports the assessment requirement for students to analyse the requirements for electricity to move through a circuit.

---

### 7.3 Virtual-to-Physical Circuit Building

A powerful routine would be:

1. Build the circuit in Tinkercad.
2. Take a screenshot.
3. Draw a circuit diagram.
4. Explain how the circuit works.
5. Build it with real components.
6. Test it.
7. Compare the virtual and physical versions.
8. Reflect on what was easier, harder or surprising.

This helps students understand that simulation is a model. It is useful, but it is still a simplified version of the real world.

---

### 7.4 Conductors and Insulators Investigation

Tinkercad can be used to design the test circuit, but the actual testing should be physical because students need to test real materials.

#### Possible Materials to Test

- aluminium foil
- paper clip
- graphite pencil line
- coin
- metal spoon
- plastic ruler
- rubber band
- cardboard
- paddle-pop stick
- dry paper
- tap water
- salt water

#### Investigation Structure

**Question:**  
Which classroom materials allow electrical energy to pass through a circuit?

**Prediction:**  
Students classify materials as likely conductors or insulators.

**Method:**  
Students use a simple testing circuit with a bulb, LED or buzzer.

**Data:**  
Students record whether the component turns on.

**Conclusion:**  
Students explain patterns and link them to material properties.

**Safety Link:**  
Students explain why wires often have metal inside and plastic around the outside.

---

### 7.5 Three Representations Routine

For each major circuit, students should produce three connected representations:

1. **Component Layout**  
   What the circuit looks like in Tinkercad or on a breadboard.

2. **Circuit Diagram**  
   A simplified scientific representation using symbols.

3. **Written Explanation**  
   A clear explanation of how electrical energy is transferred and transformed.

#### Example Explanation

Electrical energy is transferred from the battery through the wires and switch to the LED. When the switch is closed, the circuit is complete and electricity can flow. The LED transforms electrical energy into light energy and some heat energy.

This routine should be repeated across the unit so that students become fluent in moving between models, diagrams and explanations.

## 8. Arduino as an Extension: Control Systems

Arduino should be introduced after students understand simple circuits.

The key idea is:

> Some electrical systems are controlled by code. Sensors provide input, the microcontroller makes a decision, and output components respond.

### Recommended Arduino / Tinkercad Projects

| Project | Components | Science / Technology Concept |
|---|---|---|
| Blinking LED | Arduino, LED, resistor | Output, timing, electrical to light energy |
| Traffic Lights | Arduino, red/yellow/green LEDs, resistors | Sequencing, systems, coded control |
| Push-Button Light | Arduino, button, LED | Input, output, switch logic |
| Buzzer Alarm | Arduino, button, buzzer, LED | Warning systems, safety design |
| Night Light | Arduino, photoresistor, LED | Sensor input, light levels, conditional output |
| Mini Fan | Arduino, motor, transistor or motor driver | Electrical to movement energy |
| Temperature Warning | Arduino, temperature sensor, LED or buzzer | Data, thresholds, response systems |

For Year 6, the most suitable first Arduino pathway is:

1. Blink an LED.
2. Control an LED with a button.
3. Create a simple warning system.
4. Use a light sensor to create a night light.
5. Design a practical system for a school or home safety problem.

## 9. Renewable Energy and Sustainability Expansion

The existing unit includes fossil fuels, renewable energy, bioenergy, biowaste and bagasse. This section can be strengthened by connecting electricity generation to circuit concepts.

Students should understand that electricity generation often involves energy transformations such as:

- chemical energy → heat energy → movement energy → electrical energy
- movement energy from wind → movement energy in turbine → electrical energy
- light energy from the Sun → electrical energy in solar panels
- potential energy of water → movement energy → electrical energy
- chemical energy in biomass → heat energy → movement energy → electrical energy

### Current Australian Electricity Context

Energy.gov.au reports that in 2025 Australia’s electricity generation came from both fossil fuels and renewables. Renewables contributed 39.5% of total electricity generation, while fossil fuels contributed 60.5%. Coal accounted for 42.7%, gas 16.2% and oil 1.7%.

This is important because some older teaching resources imply that coal accounts for around three-quarters of Australian electricity. A revised unit should use updated data and give students opportunities to interpret electricity generation graphs.

### Classroom-Friendly Renewable Models

| Energy Source | Possible Classroom Model |
|---|---|
| Solar | Solar panel powering LED or small fan; compare shade and full light |
| Wind | Fan blowing onto turbine/motor model; discuss variables such as blade shape and wind speed |
| Hydro | Water wheel model linked to turbine/generator concept |
| Bioenergy | Flow diagram and case study rather than live combustion |
| Bagasse | Queensland sugarcane case study: sugarcane waste → heat → steam → turbine → generator |
| Battery Storage | Discuss storing generated electrical energy for later use |

## 13. Assessment Possibilities

### Practical Design Folio

Students design, test and explain a low-voltage electrical system.

Assessment evidence:

- circuit analysis
- correct use of components
- explanation of energy transfer and transformation

- safety awareness

Students are given a faulty circuit and must:

## 16. Safety Notes

The unit must clearly distinguish between:

- safe classroom low-voltage circuits
- household mains electricity

Students should never investigate household power directly. Household circuits should be explored through diagrams, teacher explanation, videos and safety scenarios.

Students should use:

- low-voltage batteries or USB-powered microcontroller boards
- teacher-approved components
- no damaged wires
- no mains electricity
- no short circuits created intentionally with physical batteries
- eye protection if required by school risk assessment
- careful handling of small components

Arduino and Tinkercad activities should be positioned as safe models for understanding electrical systems, not as permission to experiment with household electricity.

## 17. Common Misconceptions to Address

The existing unit already identifies several important misconceptions. The expanded unit should deliberately address them through Tinkercad fault-finding and explanation routines.

| Misconception | Teaching Response |
|---|---|
| A bulb or buzzer will work if connected in any way | Students test incomplete and incorrect circuits |
| A circuit diagram must look exactly like the real circuit | Students compare layout, breadboard and symbol diagrams |
| Only metals conduct electricity | Students test graphite and salt water |
| A switch uses up electricity | Students model a switch as opening and closing a pathway |
| Electricity is safe if you are careful | Students distinguish low-voltage classroom circuits from dangerous mains electricity |
| Renewable and sustainable mean the same thing | Students compare energy sources using evidence |
| All Australian electricity comes from coal | Students interpret current electricity generation data |
| Arduino creates electricity | Students learn that Arduino controls the circuit; it does not create energy by itself |

## 20. Working Design Principle

The strongest version of this unit is not simply “Science with some Tinkercad added”.

The stronger version is:

> Students act as junior electrical engineers. They design, test, debug and improve safe low-voltage circuits, then use that understanding to explain how larger electrical systems transfer, transform and generate energy.

This creates a clearer link between circuit construction, scientific explanation, engineering design, sustainability and real-world decision-making.
