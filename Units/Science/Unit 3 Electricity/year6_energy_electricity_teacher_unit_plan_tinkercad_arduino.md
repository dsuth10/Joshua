# Year 6 Science Unit Plan  
## Energy and Electricity: Designing, Testing and Improving Circuits with Tinkercad and Arduino

**Year level:** Year 6  
**Learning area:** Science  
**Suggested duration:** 10 weeks / 20 lessons  
**Suggested lesson length:** 45–60 minutes  
**Unit type:** Curriculum-aligned Science unit with Tinkercad Circuits, physical circuit construction and optional Arduino-compatible microcontroller extension  
**Core unit question:** *How can we design safe electrical systems that transfer and transform energy to solve real problems?*

---

## 1. Unit Purpose

This unit expands the existing Year 6 *Energy and Electricity* unit into a practical, design-rich sequence where students investigate electrical circuits through virtual simulation, physical circuit building, scientific investigation and engineering design.

Students learn that electrical energy can be transferred through complete circuits and transformed into light, sound, movement and heat. They investigate the role of circuit components, switches, conductors and insulators, and apply safety principles when working with low-voltage classroom circuits. Students then connect their understanding of circuits to electricity generation, renewable and non-renewable energy sources, sustainability and community decision-making.

Tinkercad Circuits is used as a safe design and testing environment. Students predict, build, simulate, troubleshoot and explain circuits before selected circuits are rebuilt with physical components. Arduino-compatible microcontroller boards are introduced as an extension to show how coded control systems can use inputs, process simple instructions and control outputs.

The unit keeps Science as the main learning area. Coding and electronics are used only where they strengthen understanding of energy transfer, transformation, circuit components, variables, evidence and communication.

## 2. Existing Unit Foundation

This revised unit builds from the existing Year 6 Science unit, which focuses on electrical circuits, conductivity, safety, electricity generation, sustainability, bioenergy and an electrical safety campaign.

The current unit summary organises the work into two broad parts:

1. **Electrical circuits, conductivity and safety** — reviewing energy forms, constructing and diagramming circuits, investigating conductors and insulators, and applying electrical safety knowledge.
2. **Electricity generation, energy sources and sustainability** — comparing fossil fuels and renewable sources, analysing sustainability and exploring biowaste and sugarcane bagasse as alternative energy sources.

The original unit plan also includes assessment opportunities where students analyse circuit requirements, describe energy transformations and explain how scientific knowledge informs energy-source decisions.

This expanded version keeps those core expectations but strengthens the practical learning sequence through:

- virtual circuit construction and simulation
- real circuit construction using low-voltage materials
- systematic troubleshooting
- stronger use of circuit diagrams and annotated representations
- explicit fair testing of conductors and insulators
- Arduino/microcontroller extension activities
- an engineering design challenge and design folio
- updated Australian electricity-generation data

## 3. Unit Rationale

Electricity is a highly familiar part of students’ daily lives, but many students hold misconceptions about how circuits work. They may think a device can be connected to a battery in any way and still work, that electricity is “used up” before returning to the source, that circuit diagrams must look like the physical circuit, or that only metals conduct electricity.

This unit addresses those misconceptions by giving students repeated opportunities to make, test and improve circuits. Tinkercad allows students to fail safely and repeatedly before using real equipment. Physical kits allow them to experience the practical realities of loose wires, component polarity, weak connections and troubleshooting. Arduino-style tasks show that circuits can be controlled by simple programmed instructions, giving students a bridge between Science, Digital Technologies and engineering.

The overall learning design is:

**Predict → Build virtually → Simulate → Explain → Build physically → Troubleshoot → Improve → Communicate**

This cycle supports students to think and work like scientists and engineers. They gather evidence, identify variables, represent systems, explain cause and effect, make decisions and communicate their findings using appropriate scientific language.

### Assessment overview

| Assessment type | Timing | Evidence collected | Curriculum connection |

| Formative checks | Lessons 2–7 | Tinkercad screenshots, exit tickets, circuit explanations, teacher observations | Circuit requirements, components, transformations |

| Summative task | Lessons 19–20 | Engineering design folio and presentation OR supervised response supported by practical evidence | Circuit analysis, energy transformations, communication, decision-making |

### Suggested summative assessment task

**Task title:** *Design, Test and Explain an Electrical System*

Students design a safe low-voltage electrical system that performs a useful function or demonstrates an energy transformation. They must prototype the circuit in Tinkercad and, where possible, rebuild it physically.

Possible design problems:

- Create a warning system that uses a switch, LED and buzzer.
- Design a model room or house with controlled lighting.
- Build a night-light circuit that responds to light levels.
- Create a mini traffic-light sequence using LEDs.
- Build a model safety display that demonstrates an electrical hazard and a safe solution.
- Design a renewable-energy demonstration model, such as a solar-powered fan or LED.

### Required student evidence

Students submit or present:

1. A labelled Tinkercad screenshot of the circuit.
2. A circuit diagram using accepted symbols or clearly labelled component representations.
3. A written explanation of how electricity flows through the circuit.
4. A written explanation of the energy transformations in the circuit.
5. A safety risk assessment for low-voltage circuit construction.
6. A physical build photo, teacher observation checklist or explanation of why the virtual prototype remained virtual.
7. A troubleshooting log showing at least one problem and how it was fixed.
8. An improvement reflection explaining how the design could be made safer, more reliable or more useful.

## 9. Safety and Risk Management

This unit must use **low-voltage classroom circuits only**. Students must never experiment with household mains electricity, power points, chargers, extension leads or appliances.

### Core safety controls

- Use batteries, battery holders, USB-powered Arduino boards and low-voltage components only.
- Teacher checks all physical circuits before students connect power.
- Students do not connect LEDs directly to high-voltage sources; use appropriate resistors.
- Avoid deliberate short circuits. If investigating faults, use Tinkercad first.
- Keep liquids away from powered components unless conducting a carefully controlled teacher-approved conductivity demonstration.
- Disconnect power before changing wiring.
- Use eye protection if components are likely to spin, flick, heat or detach.
- Keep motors, fans and moving parts away from hair, clothing and fingers.
- Do not allow students to modify USB cables, mains plugs or household appliances.
- Use classroom roles: safety officer, builder, tester, recorder and reporter.
- Students complete a risk assessment before the final design project.

### Recommended student safety rule

**“Power off before you change the circuit.”**

## 11. Pedagogical Structure

Each practical circuit lesson should follow the same predictable routine:

1. **Question:** What are we trying to find out?
2. **Prediction:** What do you think will happen? Why?
3. **Virtual build:** Build and simulate in Tinkercad.
4. **Evidence:** Record screenshot, result, problem or measurement.
5. **Explanation:** Use scientific language to explain what happened.
6. **Physical build:** Rebuild selected circuits using real materials.
7. **Troubleshooting:** Identify faults and improve the design.
8. **Reflection:** What did the circuit teach us about energy transfer or transformation?

This repeated routine lowers cognitive load and helps students focus on deeper Science ideas.

## 14. Detailed 20-Lesson Teaching Sequence

### Lesson 1 — Energy all around us

**Focus:** Review forms and sources of energy.  
**Learning intention:** Students understand that different forms of energy can be generated from a number of sources.  
**Success criteria:** Students can identify examples of light, heat, sound, movement and electrical energy and distinguish between a form of energy and a source of energy.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Create a class energy brainstorm: Where do we see energy in everyday life?
2. Sort examples into energy forms: light, sound, heat, movement and electrical.
3. Discuss the difference between an energy source and an energy form.
4. Teacher demonstration: battery-powered torch, buzzer or motor.
5. Students complete a diagnostic concept map: “What I think I know about electricity.”

**Tinkercad connection:** Teacher shows a simple virtual LED circuit as a preview of how electrical energy can be transformed into light.  
**Evidence:** Concept map and exit ticket.  
**Vocabulary:** energy form, energy source, electrical energy, transformation.

---

### Lesson 2 — What makes a circuit complete?

**Focus:** Complete and incomplete circuits.  
**Learning intention:** Students identify the requirements for electricity to move around a circuit.  
**Success criteria:** Students can build a circuit that works and explain why an incomplete circuit does not work.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Pose the question: “What must be true for a bulb or LED to work?”
2. Demonstrate an incomplete circuit and invite predictions.
3. Students build a basic battery–wire–bulb or battery–LED circuit in Tinkercad.
4. Students intentionally break the circuit in three ways and record what happens.
5. Class builds a shared explanation of a complete circuit.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Make it glow, then make it stop working in a way you can explain.  
**Evidence:** Screenshot of working circuit and one broken version, with explanation.  
**Vocabulary:** circuit, complete circuit, open circuit, closed circuit, cell, wire.

---

### Lesson 3 — Components that transform energy

**Focus:** Bulbs, LEDs, buzzers and motors.  
**Learning intention:** Students explain how components transform electrical energy into other forms.  
**Success criteria:** Students can identify the input energy and output energy for common components.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Review complete circuits.
2. Introduce components: bulb, LED, buzzer and motor.
3. In Tinkercad, students build or modify circuits to make each output work.
4. Students complete an energy transformation table.
5. Discuss why LEDs require correct polarity and often need resistors.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Build three working output circuits: light, sound and movement.  
**Evidence:** Energy transformation table.  
**Vocabulary:** component, LED, buzzer, motor, resistor, polarity, output.

---

### Lesson 4 — Switches and circuit diagrams

**Focus:** How switches control circuits and how circuits are represented.  
**Learning intention:** Students explain how a switch works and draw a circuit diagram using accepted conventions.  
**Success criteria:** Students can show how a switch opens and closes a circuit and represent the circuit clearly.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Teacher demonstration: switch open vs switch closed.
2. Students add a switch to a Tinkercad circuit.
3. Students compare push button, slide switch and rocker-style switch behaviour.
4. Introduce standard circuit symbols or simplified accepted classroom symbols.
5. Students draw a circuit diagram from their virtual circuit.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Add a switch that controls the LED without removing any wires.  
**Evidence:** Tinkercad screenshot and matching circuit diagram.  
**Vocabulary:** switch, open, closed, circuit diagram, symbol, representation.

---

### Lesson 5 — Circuit detectives: finding faults

**Focus:** Troubleshooting and explaining why circuits fail.  
**Learning intention:** Students analyse circuit faults and explain what must change for electricity to flow.  
**Success criteria:** Students can identify a fault, fix it and justify the fix using circuit language.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Teacher presents a broken circuit and models “think-aloud” troubleshooting.
2. Groups receive three broken Tinkercad circuits.
3. Students identify the likely fault before pressing simulate.
4. Students fix each circuit and record the reason it failed.
5. Groups write one “fault card” for another group to solve.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Diagnose and repair three broken circuits.  
**Evidence:** Troubleshooting log.  
**Vocabulary:** fault, troubleshoot, evidence, connection, positive, negative.

---

### Lesson 6 — Building physical circuits safely

**Focus:** Rebuilding selected virtual circuits with real components.  
**Learning intention:** Students construct low-voltage circuits safely and compare virtual and physical circuits.  
**Success criteria:** Students can build a working circuit, identify differences between virtual and physical construction, and follow safe procedures.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Review safety rule: power off before changing the circuit.
2. Teacher demonstrates safe handling of batteries, wires and components.
3. Students rebuild a simple LED, bulb, buzzer or motor circuit physically.
4. Students compare their physical circuit with their Tinkercad screenshot.
5. Students record at least one practical difficulty and how they solved it.

**Tinkercad connection:** Use Tinkercad screenshot as the build guide.  
**Evidence:** Teacher observation checklist, photo and reflection.  
**Vocabulary:** low voltage, safety, terminal, connection, comparison.

---

### Lesson 7 — Planning a conductors and insulators investigation

**Focus:** Designing a fair test circuit.  
**Learning intention:** Students plan an investigation to distinguish between conductors and insulators.  
**Success criteria:** Students can identify the variable changed, measured and controlled in a conductivity test.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Pose the question: “Which classroom materials allow electricity to pass through?”
2. Students predict which materials will conduct electricity.
3. Teacher models a conductivity test circuit.
4. Students design their test circuit in Tinkercad.
5. Students plan their investigation using a scaffold: question, prediction, materials, method, variables, risks and data table.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Design a test circuit that can show whether a material conducts electricity.  
**Evidence:** Investigation plan and risk notes.  
**Vocabulary:** conductor, insulator, variable, fair test, prediction, risk.

---

### Lesson 8 — Testing conductors and insulators

**Focus:** Conducting the fair test and drawing conclusions.  
**Learning intention:** Students use a circuit to test materials and explain the purpose of conductors and insulators.  
**Success criteria:** Students can collect data, classify materials and explain why both conductors and insulators are useful.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Review investigation plan and safety controls.
2. Students build the test circuit using low-voltage components.
3. Students test approved materials and record results.
4. Students classify materials as conductors or insulators.
5. Students write a conclusion using evidence.

**Physical investigation:** Paper clip, foil, coin, graphite, rubber, plastic, cardboard, wood and other safe classroom materials.  
**Evidence:** Data table, conclusion and reflection on method reliability.  
**Vocabulary:** classify, result, data, evidence, conclusion, reliability.

---

### Lesson 9 — Electrical safety at home and school

**Focus:** Low-voltage classroom circuits compared with household electricity.  
**Learning intention:** Students identify electrical safety hazards and propose ways to reduce risk.  
**Success criteria:** Students can explain why household electricity is dangerous and identify safe behaviours around electricity.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Compare a safe classroom circuit with household electrical systems.
2. Discuss why students must never experiment with power points or appliances.
3. Analyse images of household or school electrical hazards.
4. Students identify the hazard, possible consequence and safer action.
5. Students begin a safety message bank for later campaign work.

**Tinkercad connection:** Use virtual circuits to model safety principles without using dangerous real examples.  
**Evidence:** Hazard analysis table.  
**Vocabulary:** hazard, risk, consequence, safety measure, mains electricity.

---

### Lesson 10 — Professional safety and risk assessment

**Focus:** Safety methods used by electricians and student risk assessment.  
**Learning intention:** Students understand that professionals use safety procedures and PPE when working with electricity.  
**Success criteria:** Students can identify PPE and describe how risk assessments reduce danger.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Review electrician PPE and safety procedures.
2. Discuss why training, isolation, testing and PPE matter.
3. Students complete a model risk assessment for a low-voltage classroom circuit.
4. Students refine their safety campaign message.
5. Short formative check: “What makes classroom circuits safer than household circuits?”

**Evidence:** Risk assessment and safety explanation.  
**Vocabulary:** PPE, risk assessment, isolate, procedure, professional practice.

---

### Lesson 11 — Arduino as a control system

**Focus:** Microcontrollers, inputs and outputs.  
**Learning intention:** Students understand that a microcontroller can control circuit outputs using instructions.  
**Success criteria:** Students can distinguish between a normal circuit and a controlled circuit with inputs, processing and outputs.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Introduce Arduino-compatible UNO board as a microcontroller board.
2. Explain the idea of input → process → output.
3. Teacher demonstrates a blinking LED in Tinkercad Circuits.
4. Students modify blink speed using blocks or simple text code.
5. Students explain whether the energy transformation changed when code was added.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Make an LED blink, then change the timing.  
**Evidence:** Screenshot and input-process-output explanation.  
**Vocabulary:** microcontroller, input, output, code, instruction, sequence.

---

### Lesson 12 — Sequenced outputs: traffic lights

**Focus:** Ordered outputs and coded control.  
**Learning intention:** Students create a circuit where outputs happen in a controlled sequence.  
**Success criteria:** Students can explain how a coded sequence controls electrical outputs.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Review blinking LED as controlled output.
2. Students build a three-LED traffic-light model in Tinkercad.
3. Students use block code or starter code to sequence red, yellow and green LEDs.
4. Students annotate their circuit and code using plain-language comments.
5. Discuss where controlled circuits are used in real life.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Create a working traffic-light sequence.  
**Evidence:** Tinkercad simulation, screenshot and sequence explanation.  
**Vocabulary:** sequence, timing, output, model, system.

---

### Lesson 13 — Button input and buzzer output

**Focus:** Switches as inputs in controlled systems.  
**Learning intention:** Students explain how button input can control outputs such as LEDs or buzzers.  
**Success criteria:** Students can build or interpret a button-controlled circuit and explain the cause-and-effect relationship.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Compare a normal switch circuit with an Arduino button input.
2. Teacher demonstrates button pressed → LED on or buzzer on.
3. Students build a simple warning circuit in Tinkercad.
4. Students modify the output: LED only, buzzer only, or LED plus buzzer.
5. Students explain the system using input-process-output language.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Create a button-controlled warning system.  
**Evidence:** Input-output explanation and screenshot.  
**Vocabulary:** button, sensor, input, trigger, response.

---

### Lesson 14 — Sensor circuits: designing a night light

**Focus:** Light sensors and threshold decisions.  
**Learning intention:** Students investigate how a sensor can change the behaviour of a circuit.  
**Success criteria:** Students can explain that a sensor provides input data and the system responds when conditions change.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Introduce light-dependent resistor/photoresistor as a light sensor.
2. Teacher demonstrates a night-light model: darker → LED turns on.
3. Students explore or modify a prepared Tinkercad sensor circuit.
4. Students identify the input, output and condition for change.
5. Students reflect on how sensor systems are useful in homes, schools or public places.

**Tinkercad challenge:** Modify a night-light system so it behaves sensibly.  
**Evidence:** Annotated input-process-output diagram.  
**Vocabulary:** sensor, threshold, condition, data, automatic.

---

### Lesson 15 — How electricity is generated

**Focus:** Energy transformations in electricity generation.  
**Learning intention:** Students identify energy transformations that occur when electricity is generated.  
**Success criteria:** Students can describe the transformation chain for at least one electricity-generation method.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Review classroom transformations: electrical → light/sound/movement.
2. Introduce large-scale generation: energy source → movement → generator → electrical energy.
3. Compare coal/gas generation with wind, hydro and solar.
4. Students create energy transformation chains.
5. Discuss how a model simplifies real systems.

**Tinkercad/physical connection:** Demonstrate motor/generator idea conceptually if suitable equipment is available.  
**Evidence:** Energy transformation chain diagram.  
**Vocabulary:** generator, turbine, source, transformation chain, fossil fuel.

---

### Lesson 16 — Renewable energy models and stations

**Focus:** Solar, wind, hydro and other renewable sources.  
**Learning intention:** Students understand that electricity can be generated from a variety of renewable sources.  
**Success criteria:** Students can identify energy transformations and advantages/limitations for renewable sources.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Rotate through renewable energy stations: solar, wind, hydro, geothermal and bioenergy research.
2. Students record energy source, transformation chain, advantage, limitation and suitable location.
3. Optional practical station: small solar panel powering LED or motor.
4. Students compare reliability, location needs and environmental impact.
5. Class builds a shared comparison chart.

**Evidence:** Renewable energy comparison table.  
**Vocabulary:** renewable, solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, location, limitation.

---

### Lesson 17 — Australia’s electricity mix and energy decisions

**Focus:** Interpreting current data and making evidence-based decisions.  
**Learning intention:** Students explore different energy sources used to generate electricity in Australia and explain benefits and drawbacks.  
**Success criteria:** Students can use data to support a decision about energy sources for a specific purpose or location.

**Learning sequence:**

1. View current Australian electricity-generation data.
2. Students interpret percentages of fossil fuels and renewables.
3. Discuss how Australia’s energy mix is changing over time.
4. Scenario task: choose energy sources for a town, remote community, school or farm.
5. Students justify their choice using evidence and trade-offs.

**Evidence:** Energy decision brief.  
**Vocabulary:** evidence, percentage, energy mix, trade-off, sustainable, reliable.

### Lesson 18 — Alternative energy: waste, biogas and bagasse

**Focus:** Waste as an energy source, including sugarcane bagasse.  
**Learning intention:** Students understand that biowaste and sugarcane waste can be used as alternative energy sources.  
**Success criteria:** Students can describe how waste material can be transformed into useful energy and evaluate benefits and limitations.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Introduce bioenergy, biogas and bagasse.
2. Connect to Queensland contexts where relevant, especially sugarcane regions.
3. Students create a process diagram: waste material → fuel/heat → movement/steam → generator → electricity.
4. Students identify benefits, limitations and sustainability questions.
5. Link back to scientific knowledge informing decisions.

**Evidence:** Bioenergy process diagram and sustainability reflection.  
**Vocabulary:** bioenergy, biowaste, biogas, bagasse, sustainability, resource.

---

### Lesson 19 — Engineering design challenge: prototype

**Focus:** Designing and testing a circuit for a purpose.  
**Learning intention:** Students apply their circuit knowledge to design a safe electrical system.  
**Success criteria:** Students can prototype a circuit, explain how it works and identify improvements.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Introduce final design challenge and success criteria.
2. Students select or are assigned a design brief.
3. Students sketch their idea and list required components.
4. Students build and test the circuit in Tinkercad.
5. Where appropriate, students begin a physical build.
6. Students complete a troubleshooting and improvement log.

**Design options:** Warning system, model house lighting, traffic-light model, night light, safety display or renewable-energy demonstration.  
**Evidence:** Prototype, screenshot, draft diagram and troubleshooting log.  
**Vocabulary:** prototype, design brief, criteria, constraint, troubleshoot, improve.

### Lesson 20 — Present, explain and reflect

**Focus:** Communicating scientific understanding through a design folio or presentation.  
**Learning intention:** Students communicate how their circuit transfers and transforms energy and evaluate their design.  
**Success criteria:** Students can use scientific language, diagrams and evidence to explain their circuit and reflect on improvements.

**Learning sequence:**

1. Students finalise design folio evidence.
2. Students present their circuit or prototype to peers.
3. Peer feedback: one strength, one question, one improvement idea.
4. Students complete final reflection.
5. Teacher collects summative evidence and optional supervised written response.

**Evidence:** Design folio, presentation, final reflection and teacher observation.  
**Vocabulary:** communicate, justify, evaluate, evidence, scientific explanation.

## 17. Misconceptions to Address

| Misconception | Teaching response |
|---|---|
| A bulb or buzzer can be connected to a battery in any way and still work. | Use Tinkercad and physical faults to show complete circuit requirements and polarity. |
| Electricity is “used up” before returning to the battery. | Emphasise circuit as a complete path and components as energy transformers. |
| Circuit diagrams must look exactly like the physical layout. | Compare physical layout, Tinkercad layout and schematic symbols. |
| Only metals conduct electricity. | Test graphite and salt water under safe, controlled conditions. |
| If an appliance is switched off, it is always safe. | Explain that household appliances connected to mains electricity can still be dangerous. |
| Renewable and sustainable mean the same thing. | Compare regeneration with long-term environmental, social and economic effects. |
| Coding is the main point of Arduino. | Reframe Arduino as a way to control circuit inputs and outputs to demonstrate Science concepts. |

## 19. Teacher Notes on Tinkercad and Arduino Use

### Tinkercad role

Tinkercad should be used as the unit’s main simulation environment. Its purpose is to let students safely test circuit ideas, make mistakes, repair faults and record evidence. The most valuable learning comes from prediction and troubleshooting, not from copying a finished circuit.

Recommended teacher moves:

- Provide starter circuits for early lessons.
- Provide broken circuits for troubleshooting.
- Use screenshots as assessment evidence.
- Ask students to annotate screenshots with arrows showing energy transfer.
- Require written explanations after each simulation.
- Move from virtual to physical builds only when students can explain the circuit.

### Arduino role

Arduino-compatible boards should be treated as an extension that shows how circuits can be controlled by programmed instructions. Use the term **microcontroller board** rather than single-board computer.

Keep Arduino activities simple:

- blinking LED
- traffic-light sequence
- button-controlled LED or buzzer
- light sensor night-light model

Avoid allowing the coding to become the main assessment unless the unit is deliberately being integrated with Digital Technologies. For Science, assess whether students can explain inputs, outputs, components, energy transfer and energy transformation.

## 20. Final Unit Product Options

At the end of the unit, students could produce one of the following:

1. **Design folio:** screenshots, diagrams, explanations, safety notes and reflection.
2. **Interactive safety display:** circuit plus multimodal safety message.
3. **Model house:** controlled lighting system with switches or sensors.
4. **Warning system:** LED and buzzer alarm with switch or button.
5. **Sustainable energy proposal:** model plus written recommendation for an energy source.
6. **Circuit detective portfolio:** repaired circuits with explanations of each fault.

The strongest option for curriculum alignment is the **design folio**, because it captures practical construction, scientific explanation, safety, data and communication.
