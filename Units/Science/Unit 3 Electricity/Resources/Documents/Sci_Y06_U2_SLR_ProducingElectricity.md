**Producing electricity****: Teacher note**

The focus of the learning object *Energy chains: make** electricity* is on the energy transformations that occur when generating electrical energy.

**Note****:**The learning object also refers to energy efficiency. This is not the subject of this unit, and will be addressed in Year 8.

The primary learning to be taken from student interaction with this learning object is the concept that energy transformations occur when energy changes from one form to another. Electricity is generated when movement energy is transformed into electrical energy by an electrical generator.

The movement energy can be transferred to the generator from a turbine (a propeller-type device) that is turned at high speeds.

The movement energy required to turn a turbine can be transferred from the movement of wind, falling water or tidal flow, but is most often from the production of steam.

The heat energy required to produce steam can be supplied from a number of different sources: burning of fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas); nuclear material; and the high temperatures found deep within Earth’s crust (geothermal).

The following sources provide different forms of energy for use in generating electricity.

| **Energy source** | **Energy ****form** |
| --- | --- |
| Wind | Movement |
| Tidal flow | Movement |
| Wave motion | Movement |
| Geothermal | Heat |
| The sun | Light |
| The sun | Heat |
| Oil | Chemical |
| Petrol | Chemical |
| Coal | Chemical |
| Natural gas | Chemical |
| Nuclear material | Nuclear |

<br>

Students in Year 6 should familiarise themselves with the different energy sources and consider the advantages and disadvantages of using each for generating electricity. They should be encouraged to sequence the energy transformations in a chain and explain, in simple terms, the energy transformations in the sequence, e.g. light energy from the sun falls on solar panels and is transformed by solar cells into electrical energy.
