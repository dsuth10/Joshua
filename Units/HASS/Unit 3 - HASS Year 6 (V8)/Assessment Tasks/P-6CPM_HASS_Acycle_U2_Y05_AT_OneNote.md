## 4. First Nations Peoples & Environmental Connections

*Examine Source 1 (Dayak Peoples of Borneo) and Source 2 (Pitjantjatjara People of Central Australia) below.*

### Source 1: The Dayak Peoples (Borneo, South-East Asia)

![Dayak peoples of Borneo locator topography map](<assets/dayak_borneo_locator_map.png> "width=600")

![A Dayak longhouse community](<assets/dayak_longhouse.jpeg> "width=600")

* **People and Place:** The Dayak peoples are indigenous groups who live on the island of Borneo (shared by Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei). There are many ethnic groups amongst the Dayak peoples, speaking roughly 140 different languages and dialects. For thousands of years, Dayak peoples have relied on the equatorial rainforest for resources, adapting to high year-round temperatures and heavy rainfall.
* **Food & Resources:** Dayak life centres on cyclical rice paddy planting. They move farming plots periodically to allow the rainforest to regenerate. They also cultivate vegetables, catch freshwater fish, and hunt forest animals using spears and blowpipes.
* **Culture & Community:** Traditionally, Dayak peoples lived in large communal longhouses built along major rivers, where an entire village community resided under one roof. Dance, music, and ceremony are central to spiritual life. Elaborate tattoos and traditional brass ornaments are important cultural expressions.

### Source 2: The Pitjantjatjara People (Central Australian Desert)

![Pitjantjatjara traditional country map](<assets/pitjantjatjara_australia_map.png> "width=600")

![Uluru in Central Australia](<assets/uluru.jpeg> "width=600")

* **People and Place:** The Pitjantjatjara (*pronounced pit-jan-jah-jarra*) are an Aboriginal people of the Central Australian desert (Anangu Pitjantjatjara Yankunytjatjara lands). Sacred sites include Uluru and Kata Tjuta. The landscape consists of red sand plains, dunes, and low spinifex scrub in an arid desert climate.
* **Food & Resources:** Pitjantjatjara people traditionally hunt bush foods such as the red kangaroo (*malu*), emu (*kalaya*), bush turkey, and sand goanna (*tinka*). They harvest edible insects (such as honey ants - *tjala*, and witchetty grubs) and bush plants (bush plums, desert figs, and bush tomatoes). Fire is used skillfully as a landscape management tool to regenerate native plants and attract game. In an arid land where surface water is scarce, rock holes (*tjukula*) and soaks (*kapi*) are carefully protected and maintained.
* **Culture & Community:** Knowledge, law (*Tjukurpa*), and history are passed down through storytelling, songlines, inma (dance/ceremonies), and sand/dot painting. Elders guide young people in caring for Country. The land is inseparable from identity, with every topographical feature holding spiritual meaning.
