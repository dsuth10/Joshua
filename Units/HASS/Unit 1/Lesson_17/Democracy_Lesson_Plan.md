# Lesson 17: Who runs Australia? Understanding the monarch, Governor-General, House of Representatives and Senate

**Duration:** 60 minutes

## Lesson Overview
This lesson teaches students how Australia’s federal Parliament is structured and what each part does in a democratic system. Students learn that Parliament includes the monarch, represented in Australia by the Governor-General, and the two houses of Parliament: the House of Representatives and the Senate.

## Learning Intention
Students will explain the role of the monarch, the Governor-General, the House of Representatives and the Senate in Australia’s democracy.

## Success Criteria
Students can identify each part of Parliament and describe its main role in making laws, representation and government.

## Lesson Process

### Hook
Write **"Who runs Australia?"** on the board.
Ask students to brainstorm possible answers individually, then share responses with a partner.
*Teacher Note: Use this to surface misconceptions, because many students will name only the Prime Minister and may not yet understand the constitutional roles of the monarch, Governor-General and Parliament.*

### Warm-up (5 minutes)
Students complete a quick think-pair-share on **"Who makes decisions in Australia?"** and **"What does democracy mean?"**.
Record student ideas on the board under headings such as "symbolic role," "elected role," and "law-making role" to prepare for the lesson structure.

### Explicit Teaching (15 minutes)
Explain that Australia’s Parliament has three parts: the monarch, the Senate and the House of Representatives, and that the Governor-General represents the monarch in Australia.

Teach each role simply:
*   **The Monarch** is Australia’s head of state, but does not run the country day to day.
*   **The Governor-General** represents the monarch and performs constitutional duties such as appointing the Prime Minister, giving Royal Assent and dissolving Parliament for elections.
*   **The House of Representatives** is where government is formed and where members represent local electorates.
*   **The Senate** represents states and territories, reviews laws and checks the work of government.

### Guided Comparison Activity (15 minutes)
Distribute the **Comparison Worksheet**.
As a class, fill in one example together (e.g., the Monarch).
Have students work in pairs to complete the remaining sections using the board summary.
Prompts include: "Who are they?", "How are they chosen?", "What is their main job?", and "How do they help democracy work?".

### Interactive / Parliament Focus (15 minutes)
Discuss how one chamber (House of Representatives) forms government and how elected representatives speak for communities.
Contrast this with the Senate’s role in representing states and territories, debating bills, working in committees and scrutinising government.
Create a simple two-column class chart on the board:
*   **House** = forms government / represents electorates
*   **Senate** = reviews laws / represents states and territories

### Review and Exit Ticket (10 minutes)
Students complete the short **Exit Ticket** answering 4 questions to match each institution to its role.
Finish by revisiting the opening question ("Who runs Australia?") and asking students to refine their answer using the correct civic language.

## Teaching Notes
Keep the explanation concrete and avoid overloading students with constitutional detail in the first lesson.

A strong phrasing is: *"The monarch is the formal head of state, the Governor-General represents the monarch, the House of Representatives usually forms government, and the Senate reviews laws and represents the states and territories."*

**Useful teacher questions:**
*   Why do you think Australia has two houses of Parliament instead of one?
*   Why might a democracy need elected representatives as well as constitutional rules?
*   Why is it important that laws are checked by more than one group?

A helpful scaffold is to distinguish between *symbolic/constitutional roles* and *elected/law-making roles*, because this helps students see why the monarch and Governor-General are different from senators and members of parliament.

---

## Optional Extension
If you want the lesson to become more active, extend it into a simple role-play in which some students are members of the House, some are senators, one is the Governor-General, and the class walks through how a bill becomes law.
The PEO classroom activities page includes "Make a law: House of Representatives" and "Make a law: Senate," which are designed for exactly this kind of experiential learning.
You could also add a local representation task by having students identify their federal electorate and discuss how a member of the House represents a community.
