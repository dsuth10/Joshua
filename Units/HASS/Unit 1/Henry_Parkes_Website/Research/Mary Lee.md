## Mary Lee (1821–1909)

## Born in County Monaghan — A Life Before Australia

Mary Lee was born **Mary Walsh** on 14 February 1821 on the Kilknock Estate in County Monaghan, in the north of Ireland. She was raised by landed Protestant parents and received a notably good education for a woman of her era — a fact that later distinguished her from many of the women whose causes she championed. Very little else is reliably recorded about her early years in Ireland, which is a telling historical silence: the women of her generation left few personal records.[^1][^2]

In 1844, at age 23, she married **George Lee**, the organist and vicar-choral of Armagh Cathedral — a prestigious Church of Ireland position in one of Ireland's most important ecclesiastical centres. It was a middle-class, respectable life in the shadow of Armagh's great Gothic cathedral. The couple had **seven children** — four sons and three daughters — and Mary ran a school for girls in Ireland, suggesting her instinct for education and organisation was deeply rooted long before she encountered politics.[^3][^4][^2][^1]

The Ireland that shaped her was post-Famine, politically alive with questions of land, poverty and justice. The Catholic emancipation struggles, the Tenant League, and fierce debates around the rights of the poor formed the social backdrop of her formative years. She later described herself as once being *"the slip of an old red-hot Tory stem"* — suggesting her politics transformed dramatically over her lifetime.[^3]

## The Voyage That Changed Everything

George Lee died around 1879, leaving Mary a widow in her late 50s with her Australian son, **John Benjamin ("Ben") Lee**, already settled in Adelaide and gravely ill. In 1879, she and her daughter **Evelyn** boarded the steamship ***Orient*** — on its **maiden voyage** from England to Australia — and sailed for Adelaide to nurse him back to health.[^5][^6][^1]

Ben died on **2 November 1880**. Mary and Evelyn should have returned to Ireland, but they could not — she was financially unable to afford the return passage, and she had quickly found in Adelaide a city she came to love deeply. Stranded in a new country at age 58, a widow with no independent income, she took in boarders at her North Adelaide home to sustain herself. What might have been a quiet, constrained widowhood instead became one of the most remarkable late-life transformations in Australian history.[^3][^1][^5]

## Finding Her Purpose — Social Purity and the Age of Consent

Lee's first foothold in Adelaide's reform world came through **Reverend J. C. Kirby's Social Purity Society**, of which she became foundation secretary of the ladies' committee in **1883**. The Society's central campaign at the time was blunt and urgent: raise the age of consent from the appalling legal minimum of **twelve years** to sixteen. Through intensive lobbying, the orchestration of public debate, and deputations to parliament, Lee played a central role in the Society's campaign.[^5]

Her efforts succeeded: the **Criminal Law Consolidation Amendment Act (1885)** was passed, raising the age of consent to sixteen. Kirby himself gave Lee *"the principal credit"* for this legislative change. It was a foundation lesson: a determined, organised campaign backed by public pressure could overcome parliamentary inertia. She never forgot it.[^3][^5]

## The Logic of Suffrage

Through her Social Purity work, Lee came to an insight that proved to be the intellectual cornerstone of the entire suffrage movement: legal protections for women — in their bodies, their workplaces, their families — could only be reliably secured **once women could vote those protections into law themselves**. She articulated this with characteristic directness: she regarded manhood suffrage as *"only half a victory"* and female suffrage as *"the pivot on which turns the whole question of the moral, social and industrial status of women"*.[^3]

Acting on this logic, Lee and other Purity Society members co-founded the **South Australian Women's Suffrage League** on **13 July 1888** — with Sir Edward Stirling as president and Lee as co-secretary, quickly becoming sole secretary. The League's objects were deliberately narrow: enfranchisement on exactly equal terms with men, without initially claiming the right to stand for parliament. This tactical focus was Lee's doing — she understood that a single, clear demand was harder to dismiss or dilute than a broad platform.[^7][^3]

## A Campaigner of Extraordinary Tenacity

From 1888 to 1894, Lee conducted one of the most sustained grassroots political campaigns in Australian colonial history. She was **short, plump and erect**, described as going *"energetically about the city, suburbs and country"*. She spoke at League meetings and socials, at Democratic clubs, at **Woman's Christian Temperance Union** meetings (despite personally disliking total abstinence), and at public halls across the colony. She was a skilled orator who deployed **historical, literary and biblical allusions** with equal fluency, holding audiences even when she was the target of abuse and ridicule.[^3]

Her methods were simultaneously practical and strategic:

- She collected **shilling subscriptions** door-to-door to fund the League's operations[^3]
- She wrote prolific **newspaper articles and letters** to the *Register* and other Adelaide papers, including her famous declaration: *"If I die before it is achieved, like Mary Tudor and Calais, 'Women's enfranchisement' shall be found engraved upon my heart"*[^3]
- She organised **deputations** to Premiers Playford, Holder and Downer[^3]
- She corresponded with suffragists interstate — personally advising **Lady Windeyer in Sydney** on how to organise the Womanhood Suffrage League of New South Wales along South Australian principles, and corresponding regularly with **Rose Scott**[^3]
- She secured the formal backing of the **United Labor Party** from 1891 — a decisive strategic move that introduced organised labour's voting bloc to the cause[^3]

In December 1892, she travelled to **Broken Hill** during the famous miners' strike — four days by train in extreme heat — personally assessing the needs of families, then organising Adelaide women to distribute food and clothing to those in distress. Her commitment extended far beyond the ballot box.[^2]

## The Working Women's Trade Union — A Parallel Campaign

Lee never treated suffrage as a cause detached from economic justice. At a public meeting on **sweating** (the exploitation of poorly paid outworkers) in December 1889, she proposed the formation of a trade union for women — a radical idea in a colonial city. The **Working Women's Trades Union (WWTU)** was formally established in January 1890, with Lee as its secretary for the first two years. She personally visited **clothing factories and workshops** to persuade employers to adopt the union's log of fair prices, with some measurable success.[^3][^7][^8][^9]

By 1893 she was vice-president of the WWTU and sat as its delegate on the **United Trades and Labour Council** — one of the first women ever to hold such a position in the Australian trade union movement — where she worked on a sub-committee examining sweating in the clothing trades alongside **Augusta Zadow**.[^8][^3]

## The Great Petition — 400 Feet Long

Six suffrage bills had failed in the SA Parliament between 1886 and 1893, most defeated by the requirement for an absolute majority or encumbered by property qualifications that Lee refused to accept. When Premier Kingston's 1893 bill was defeated again — this time by an attached referendum condition — Lee's patience finally snapped, and she publicly called the Labor Party *"a lot of nincompoops"* for their role in the failure, a quote the press gleefully reported.[^3]

When Kingston brought an **unencumbered bill** to the House in July 1894, Lee organised the League's most formidable weapon: a **colony-wide petition** that she and her volunteers gathered across the entirety of South Australia, including what is now the Northern Territory. The result was extraordinary: **11,600 signatures** on a document measuring **400 feet (122 metres) in length** — the longest petition ever presented to the South Australian Parliament. Women *"deluged"* members with telegrams and thronged the public galleries of the House of Assembly.[^10][^3]

The **Constitution Amendment Act** was passed on **18 December 1894**, making South Australian women the **first in Australia** to gain the vote on the same terms as men. Crucially, the Act went further than any comparable legislation anywhere in the world at the time — it also granted women the **right to stand for parliament** and the **right to postal votes**. These provisions were unique globally. Lee received congratulatory letters from Kingston and Chief Secretary Sir J. H. Gordon, the latter acknowledging that women's suffrage *"is mainly due to your persistent advocacy and unwearied exertions"*.[^5][^11][^3]

## After the Victory — Official Visitor and Decline

On her **75th birthday in 1896**, Premier Kingston publicly presented Lee with a purse of fifty sovereigns donated through the Mary Lee Testimonial Fund, alongside a handsomely bound address of appreciation at the Adelaide Town Hall. That same year, the government appointed her the **first female official visitor to South Australia's lunatic asylums** — a role she performed with courage and compassion for twelve years, advocating for humane treatment and specifically arguing that *"special provision should be made for brilliant and disturbed patients"* when dealing with the contentious case of Paris Nesbit's release from Parkside Lunatic Asylum.[^3]

Two trade unions nominated her to stand for parliament in 1895. She declined — preferring, she said, to work *"on the side of right… unfettered by pledge or obligation to any party whatever"*. The independence that had been her greatest strength was also, in the end, her isolation.[^3]

## Death in Poverty — Memory Long Neglected

Mary Lee's final years were marked by deepening poverty. As her financial resources dwindled she was forced to sell her personal library. In 1902, Reverend Kirby organised a public relief appeal on her behalf — it attracted poor response. He observed with quiet devastation that many had benefited from her work, but that *"her advanced views and outspokenness had not made her widely loved"*. She complained bitterly to Rose Scott that her entire public career had been conducted entirely at her own personal expense.[^3]

She died on **18 September 1909** at her North Adelaide home, aged 88 — survived by her daughter Evelyn and one son in England. She was buried at the Wesleyan cemetery at Walkerville with her son Ben. Her only memorial was her small white marble tombstone, engraved simply: *"Late Hon. Sec. Women's Suffrage League of S.A."*.[^3]

Her work **remained unrecorded by historians until 1980** — seven decades after her death. The City of Adelaide eventually named **Mary Lee Park** (Park 27B) in her honour in 2017, and a **bronze commemorative bust** stands on North Terrace near Government House, unveiled in 1994 for the centenary of women's suffrage. The woman who gave South Australia — and by extension, Australia — the world's most complete women's suffrage legislation spent her last years in a borrowed house, largely forgotten by those she had liberated.[^8][^11][^3]
