Henry Parkes

Focus question: What was the significance of Henry Parkes and how did his actions contribute to Australia’s Federation?

Identify the significance of Henry Parkes as a key figure leading to Federation

Historical context

Henry Parkes served several terms as Premier of New South Wales and was an ardent advocate of Federation. His ‘Tenterfield Oration’ provided the impetus for a nationwide discussion on the benefits of Federation and the issues to be resolved. The Australasian Federation Convention, in 1891, highlighted just how important the issues were. 

Henry Parkes is a significant figure in the story of Australia’s Federation, because it was his vision and actions that put Australia on this road to Federation. Well before it was popular, Henry Parkes spoke openly about his idea of a united Australia. Despite hostile divisions between the colonies, he pushed forward with his vision and was the driving force behind the first Federal Convention in 1891, where a draft constitution was written. His legacy was to inspire Australia to follow his ideas and realise his vision. Parkes’s influential contributions and leadership in advancing the cause for uniting Australia as ‘one’, has resulted in him being remembered as the ‘Father of Federation’. 

Suggested learning activities

To encourage students to consider the importance/significance of Henry Parkes prior to investigating his life in more detail, show students the image of the commemorative $5 note, produced in 2001, with an image of Henry Parkes on it. (This image can be found on slide 6 of the slideshow Henry Parkes). 

Ask students to describe what other images they can see on the note besides the image of Parkes. (They will not be able to identify these but should be able to describe their appearance.) Explain to students that these are people and places that were significant in the Federation movement and that they will be learning about these in later lessons. Besides the portrait of Henry Parkes, the following features can be seen: 

The Royal Exhibition Building in Melbourne (located on the right of the Parkes portrait). This building hosted the first Commonwealth Parliament in 1901.

Located centre-right is a scene from the First Federal Parliament. His Royal Highness, The Duke of Cornwall and York, is at the front of the group. 

The Federation Pavilion at Centennial Park in Sydney can also be seen to the right of Parkes’s portrait. This pavilion was where the proclamation of the Commonwealth of Australia took place on 1st January, 1901. Over 60 000 people gathered around the pavilion to hear the Queen’s proclamation and to witness the swearing in of the Governor-General and Federal Ministers. 

The Tenterfield School of Arts building can be seen to the left of Parkes — this was where Parkes delivered his famous Tenterfield Oration in 1889. 

The badges of the six Australian states can be seen to the left of Henry Parkes’s signature at the bottom of the note. 

The $5 note is currently the only note that bears the head of the Queen. It is customary for any currency which bears the head of the sovereign to not have another person’s head on the currency. For example, all Australian coins have the head of the Queen but the reverse sides have symbols of the country. Australian notes have the images of people who contributed to Australia on both sides, with the exception of the $5 note. To place Henry Parkes’s image on the $5 instead of the Queen’s image, underlines just how important he was to the history of Australia. On the reverse side of the 2011 commemorative $5 note is the image of Catherine Spence.The current $5 note has only the Queen’s head on it.

Historical context

Slide 2 in the slideshow Henry Parkes explains that Parkes came from a poor, working-class family in England. After migrating to Australia as a young man, Parkes worked in many jobs and had started businesses but he did not experience success or fulfilment. Despite his failures, this man went on to become the Premier of New South Wales five times, and his passion and support for the notion of a federated Australia was unwavering. He was a staunch believer that anyone could achieve anything in Australia, and he ultimately wanted to make his adopted country stronger through Federation.

Examine a timeline of important events and developments relating to the contributions of Henry Parkes 

Historical context

The idea of having some kind of authority to represent all the colonies had begun as early as the 1840s. In 1850, three prominent individuals, the Reverend John Dunmore Lang, a Presbyterian minister and political activist; James Wiltshire, a member of the NSW Legislature; and Henry Parkes formed the Australian League, dedicated to ending convict transportation and establishing an Australian Republic.

In 1863, the colonies held their first Colonial Conference to discuss issues that were common to all of them. All told, seven of these Colonial Conferences were held between 1863 and 1888 to discuss the pressing matters of inter-colonial trade, tariffs and Chinese immigrants. Few issues were agreed on. Henry Parkes voiced his support for some kind of colonial agreement at the 1887 conference and, in 1889, he delivered a stirring speech at Tenterfield in New South Wales calling for a federal system of government for the colonies. 

Parkes’ speech aroused further interest in some kind of federation and further Conferences were held. The Australasian Federal Conference in Melbourne, in 1890, for the Premiers from all colonies (and New Zealand), met to discuss Parkes’s Federation proposal and, in 1891, another Federation Convention was held in Sydney to draw up a draft constitution for a federated Australia.

Extensive strikes among workers on the wharves and the Great Shearers’ Strike in 1891 saw the birth of the Australian Labor Party and even more impetus for change. Those in favour of Federation encouraged groups along the New South Wales/Victorian border to agitate for change. With New South Wales a free trade colony and Victoria a protected trade colony, towns along the border were severely affected by these different policies. Federation Leagues were formed in these border regions.

 In 1893, a Federation League rally was held at Corowa, a border town. This Corowa Conference decided that the decision about Federation should be made by the people of the colonies and referendums were proposed. The impetus for Federation was then stalled for several years as all the colonies were affected by a worldwide Depression and colonial governments focused on economic issues. 

In 1898, a referendum was held to ratify the proposed Constitution in Victoria, New South Wales, Tasmania and South Australia but only a small minority of eligible voters participated in the referendum. New South Wales voted ‘no’ and Queensland and Western Australia did not take part.

In 1899 a secret Premiers’ Conference was held; a private conference of only the Premiers of the six colonies. They agreed on changes to the Constitution and discussed a new federal capital city. Later in 1899, referendums on Federation were held and won in South Australia, New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania and Queensland.

In 1900, the British Parliament agreed and Queen Victoria gave royal assent to the Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act in July. The Western Australian referendum on Federation was held and won in 1900.

On 1st January 1901, the Commonwealth of Australia was proclaimed.

Analyse sources to identify their origin and purpose and describe points of view about Henry Parkes

Suggested learning activities

The sheet Father of Federation provides a visual source (Source 1) and a written source (Source 2) relating to Henry Parkes and the Federation movement. The visual source is called ‘Federated Australia’ drawing, 1900. This drawing was published in The Daily Telegraph, a popular Sydney newspaper, in 1900. The written source contains the main arguments put forward by Henry Parkes in his Tenterfield Oration speech. There are activities on the sheet for students to complete.

When describing the picture in Source 1, students should try to include all aspects of the image, including: 

There is a man dressed in a long coat and trousers, with a bushy white beard and hair, drawing back a curtain.

There is a map of Australia with the words, ‘Federated Australia’, on it. No colonial divisions are marked on the map.

There is a woman standing on the map dressed in flowing robes with her arms outstretched.

The woman is holding a flag.

The flag is divided into four quarters with the British ensign in the top right quarter. The lines dividing the flag are thick and have stars on them.

The sun is shown rising behind the woman and it has many rays coming from it.

When explaining the meaning of the images (that is, interpreting them) students should refer to:

Henry Parkes is opening the curtain to symbolise that Federation was his idea; his show. 

Behind the curtain is a map of Australia, united as one entity without borders. 

The young woman holding the flag symbolises a new birth for Australia. (Countries have traditionally been referred to as female.)

The flag symbolises the ongoing links with Britain but the cross with stars is the Eureka flag, which symbolises the desire for democracy and independence.

The rising sun with brilliant rays coming from it also symbolises a new era for Australia.

The artist’s point of view is that Henry Parkes was the ‘showman’ who opened people’s eyes to the benefits of Federation and brought a new era to Australia.

The purpose of this illustration may have been to highlight the new era Australia was embarking on and to acknowledge the role of Henry Parkes in ‘drawing back the curtain’ on this new era.

Questions for Source 2 on the sheet Father of Federation focus on the importance of defence in Parkes’s overall arguments for Federation and the desire for a peaceful and well-organised process.
