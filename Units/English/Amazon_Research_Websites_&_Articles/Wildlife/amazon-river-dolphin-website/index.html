Amazon River Dolphin

Blushing Sentinel of the Flooded Forest

Floating through chocolate-brown channels in a haze of pink, the Amazon river dolphin—locally the boto—is equal parts mystery and barometer. Scientists now warn that populations of this freshwater cetacean are halving every decade in some reserves, mirroring the health of South America's great rivers and the people who rely on them. Meet the Boto

Discover the fascinating characteristics of this unique freshwater cetacean

Size & Appearance

Reaching up to 2.5m and 185kg, adult males outweigh females by as much as 55 percent, one of the most extreme cases of sexual dimorphism among dolphins. Flexible, paddle-like pectoral fins and unfused cervical vertebrae let the dolphin rotate its head 90° and weave between submerged tree trunks when the forest floods each wet season. Learn more about their anatomy and physical characteristics.

The Pink Phenomenon

Calves are slate-grey, but repeated skin abrasion and capillary expansion gradually turn males a flamboyant pink—a colour that can intensify when the animal is excited. Discover more about their unique coloring and behavioral patterns.

Vision & Sonar

Although their eyes are small, botos see surprisingly well in murky water and rely on a large melon to focus bursts of biosonar that map prey hiding in silted depths. A long, whiskered rostrum armed with molar-like teeth crushes armored catfish and river crabs that oceanic cousins could never tackle. Learn more about their sensory adaptations.

Lifestyle in a Liquid Labyrinth

From Bolivia to Venezuela, three recognised subspecies inhabit the Amazon and Orinoco basins

Shifting from main channels during the dry season into seasonally flooded várzea forests where they pursue shoals among buttress roots. Most adults travel alone or in pairs, but loose feeding aggregations of a dozen animals appear when migrating fish swarm sand-bars. Learn more about their habitat and behavior.

Hunting Patterns

Dawn and dusk are peak hunting windows; daytime finds many dolphins resting in shady creek mouths.

Playful Nature

Indulging in the boto's famous play—tossing logs, wresting turtles from the surface, and occasionally "tail-walking" beside passing boats.

Reproduction

Mating peaks at the start of the flood pulse. After an 11-month gestation, a single calf nurses for more than a year.

Adaptations for Flood-Pulse Living

Specialized features that enable survival in the dynamic Amazon ecosystem

Skeletal Agility

Unfused neck bones grant exceptional maneuverability in submerged forests. Learn more about their skeletal adaptations.

Blubber-light Build

A lower lipid layer reduces buoyancy, helping dolphins dive beneath floating mats and log jams. Learn more about their physical adaptations.

Color Modulation

Dermal capillaries allow rapid "blushing," possibly aiding social signalling in turbid water. Learn more about their color changes.

Acoustic Toolbox

Powerful broadband clicks penetrate suspended sediments that scatter light, while a large lower-jaw fat pad channels echoes to the ear complex. Learn more about their echolocation abilities.

Keystone of a Riverine Web

The vital role of Amazon River Dolphins in ecosystem health

As apex piscivores, Amazon river dolphins regulate mid-level predators such as piranhas and predatory catfish, indirectly protecting herbivorous fish critical to local fisheries. Their movements redistribute nutrients: each surfacing breath vents carbon-rich air; each dive fertilises floodplain trees with nitrogen-laden waste. Indigenous communities view the boto as a sentinel—when dolphins disappear, fish landings and water quality soon decline.

Rising Threats

Multiple challenges facing the Amazon River Dolphin population

Dam Cascades

175 hydroelectric dams already fragment Amazon tributaries, with 428 more planned; barrier chains sever migration routes and alter flood pulses that cue breeding. Learn about fishing and dredging impacts and hydropower project risks.

Mercury Pollution

Artisanal gold mining releases an estimated 200 t of mercury annually; tissue analyses show 57 percent of sampled dolphins exceed WHO toxicity thresholds. Learn about mercury threats, scientific research, and mercury poisoning sources.

Fishing Conflict & Baiting

Botos entangle in gillnets and are still harpooned for use as bait in the lucrative piracatinga catfish fishery despite a 2015 moratorium. Read the fishery report and scientific analysis.

Climate-Driven Die-Offs

A 2023 mega-drought warmed Lake Tefé above 39 °C, killing 153 dolphins—around 10 percent of the local population—in a single week. Learn about climate impacts and the Lake Tefé tragedy.

Population Decline

Long-term monitoring in Mamirauá Reserve reveals a 94 percent decline in boto abundance since 1994, largely from deliberate kills and accidental net mortality. Read about hunting and fishing impacts.

Glimmers of Hope

Conservation efforts and positive developments

In 2023 nine Amazon-basin nations signed the Global Declaration for River Dolphins, pledging to reverse population declines through protected migratory corridors, mercury-free mining initiatives, and by-catch mitigation such as acoustic pingers.
