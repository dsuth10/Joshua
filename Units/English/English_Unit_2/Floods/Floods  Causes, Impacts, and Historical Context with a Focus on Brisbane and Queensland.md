# Floods: Causes, Impacts, and Historical Context with a Focus on Brisbane and Queensland

## Executive overview

Floods are overflows of water that extend beyond the normal limits of a river, creek, lake, estuary, or drainage system onto land that is usually dry. They occur in many different physical settings and can be driven by heavy rainfall, tropical cyclones, dam releases, storm tides, and sea‑level rise, often acting together in complex ways. Queensland, including Brisbane, is one of the most flood‑affected regions in Australia, with a long history of severe riverine and coastal flooding that has caused substantial social, environmental, and economic impacts.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

This report introduces the main types and causes of floods, then examines historical Queensland and Brisbane floods and their costs, and outlines social, environmental, and economic impacts at a level suitable as background for senior secondary geography or earth science.

## What is a flood?

A flood is generally defined as water covering land that is normally dry, usually when water escapes from a river, creek, lake, estuary, stormwater system, or when elevated sea levels overtop the shoreline. Floods range from shallow flash floods on city streets to large riverine floods that spread across thousands of square kilometres and last for weeks.[^2][^1]

Flood characteristics vary with the quantity, intensity, duration, and spatial pattern of rainfall, the size and shape of the catchment, soil moisture, vegetation, land use, and the presence of dams or levees. No two floods are identical, even in the same catchment, which is an important idea when comparing events such as Brisbane’s 1974, 2011, and 2022 floods.[^6][^3][^7][^1]

## Main types and causes of flooding

### Riverine (river) flooding

Riverine flooding occurs when prolonged or intense rainfall over a catchment causes rivers, creeks, and floodplains to exceed the capacity of their channels and overflow onto surrounding land. In Queensland this is commonly associated with tropical cyclones, monsoon troughs, and slow‑moving low‑pressure systems that produce heavy and sustained rain.[^4][^1][^2]

In inland parts of Queensland and other Australian states, riverine floods can cover thousands of square kilometres and persist for weeks or months as floodwaters slowly move through large river systems. Riverine floods are a major cause of disaster impacts in Queensland, affecting towns, transport networks, agriculture, and mining.[^5][^1][^2]

### Flash flooding and overland flow

Flash flooding is rapid onset flooding that occurs within about six hours of intense rainfall, often from severe thunderstorms or short‑lived but very heavy rain bands. In urban areas, flash flooding can occur when stormwater systems and creeks cannot cope with the volume of runoff, leading to fast‑moving water across roads, parks, and properties.[^1][^2]

Because flash floods rise and fall quickly, they can surprise people, wash vehicles off causeways, and cause serious injuries or fatalities even when flood depth is relatively shallow. In Brisbane’s 2022 event, a combination of creek flooding and overland flow led to record heights in some northern suburbs and inundated areas that had not flooded in 2011.[^7][^2][^1]

### Coastal flooding: storm tides and storm surges

Coastal flooding happens when elevated sea levels caused by high tides, storm surges, or a combination of the two (storm tide) inundate low‑lying coastal areas and estuaries. Storm surges are abnormal rises in sea level driven mainly by strong onshore winds and low atmospheric pressure during severe storms or tropical cyclones.[^8][^4][^1]

In Queensland, storm tide flooding can affect coastal communities, ports, and estuaries, and it can coincide with riverine flooding if heavy rain inland occurs during the same system. Sea‑level rise due to climate change is expected to increase the frequency and severity of coastal flooding by raising the baseline on which tides and storm surges act.[^8][^4][^1]

### Dam‑related and infrastructure flooding

Flooding can also result from water releases or failure of dams, levees, and other infrastructure. Managed releases are sometimes necessary to protect dam safety when water levels approach the design limit, but large releases can contribute to downstream flooding if timed with heavy inflows.[^4][^1]

Conversely, dams and levees can reduce flood peaks by temporarily storing water, as with Wivenhoe Dam on the Brisbane River, which was built partly to provide flood mitigation after the 1974 floods. However, they cannot eliminate flood risk entirely, especially when rainfall exceeds design assumptions or when other systems such as creeks and stormwater networks are overwhelmed.[^9][^3][^7][^1]

### Meteorological drivers relevant to Queensland

Several large‑scale climate and weather patterns influence flood risk in Queensland:

- **La Niña events** in the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) system tend to bring above‑average rainfall to eastern Australia, increasing the likelihood of riverine and flash flooding.[^10][^5]
- **Tropical cyclones** and ex‑tropical cyclones can produce extreme rainfall, storm surges, and severe winds along coastal and inland Queensland.[^1][^4]
- **Monsoon troughs and slow‑moving lows** can persist over Queensland for days, delivering prolonged heavy rainfall that saturates catchments.[^6][^1]

The 2010–2011 Queensland floods occurred during a very strong La Niña event, with record or near‑record rainfall across much of the state. Repeated La Niña years before 2022 also left soils saturated, increasing runoff and flood risk when extreme rainfall arrived.[^10][^7][^5]

## Flooding in Brisbane and Queensland: historical context

### Overview of Queensland’s flood exposure

Queensland is Australia’s most disaster‑prone state and has experienced numerous major flood events, including in 1893, 1974, 2010–2011, 2013, and 2022. Many communities are built on floodplains, along rivers, or on low‑lying coastal land, which historically offered fertile soils, transport routes, and flat building sites.[^3][^5][^6][^1]

The Queensland Reconstruction Authority and other agencies emphasise that riverine floods in inland Queensland can last weeks, affecting thousands of square kilometres, while coastal and urban floods may be shorter but more intense and damaging in built‑up areas.[^6][^1]

### Brisbane River flood history

Since European settlement, major Brisbane River floods have been recorded in 1841, 1890, 1893, 1931, 1974, 2011, and 2013, with particularly severe events in 1893, 1974, and 2011. Before the construction of Wivenhoe Dam in the 1980s, the river was subject to relatively frequent flooding, with the 1974 event prompting major changes to floodplain planning and dam construction.[^9][^3]

The Brisbane River Catchment Historical Factsheet highlights the 1893, 1974, 2011, and 2013 events as key reference floods for understanding the range of possible river levels and impacts. The 2022 event, while influenced by different mechanisms, is now another critical data point for understanding compound flooding involving rivers, creeks, and overland flow.[^3][^7]

### The Great Flood of 1893

In February 1893, a tropical cyclone triggered extreme rainfall in the Brisbane River catchment, causing the river to burst its banks and flood the city. Water levels were recorded at about 8.35 metres above low tide at the City Gauge, the second‑highest level on record there.[^9][^3]

The flood destroyed critical infrastructure, including the Victoria Bridge and the Indooroopilly railway bridge, and caused at least 11 deaths and many injuries, along with extensive property damage estimated at around £4 million, a huge sum for the time. Two further floods followed within weeks, also linked to tropical cyclones, illustrating how successive events can occur within one wet season.[^3][^9]

### The 1974 Brisbane floods

The 1974 floods were driven by an extremely wet spring and summer followed by heavy rain from Cyclone Wanda in late January, which delivered about 642 mm of rain over 36 hours to Brisbane. The Brisbane River peaked at about 6.6 metres at the City Gauge, overtopping its banks and inundating approximately 8,500 homes.[^9][^3]

This event highlighted the vulnerability of low‑lying suburbs, industrial areas, and the central city, and led directly to the planning and construction of Wivenhoe Dam upstream to provide both water supply and flood mitigation storage. It also led to changes in land‑use planning, building codes, and emergency management practices in Brisbane and surrounding areas.[^3][^9]

### The 2010–2011 Queensland floods and 2011 Brisbane flood

From late 2010 into early 2011, extensive flooding affected about 75 per cent of Queensland, with major impacts on inland towns, regional centres, and the Brisbane–Ipswich area. The event was associated with a strong La Niña pattern and repeated heavy rainfalls over already saturated catchments.[^5][^10]

Brisbane experienced a major riverine flood in January 2011 when inflows from the upper catchment and controlled releases from Wivenhoe Dam combined with local rainfall to raise river levels. Thousands of properties in Brisbane and Ipswich were inundated, 33 people across Queensland lost their lives, and critical infrastructure including roads, rail, and ports suffered extensive damage.[^10][^5][^3]

Economically, the 2010–2011 floods were described as Australia’s largest natural disaster in recent memory, with a World Bank report estimating total damages and economic losses at about US$15.9 billion and public reconstruction costs around US$7.2 billion. Australian estimates suggested the floods would cost the national economy at least A$10 billion, with insured losses of about A$1.5 billion from around 38,460 claims, nearly half relating to homes.[^11][^5][^10]

### The 2022 South East Queensland floods and Brisbane

Between 23 and 28 February 2022, intense rainfall delivered 400–1,100 mm across the Brisbane local government area, with an average of about 795 mm and more than one metre of rain in some suburbs over three days. This exceeded three‑day rainfall records from 1974 in many locations, especially on Brisbane’s north side.[^7]

Unlike 2011, which was primarily a riverine flood, the 2022 event involved simultaneous flooding of the Brisbane River, creeks, and widespread overland flow. Kedron Brook and other creeks broke records, flooding streets and homes in areas previously dry in 2011, partly because two La Niña years had already saturated the ground and increased runoff.[^7]

An independent Deloitte report prepared for the Queensland Government estimated the total social, financial, and economic cost of the 2022 South East Queensland rainfall and flooding event at about A$7.7 billion. This included an estimated human and social cost of around A$4.4–4.5 billion, damage to homes and businesses of about A$2 billion, impacts on more than 2250 primary producers, and significant disruption to small businesses and public infrastructure.[^12][^13]

## Historical economic costs of major Queensland floods

### 2010–2011 Queensland floods

The 2010–2011 floods caused direct physical damage to residential, commercial, and public infrastructure, and significant losses across mining, agriculture, and tourism sectors. Approximately 3,572 businesses were inundated, 19,000 kilometres of road were damaged, and about 28 per cent of the Queensland rail network was affected, along with three major ports.[^5]

The World Bank’s assessment estimated total damages and economic losses (including public and private assets and lost production) at about US$15.9 billion, with public reconstruction costs of about US$7.2 billion. Australian officials indicated that the floods would cost the national economy at least A$10 billion, with insured losses of about A$1.5 billion from tens of thousands of claims, many from Brisbane residents.[^11][^10]

### 2022 South East Queensland floods

The Deloitte study for the Queensland Reconstruction Authority estimated total social, financial, and economic costs of the 2022 South East Queensland floods at about A$7.7 billion. The largest component was human and social costs (including mental health, disease, and social impacts) at approximately A$4.4–4.5 billion, reflecting the long‑lasting toll on individuals and communities.[^13][^12]

The report estimated about A$2 billion in direct damage to residential and commercial property, with around 18,000 homes and businesses affected and over 97,000 insurance claims totalling about A$1.36 billion, leaving significant uninsured losses. Small businesses saw total damage of approximately A$324 million, and the agricultural sector lost more than A$250 million.[^12]

### Interpreting economic cost estimates

Economic cost estimates vary depending on methodology and what is included (for example, only physical damage versus broader indirect and long‑term impacts). Modern studies increasingly attempt to capture intangible and long‑term costs such as mental health impacts, educational disruption, and social cohesion, which can rival or exceed the value of physical damage.[^13][^12][^11]

For teaching purposes at senior secondary level, it can be useful to distinguish:

- **Direct tangible costs:** repair or replacement of buildings, infrastructure, and equipment.
- **Indirect tangible costs:** lost production, business interruption, transport delays.
- **Intangible costs:** health and mental health impacts, loss of life, disruption to education, community stress and displacement.

## Social impacts of floods

### Human safety and health

Floods pose immediate risks to life through drowning, injuries from fast‑moving water and debris, and accidents such as vehicles being swept away at flooded crossings. The 2010–2011 Queensland floods caused 33 confirmed deaths, illustrating the seriousness of these hazards.[^2][^1][^5]

Beyond immediate physical harm, floods have major mental health consequences, including increased anxiety, depression, post‑traumatic stress, and family stress, which can last for years. The 2022 Deloitte report estimated the health, social, and community impacts of the SEQ floods at around A$4.4–4.5 billion, underscoring the scale of these often less visible effects.[^12][^13]

### Housing, displacement, and community disruption

Flooding can render homes uninhabitable for weeks or months, forcing evacuations and temporary or long‑term displacement of residents. In events such as the 2011 and 2022 floods, thousands of households in Brisbane and across Queensland experienced inundation, requiring large‑scale evacuation centres, emergency accommodation, and support services.[^10][^12][^5]

Community facilities such as schools, sports clubs, and community centres are often damaged or used as evacuation hubs, interrupting education and social activities. However, floods also generate strong community responses, with large volunteer efforts like the “Mud Army” in Brisbane demonstrating social solidarity and mutual aid.[^13][^12][^5][^10]

### Inequality and vulnerability

Flood impacts are not distributed evenly. Low‑income households, renters, elderly people, people with disabilities, and those without insurance are often more severely affected and take longer to recover. Housing in high‑risk areas is sometimes cheaper, leading vulnerable groups to be over‑represented on floodplains and in low‑lying suburbs.[^12][^13]

Recovery resources such as savings, insurance, and access to information also differ, meaning some groups can rebuild quickly while others may face prolonged hardship or be forced to relocate permanently. These patterns make floods an important topic for studying environmental justice and social equity.[^13][^12]

## Environmental impacts of floods

### Natural ecosystem processes

Flooding is a natural process in many river and wetland ecosystems and plays a key role in transporting sediment and nutrients, replenishing wetlands, and supporting fish breeding and riparian vegetation. Smaller or moderate floods can benefit graziers and ecosystems by spreading water and allowing temporary vegetation to establish, which can slow subsequent flood pulses.[^6]

In floodplain wetlands, periodic inundation maintains biodiversity and productivity, supporting birds, fish, invertebrates, and plants adapted to wet‑dry cycles. For this reason, environmental flow regimes sometimes deliberately release water to mimic natural flood pulses.[^6]

### Environmental damage from extreme or human‑modified floods

Very large or rapid floods, especially where catchments are heavily modified by agriculture, urbanisation, or mining, can cause substantial environmental damage. Impacts include erosion of riverbanks, deposition of large amounts of sediment and debris, and damage to riparian vegetation.[^5][^6]

Floods in agricultural and urban catchments can carry pollutants such as nutrients, pesticides, heavy metals, plastics, and sewage into rivers, wetlands, and coastal waters, affecting water quality and ecosystem health. Damage to infrastructure such as wastewater treatment plants during floods can further increase pollution loads.[^6]

### Longer‑term landscape change

Historically, severe floods like those of 1893 and 1974 have altered the morphology of the Brisbane River, creating new shoals and sandbanks and changing channel depths. These geomorphic changes can affect navigation, flood behaviour in later events, and the distribution of habitats along the river.[^9]

Repeated floods can reshape floodplains, redistribute topsoil, and change patterns of vegetation, which in turn influence how future floods move through the landscape. Understanding these feedbacks is important for long‑term floodplain management and restoration planning.[^6]

## Economic impacts of floods

### Damage to infrastructure and property

Floods damage residential, commercial, and public buildings, roads, bridges, rail lines, ports, and utilities such as electricity, water, and telecommunications. In the 2010–2011 Queensland floods, 19,000 kilometres of road and around 28 per cent of the rail network were damaged, alongside three major ports.[^12][^5]

The 2022 SEQ floods caused an estimated A$2 billion in damage to homes and commercial properties, affecting about 18,000 buildings. Repairing or rebuilding this infrastructure requires large public and private expenditure and can take years.[^13][^12]

### Impacts on businesses, agriculture, and industry

Floods disrupt business operations through physical damage, loss of stock, supply chain interruptions, and workforce displacement. Small businesses in SEQ were estimated to have suffered around A$324 million in damage in the 2022 event, while mining, agriculture, and tourism lost around A$4 billion in the 2010–2011 floods.[^5][^12]

Agriculture is particularly vulnerable, with crop losses, livestock deaths, and damage to fences, irrigation infrastructure, and soil structure. The 2022 floods affected more than 2250 primary producers across 17 local government areas, with estimated agricultural losses exceeding A$250 million.[^12][^6]

### Macroeconomic and fiscal effects

At the state and national level, large flood events reduce economic output in the short term and require substantial government spending on relief, recovery, and reconstruction. For example, the 2010–2011 floods were estimated to cost the Australian economy at least A$10 billion and required reconstruction programs worth billions of dollars.[^11][^10][^5]

However, reconstruction spending can also stimulate certain sectors such as construction and materials, leading to complex patterns of economic impact over time. For teaching, it is useful to explore these short‑term negatives and medium‑term mixed effects, and to discuss how investment in resilience (such as better planning, infrastructure upgrades, and early warning systems) can reduce future costs.[^11]

## Bringing it together for senior secondary analysis

### Comparing different flood types and causes

When designing student investigations, Brisbane and Queensland provide case studies of multiple flood types:

- **Riverine floods** (1893, 1974, 2011) dominated by Brisbane River levels and catchment rainfall.
- **Compound events** (2022) where river, creek, and overland flow flooding occurred together.[^7]
- **Coastal and storm tide floods** in other Queensland coastal towns, linked to tropical cyclones and storm surges.[^4][^1]

Students can compare hydrographs, rainfall patterns, and spatial impacts across these events to understand how different drivers (cyclones, La Niña, storm surge, dam operations) combine to produce different flood characteristics.[^1][^10][^7]

### Investigating social, environmental, and economic dimensions

Queensland flood events offer rich material for integrated studies of social, environmental, and economic impacts. Socially, classes can explore case studies of affected suburbs, vulnerable groups, evacuation and recovery experiences, and mental health. Environmentally, they can examine changes to wetlands, river channels, and water quality, and consider the role of natural floods in ecosystem functioning.[^13][^12][^6]

Economically, students can analyse cost estimates, distinguish between types of costs, and discuss trade‑offs in land‑use planning, infrastructure investment, and insurance. For a Brisbane‑centred unit, comparing 1974, 2011, and 2022 allows investigation of how risk management has evolved and where residual or new risks remain.[^11][^5][^12]

### Extending beyond Brisbane

To broaden understanding, classes can compare Brisbane’s experiences with floods in other Australian locations (for example, Lismore in New South Wales, or coastal towns affected by storm tides) or international case studies involving levee breaches, dam failures, or glacial lake outburst floods. This supports the curriculum aim of recognising both local uniqueness and global patterns in natural hazards.[^8][^4]

By starting with this broad background and progressively narrowing to specific case studies and inquiry questions, students can develop sophisticated, evidence‑based understandings of floods as physical processes and as complex social, environmental, and economic phenomena.
