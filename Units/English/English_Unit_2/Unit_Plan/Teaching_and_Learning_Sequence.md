## Sequence 1 — Text Immersion: Purpose, Audience & Text Types
### Core Text: Cyclone Archive (Weeks 1–2)

*Eight lessons. Students are immersed in the Cyclone Archive as a model informative text. Focus: identifying the purpose and audience of informative texts, exploring text structures (hub page, sub-pages, headings, navigation features), reviewing prior knowledge of informative texts.*

## Sequence 2 — Language Features & Text Structure
### Core Text: Floods Archive (Weeks 3–4)

*Eight lessons. Students explore language features at clause and word level. Focus: complex sentences, expanded noun groups, cohesive devices, specialist vocabulary, visual features and image sequences.*

---

### Week 3 — Floods: Digging into Language

| | Lesson 9 | Lesson 10 | Lesson 11 | Lesson 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Learning Intention** | I can identify the structure of a complex sentence, including its main clause and at least one dependent clause. (AC9E5LA05) | I can explain how expanded noun groups provide a fuller description of a person, place, thing or idea. (AC9E5LA06) | I can explain how vocabulary is used to express greater precision of meaning, including specialist and technical terms. (AC9E5LA08) | I can explain how texts are made cohesive by using the starting point of a sentence or paragraph to give prominence to the message. (AC9E5LA04) |
| **Teaching & Learning** | **Activate:** Review simple and compound sentences. Display: "Floodwaters rose quickly." vs "Although heavy rainfall had been forecast for days, the floodwaters rose with a speed that caught residents entirely off guard." Discuss: What is the difference? **Explore:** Introduce complex sentences: one main (independent) clause + one or more dependent clauses. Use the Floods sub-pages to find examples. Discuss: How do complex sentences allow writers to show reason, time, condition, concession? **Model:** Jointly construct 3 complex sentences about floods. **Connect:** Students find 2 complex sentences in the Floods Archive, label the main clause and dependent clause, then write 2 of their own. | **Activate:** Display: "water" vs "the murky floodwater" vs "the rapidly rising, sediment-laden floodwaters of the Bremer River." Discuss: What changes? **Explore:** Introduce expanded noun groups — article + pre-modifier (adjective) + noun + post-modifier (prepositional phrase or embedded clause). Use Floods Archive for examples. **Model:** Jointly unpack one expanded noun group from the Floods sub-pages. Build an expansion step by step from a bare noun. **Connect:** Students find 3 noun groups in the Floods Archive and expand one simple noun from the text into a detailed noun group of their own. | **Activate:** Brainstorm: What words would you need to know to write about flooding? Sort into everyday vocabulary vs specialist/technical vocabulary. **Explore:** Introduce greater precision of meaning through specialist and technical vocabulary. Explore the Floods Archive glossary/tooltip system. Discuss: Why do authors choose "inundation" instead of "flooding"? "Riparian zone" instead of "riverbanks"? **Model:** Together, identify 5 specialist words from the Floods Archive. For each, explore: What is its origin? Why is it more precise? **Connect:** Students build a personal vocabulary notebook — 5 specialist words from the Floods Archive, each with definition, example sentence, and a simpler synonym. | **Activate:** Read two paragraphs from the Floods Archive — one where sentences start with the subject ("The flood caused..."), and one starting with circumstantial information ("In 1974, the Brisbane River..."). Discuss: How does the starting point change what is emphasised? **Explore:** Introduce theme (starting point) and rheme: the opening of a sentence or paragraph controls prominence. Examine the Floods sub-pages for varied sentence starting points. **Model:** Jointly rewrite a paragraph so sentences start differently for different effects. **Connect:** Students choose two sentences from the Floods Archive and rewrite each with an alternative starting point, explaining what changes in emphasis. |
| **Reading** | Floods Archive — How Floods Work sub-page; Brisbane River System sub-page | Floods Archive — Brisbane River System sub-page; Brisbane History sub-page | Floods Archive — all sub-pages (vocabulary hunt); embedded glossary/tooltips | Floods Archive — Brisbane History sub-page; Human Cost sub-page |
| **Differentiation** | **Lucas (Y2):** Identify a simple sentence and a compound sentence (with 'and', 'but', 'so') from a printed excerpt of the Floods Archive. Underline the joining word. Write one compound sentence about floods. (AC9E2LA06) | **Lucas (Y2):** Starting with "water", add one adjective and one describing phrase (e.g., "the muddy water from the river"). Draw and label. (AC9E2LA07) | **Lucas (Y2):** Find 3 words about floods that are topic-specific. Write each in a sentence using a sentence starter. (AC9E2LA09) | **Lucas (Y2):** Read two sentence openings ("The flood..." vs "In 1974..."). With teacher support, discuss: What is each sentence mainly about? Are they the same? (AC9E2LY05) |
| **Resources** | Floods Archive; complex sentence sorting cards; exercise books | Floods Archive; noun group expansion scaffold | Floods Archive; vocabulary notebook template | Floods Archive; sentence-starting analysis sheet |

### Week 4 — Floods: Visual Features & Point of View

| | Lesson 13 | Lesson 14 | Lesson 15 | Lesson 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Learning Intention** | I can explain how the sequence of images in a text has an effect on meaning. (AC9E5LA07) | I can explain how visual features contribute to the effect and meaning of an informative text. (AC9E5LA07, AC9E5LY03) | I can recognise that the point of view in a text influences how readers interpret and respond to it. (AC9E5LE03) | I can use differing ideas and authoritative sources in a text, moving beyond bare assertions. (AC9E5LA02) |
| **Teaching & Learning** | **Activate:** Display the 1974, 2011 and 2022 Brisbane flood images from the Floods Archive in scrambled order. Can students work out the chronological sequence? Why does it matter? **Explore:** Introduce image sequencing: sequential images show cause and effect, before and after, procedure steps, life cycles, timelines. Examine the Floods Archive timeline and photo story features. **Model:** Annotate a page from the Floods Archive together — how does moving from one image to the next build the reader's understanding? **Connect:** Students analyse an image sequence from the Floods Archive, writing 3 sentences explaining how the sequence builds meaning. | **Activate:** Focus on one image from the Floods Archive (e.g., the Brisbane River at peak flood). Ask: What do you notice? What is foregrounded? What draws the eye first? **Explore:** Examine visual features in detail: framing (close-up vs wide shot), placement (left/right, top/bottom), salience (what draws the eye first), layout and design. **Model:** Jointly annotate a full Floods sub-page — connecting written text and visual features. **Connect:** Students write a short analysis of one image from the Floods Archive: "The image shows... The author chose this image because... The visual feature of... creates an effect of..." | **Activate:** Read the same flood event from two perspectives (e.g., a victim's account vs a scientific report). Discuss: How does each writer's position change what information we receive and how we feel? **Explore:** Introduce point of view in informative texts — authorial choices about what to include, what images to use, and what vocabulary to select all reflect a point of view. **Model:** Identify 2–3 choices in the Floods Archive Human Cost sub-page that reveal the author's perspective. **Connect:** Students write: "The author's point of view in this text is... I can tell because..." | **Activate:** Review: What is the difference between an assertion ("Floods are dangerous") and a supported claim ("Floods claimed 35 lives in Queensland in 2011, according to the Queensland Government Flood Commission")? **Explore:** Introduce authoritative sources — what makes a source credible? Experience, qualifications, publication status. Examine how the Floods Archive references and attributes sources. **Model:** Jointly write a paragraph that includes a fact supported by a source and acknowledges a differing viewpoint. **Connect:** Students write 2 paragraphs: one bare assertion, then rewrite it as a supported claim with an authoritative source. |
| **Reading** | Floods Archive — Brisbane History sub-page (timeline + image gallery) | Floods Archive — How Floods Work sub-page; Human Cost sub-page | Floods Archive — Human Cost sub-page + teacher-provided victim perspective text | Floods Archive — all sub-pages; modelled paragraph (teacher-written) |
| **Differentiation** | **Lucas (Y2):** Look at three flood images in sequence. Draw an arrow showing what is happening. Answer: "What happened first? What happened next?" (AC9E2LA08, AC9E2LY05) | **Lucas (Y2):** Choose one image from the Floods Archive. Write or dictate: "This image shows... I think this image was chosen because..." (AC9E2LA08) | **Lucas (Y2):** Read/listen to a short first-person account about a flood. Answer: "Who is telling this story? Is it a fact or the person's opinion?" Discuss with teacher. (AC9E2LY03, AC9E2LY05) | **Lucas (Y2):** Practise the difference between "I think floods are bad" and "Floods caused damage to 100 homes."
