Lesson 19: PEEL Body Paragraphs

Organising Factual Information and Using Specialist Vocabulary

Learning Intention

I can write a logical body paragraph with a topic sentence, supporting details and topic-specific vocabulary.

Success Criteria:

  • I can identify the four parts of the PEEL structure (Point, Explanation, Evidence, Link).
  • I can write a clear topic sentence to start my body paragraph.
  • I can use precise, topic-specific vocabulary instead of everyday words.
  • I can write a complex sentence to link and elaborate my ideas.

I can write a short paragraph to describe one part of my natural disaster topic.

My Success Checklist:

  • I can use a sentence frame to write my short paragraph.
  • I can select nature, describing, and safety words from my word bank.
  • I can work with my partner or teacher to read my sentences aloud.

Activate: Mentor Text Analysis

Let's examine a body paragraph from the Bushfires Archive:

"Fires require three essential elements to ignite and sustain combustion: fuel, oxygen, and heat. Fuel, which consists of dry vegetation, leaves, and bark, accumulates during warm weather. When an ignition source occurs in extreme heat, a rapid chemical reaction begins. Consequently, this loop releases vast thermal energy."

Notice how the author swaps everyday words for precise, specialist terms:

Everyday Language Specialist Vocabulary
burning / catching fire combustion / ignite
plants, trees, leaves vegetation
gets very hot / makes heat releases thermal energy

Let's look at this simple paragraph about Bushfires:

"Fires happen in Australia. They are very hot and dry. They can be dangerous. Firefighters help keep us safe."

Observe how we use our topic word bank:

  • Nature Word "Fires" tells the reader our topic.
  • Describing Words "hot" and "dry" tell what it is like.
  • Safety Word "safe" tells how people are protected.

Explore: The PEEL Structure

A PEEL paragraph structure ensures your writing is clear, logical, and fully developed:

P - Point

Write a clear **topic sentence** that tells the reader what the paragraph is about.

E - Explanation

Explain **how** or **why** this main idea occurs.

E - Evidence

Provide **supporting factual details, measurements, or statistics** from your research.

L - Link

Summarise your paragraph and **link** it back to your overall report topic.

We can use a simple writing frame to build a perfect paragraph:

Sentence 1: State the Topic (Point)

"My natural disaster is [topic]."

Sentence 2: Share a Fact (Evidence)

"One fact is [what it is like/does]."

Sentence 3: Describe the Effect (Explanation/Link)

"This shows that it can be [describing/safety word]."

Model: Joint Construction (Tornadoes)

Let's unpack a modelled body paragraph about the formation of **Tornadoes**:

Point "Tornadoes develop when warm, humid air collides with cold, dry air in a highly unstable atmosphere. Explanation This temperature clash causes the light, warm air to rise rapidly, which creates strong convective updrafts. Evidence Meteorological data demonstrates that this rising air is forced to rotate by wind shear, turning it into a violent spinning funnel. Link Consequently, this fast-spinning vortex descends to the earth, creating a highly destructive windstorm."

Check out the precise, specialist vocabulary:

unstable atmosphere · convective updrafts · wind shear · vortex · collides

Let's look at this simple example about **Tornadoes**:

"My natural disaster is tornadoes. One fact is they have big spinning winds. This shows that they can be very fast and dangerous."
Topic tornadoes (Point)
Fact big spinning winds (Evidence)
Effect fast and dangerous (Explanation/Link)
