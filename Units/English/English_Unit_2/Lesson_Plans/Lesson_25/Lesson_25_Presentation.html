Understanding Tectonic Movements

The surface of the planet might appear perfectly solid and stationary, but beneath our feet lies a highly dynamic and volatile structure. The solid, outermost rocky layer of the Earth is known as the crust (or the continental lithosphere). This layer is not a single continuous shell; rather, it is fragmented into massive segments called tectonic plates that float upon the hot, semi-fluid mantle beneath them.

To monitor and measure these subterranean shocks, seismologists employ a specialized instrument called a seismograph. A seismograph works by detecting and recording the frequency, strength, and precise duration of the seismic waves generated during ground movement. The recorded data is plotted as a seismogram, allowing scientists to estimate the earthquake's epicentre and magnitude.

Although major plate boundaries lie far offshore from the Australian mainland, intraplate stress can build up within continental rock layers and release along ancient fault lines. A prominent historical example is the catastrophic Newcastle earthquake of 1989, which measured 5.6 on the Richter scale and caused severe structural damage, proving that even stable continental plates remain vulnerable to seismic shifts.

What is a Title?

A title sits at the very top of the page. It is always printed in the **largest, boldest font** to tell us immediately what the page is about.

What is a Heading?

Headings divide the page into smaller chapters. They help us find specific facts, like how earthquakes start or where plates meet.
