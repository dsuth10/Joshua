## [HUB][INTRO]

**H2:**
A Continent That Shakes in Silence

Earthquakes do not announce themselves. In the 60 seconds it takes to read this paragraph, somewhere on Earth, the ground will have moved. Most of these tremors are imperceptible — registered only by sensitive instruments buried deep in the bedrock. But occasionally, the energy that has been accumulating silently along a fault for thousands of years finds its release in a few violent seconds, and the consequences can be catastrophic.

Australia is not the Pacific Ring of Fire. We do not have the great subduction zone trenches of Japan, Chile, or Indonesia generating magnitude-9 megathrust events. But the notion that Australia is seismically quiet is contradicted by our own historical record. In December 1989, the city of Newcastle shook for less than a minute. Thirteen people died. Four billion dollars in damage reshaped the built environment of one of our largest regional cities. The fault responsible was not even on the map.

This archive explores the deep science of why the ground shakes — the mechanics of rupture, the architecture of tectonic plates, the behaviour of faults, and the chain of secondary hazards that a large earthquake sets in motion. It grounds that science in the Australian context: a continent moving relentlessly northward, building stress in its crust in ways that continue to surprise seismologists. And it looks at what happens when an earthquake is only the beginning.

**H1:** The Mechanics of Earthquakes

**[DECK]:**
Before the ground moves, something has to break. An earthquake is not an event that simply happens to the Earth — it is the Earth resolving a debt of stored energy that has been accumulating, sometimes for millennia, along a microscopic crack in the rock.

**Read time:** 10 min read

---

## [SUB-PAGE 2][SECTION 1]

**H2:** 1. The Elastic Earth

To understand an earthquake, start with a ruler. Bend a plastic ruler gently — it bows, stores that deformation as elastic potential energy, and straightens again when released. Bend it past its limit, and it snaps. The two halves spring apart, releasing all the stored energy in an instant.

The crust of the Earth behaves in exactly the same way, but on a timescale measured in centuries. Tectonic forces — driven by the slow convection of molten rock deep in the mantle — push and pull rock masses against each other across geological faults. The rock deforms elastically, bending and straining without breaking for decades or centuries. Then, when the accumulated stress exceeds the frictional resistance along the fault surface, the rock ruptures. The two sides spring past each other. The stored energy is released.

This is the essence of elastic rebound theory, first articulated by the American geologist Harry Fielding Reid after his meticulous study of displacement along the San Andreas Fault following the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. Reid measured fence posts and land boundaries that had been surveyed before the earthquake and found that the land on either side of the fault had been creeping slowly in opposite directions for decades before the rupture snapped everything back — and then some.

The ground we stand on is, in this sense, a slow-motion spring — and earthquakes are what happens when it runs out of patience.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 2][SECTION 2]

**H2:** 2. Hypocenter and Epicenter: Where Does an Earthquake Actually Happen?

There is an important distinction between where an earthquake begins and where it is felt most intensely at the surface. The hypocenter (also called the focus) is the actual point underground where the rock first ruptures. The epicenter is the point on the Earth's surface directly above the hypocenter.

An earthquake with a shallow hypocenter — say, five kilometres below the surface — will cause violent, concentrated shaking in a small area directly above it. The same amount of energy released from a hypocenter 200 kilometres deep will produce gentler, more diffuse shaking across a much larger region. Depth, therefore, is one of the most critical determinants of an earthquake's destructive character, separate from its magnitude.

Shallow-focus earthquakes (less than 70 km deep) account for most of the world's seismic damage. Intermediate-focus events (70 to 300 km) are common in subducting oceanic plates. Deep-focus earthquakes (300 to 700 km) are a geological curiosity — they occur in conditions where rock, by all ordinary physical logic, should not be able to fracture, and are still not fully understood. They represent the deepest mechanical instability known to exist within the solid Earth.

The 1989 Newcastle earthquake had a relatively shallow hypocenter — estimated at approximately 11 kilometres — which amplified its destructive effect on the surface. Had the same energy been released at 100 kilometres depth, Newcastle would have felt a jolt, not a catastrophe.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 2][SECTION 3]

**H2:** 3. Seismic Waves: The Message the Earth Sends

When a fault ruptures, it does not simply shake the ground at the surface. It sends energy outward in all directions as seismic waves — ripples in the rock, much as a stone dropped into still water sends ripples across the surface. But seismic waves travel through three-dimensional rock, and they come in fundamentally different types, each with its own speed, character, and capacity for damage.

Primary waves (P-waves) are compressional waves — they push and pull the rock in the same direction as they travel, like the bellows of a concertina. They are the fastest seismic waves, typically travelling at 5–8 km/s through crustal rock, and they can pass through solids, liquids, and gases alike. P-waves are the first to arrive at a seismograph station after a distant earthquake — hence Primary. They are rarely the cause of significant structural damage; they tend to feel like a sudden vertical jolt or boom.

Secondary waves (S-waves) are shear waves — they move the rock side-to-side perpendicular to their direction of travel, like a snake in motion. They travel at roughly half the speed of P-waves (approximately 3–5 km/s) and cannot propagate through liquids, which is why the detection of S-wave shadow zones was the original evidence that Earth has a liquid outer core. S-waves cause more lateral shaking than P-waves and are more damaging to structures.

Surface waves are the most destructive of all. Generated when P-waves and S-waves reach the Earth's surface, they travel along the boundary between rock and air like waves on the surface of the ocean. Love waves move the ground horizontally, shearing it side-to-side. Rayleigh waves produce a rolling, elliptical motion — the unsettling sensation of the ground appearing to breathe. Surface waves travel more slowly than body waves, but their amplitude can be enormous, and they carry by far the most energy that reaches buildings and infrastructure.

The critical practical implication of this wave hierarchy is the P-wave warning window — the time between the arrival of the (relatively harmless) P-wave and the arrival of the destructive S-waves and surface waves. Modern early warning systems detect this signature and can, in theory, provide seconds to tens of seconds of alert time before destructive shaking arrives. Japan's Shinkansen high-speed rail network uses exactly this system; sensors detect P-waves along fault zones and trigger automatic braking before S-waves reach the track.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 2][SECTION 4]

**H2:** 4. Measuring Magnitude — Why the Scale Is Not What You Think

Most people are familiar with the Richter scale, and most people are wrong about what it actually means. The scale devised by Charles Richter in 1935 was a local magnitude scale (ML) — it was calibrated specifically for southern California earthquakes and a particular model of seismograph. It was never intended to be a universal measure, and it breaks down completely for very large or very distant earthquakes.

Modern seismology uses the Moment Magnitude Scale (Mw), which measures the actual physical energy of an earthquake — the area of the fault that ruptured, multiplied by the average distance it slipped, multiplied by the stiffness of the rock. The result is the seismic moment, which is then converted to a magnitude value that remains consistent across all earthquake sizes.

The critical feature of both scales — and the one most commonly misunderstood — is that they are logarithmic. Each whole-number increase in magnitude represents approximately 31.6 times more energy released, not ten times as is sometimes assumed. The difference between a magnitude 5 and a magnitude 7 earthquake is not twice the energy. It is approximately 1,000 times the energy. A magnitude 9 event — like the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake off the coast of Sumatra — released more total energy than all other earthquakes in the preceding decade combined.

For context: the Newcastle earthquake of 1989 was Mw 5.6. The earthquake that generated the 2011 Tohoku tsunami in Japan was Mw 9.1. On the moment magnitude scale, that is a difference of roughly 63 million times the energy.

### [SIDEBAR-POP] — HISTORICAL POP-OUT — Newcastle 1989

**Eyebrow label:** Case Study · Newcastle, Australia

**H4:** The Earthquake That Changed Australian Building Codes

At 10:27 AM on the morning of 28 December 1989, the Hunter Valley city of Newcastle experienced a magnitude 5.6 earthquake. By any global standard, it was a moderate event. By Australian standards, it remains the deadliest earthquake in the nation's recorded history.

Thirteen people were killed. More than 160 were injured. The damage bill was estimated at A$4 billion in 1989 values — a figure that has been revised upward in subsequent economic analyses. The Newcastle Workers Club collapsed; the Adamstown Post Office was destroyed; entire streets of heritage sandstock brick terraces were rendered uninhabitable.

The earthquake occurred at a depth of approximately 11 kilometres on a previously unidentified fault beneath the city. Geoscience Australia's subsequent investigation found that Australia carries a substantial catalogue of unmapped faults, particularly in eastern and southwestern regions, accumulating stress far from any recognised plate boundary.

The Newcastle earthquake forced a fundamental revision of the Australian Standard for earthquake loading in structures. Before 1989, most Australian engineering codes treated seismic loading as a negligible factor outside Western Australia. After Newcastle, mandatory seismic design provisions were progressively introduced across all Australian states. In the most direct sense, this single event rewrote the rulebook by which Australian cities are built.

## [SUB-PAGE 3][HERO]

**H1:** Plate Tectonics & Earthquake Origins

**[DECK]:**
The surface of the Earth is not one piece. It is a jigsaw of enormous rock slabs, drifting on a semi-molten interior, grinding against each other at velocities measured in centimetres per year. Those boundaries are where most of the world's earthquakes are born.

**Read time:** 9 min read

---

## [SUB-PAGE 3][SECTION 1]

**H2:** 1. The Engine Beneath

The theory of plate tectonics is one of the most powerful unifying ideas in all of science — on par, in explanatory reach, with natural selection or quantum mechanics. In the century before its acceptance, geologists had accumulated a catalogue of observations that made no sense under existing theory: identical fossils on continents separated by thousands of kilometres of ocean; mountain ranges with identical geological signatures on opposite sides of the Atlantic; ancient glacial deposits in equatorial Africa. The Australian continent has its own contribution to this puzzle — Gondwana, the supercontinent from which Australia rifted approximately 45 million years ago, left geological scars that are still being read by researchers today.

The mechanism driving plate tectonics is mantle convection. The Earth's mantle — the thick layer of rock between the thin outer crust and the metallic core — is not solid in any conventional sense. Under the enormous pressures and temperatures of the deep Earth, mantle rock behaves as an extremely viscous fluid over geological timescales, circulating in vast convection cells driven by internal heat. Hot material rises at spreading centres beneath the ocean, cools as it spreads laterally, and sinks back into the mantle at subduction zones. This conveyor belt drags the tectonic plates on which the continents and oceans sit.

The plates move slowly — most travel between 2 and 15 centimetres per year, roughly the rate at which your fingernails grow. The Indo-Australian Plate, which carries Australia, moves at approximately 7 centimetres per year toward the north-northeast — one of the fastest rates of any major tectonic plate. Over the course of a human lifetime, that amounts to about 5 metres of lateral movement. Over a million years, it is 70 kilometres. The geological consequences of this relentless drift accumulate on a timescale that dwarfs human civilisation, but they manifest in seconds when a fault finally gives way.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 3][SECTION 2]

**H2:** 2. The Three Types of Plate Boundary

The character of an earthquake is deeply influenced by the type of boundary that produces it. There are three fundamental configurations, each with its own seismic signature and hazard profile.

Convergent boundaries occur where two plates move toward each other. When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate is forced beneath the lighter continental crust in a process called subduction. The descending slab grinds against the overriding plate, building enormous compressive stress. When this stress releases, it does so as megathrust earthquakes — the largest class of seismic event known. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake (Mw 9.1) and the 2004 Sumatra–Andaman earthquake (Mw 9.1–9.3) were both megathrust events at subduction zones. So was every earthquake in recorded history with a magnitude above 9.0. When two continental plates collide — as the Indian subcontinent drives into Eurasia, building the Himalayas — the process is slower but similarly compressive, generating large earthquakes across a broad deformation zone.

Divergent boundaries are where plates move apart. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where North and South America move away from Eurasia and Africa at 2–3 cm per year, is the classic example. New seafloor is created as magma wells up to fill the gap. Earthquakes at divergent boundaries tend to be moderate in magnitude — the crust is hot, thin, and relatively weak, limiting how much stress can accumulate — but they can be locally destructive. The East African Rift System, which will eventually split the African continent, is a divergent zone that periodically generates damaging earthquakes across Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, and Mozambique.

Transform boundaries are where plates slide laterally past each other. The San Andreas Fault in California is the world's most studied example — the Pacific Plate slides northwest relative to the North American Plate at approximately 5 cm per year. The Alpine Fault in the South Island of New Zealand performs the same function, running for 850 kilometres through some of the most spectacular landscape on Earth as the Pacific Plate grinds past the Australian Plate. Transform boundaries generate frequent, often shallow earthquakes that can be highly destructive in densely built areas.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 3][SECTION 3]

**H2:** 3. Subduction Zones and the Origin of Megaquakes

If plate boundaries in general are where earthquakes are born, subduction zones are where the most dangerous ones live. The geometry of subduction is deceptively simple: a cold, dense oceanic plate descends beneath a lighter plate at an angle of 20 to 60 degrees, diving into the mantle at rates of up to 10 cm per year. Along the interface between the two plates — the megathrust fault — friction locks the plates together while the slab below continues to pull downward. The locked zone accumulates stress like a compressed spring. Over centuries or millennia, the stress grows until the interface ruptures, lurching the overriding plate upward by metres in a matter of minutes.

This sudden vertical displacement of the seafloor is what generates tsunamis. During the 2011 Tohoku earthquake, the seafloor in some areas moved laterally by more than 30 metres in under three minutes. The overlying water column, unable to accommodate that instantaneous displacement, was pushed upward and outward. The resulting tsunami wave struck the Japanese coast with heights exceeding 40 metres in some locations.

Australia sits well clear of active subduction zones on its northern and northeastern margins. Between our continent and the subduction zones of the Pacific Ring of Fire lies a complex zone of island arcs, back-arc basins, and microplates across Papua New Guinea, the Solomon Islands, and the New Hebrides. However, these subduction zones are close enough that submarine landslides or large megathrust events can generate tsunamis that travel across the Pacific or Indian Ocean and arrive on Australian shores with meaningful wave heights. Geoscience Australia maintains the Joint Australian Tsunami Warning Centre (JATWC) precisely because this risk is not hypothetical.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 3][SECTION 4]

**H2:** 4. The Ring of Fire and the World Beyond It

The Circum-Pacific Belt — commonly known as the Ring of Fire — is a horseshoe-shaped zone of subduction zones and transform faults encircling the Pacific Ocean. It accounts for approximately 90% of the world's earthquakes and 75% of its active volcanoes. Countries sitting on or near the Ring of Fire — Japan, Chile, Indonesia, New Zealand, the Philippines, the United States — have built their entire disaster management infrastructure around the assumption of repeated, large seismic events.

Australia sits conspicuously outside this belt — and yet the false security this geography seems to offer is precisely what makes Australian seismic hazard so underestimated. The second most seismically hazardous zone in the world — the Alpide Belt — extends from southern Europe through Turkey, Iran, India, and into Southeast Asia, and it generates some of the most destructive earthquakes in history (the 2023 Kahramanmaras, Turkey sequence, Mw 7.8 and Mw 7.7, killed more than 50,000 people within hours). Australia's geological position places it remote from both belts — but, as the science of intraplate seismicity now demonstrates, being far from a plate boundary is not the same as being safe.

### [SIDEBAR-POP] — HISTORICAL POP-OUT — Christchurch 2010–2011

**Eyebrow label:** Case Study · Canterbury, New Zealand

**H4:** The Canterbury Sequence — When the Second Shock Is the Deadly One

At 4:35 AM on 4 September 2010, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck the Canterbury Plains southwest of Christchurch, New Zealand. The Greendale Fault — previously unmapped and entirely unknown to geologists — ruptured for approximately 30 kilometres. Given its magnitude, the death toll was remarkable: zero. The event occurred in the early hours of a Sunday morning, the ground displacement was largely in a rural area, and Christchurch's older, vulnerable buildings had survived — bruised, but standing.

Then, at 12:51 PM on 22 February 2011, a magnitude 6.3 aftershock struck. Its hypocenter was only five kilometres deep — almost directly beneath the city centre — and it occurred on a different, previously unknown thrust fault in the Port Hills south of the CBD. The combination of its extraordinarily shallow depth, its proximity to a dense urban area, and the pre-existing damage from the September event was lethal. The six-storey Canterbury Television building pancaked. The Christchurch Cathedral's spire collapsed. The central business district was largely destroyed.

185 people died on 22 February 2011. It remains the deadliest natural disaster in New Zealand since the 1931 Hawke's Bay earthquake. The total cost of rebuilding central Christchurch has been estimated at approximately NZ$40 billion — the most expensive reconstruction project per capita in the developed world.

The Canterbury sequence demonstrated two principles of enduring importance: that aftershocks can exceed initial assessments of hazard, and that a fault does not need to be known to be dangerous.

## [SUB-PAGE 4][HERO]

**Breadcrumb:** The Trembling Earth / Explainer · Earth Science

**H1:** Fault Types & Earthquake Classification

**[DECK]:**
The word "fault" in geology means a fracture in the Earth's crust along which rock on either side has moved relative to the other. But a fault is not simply a crack. Its orientation, its angle, and the direction of relative motion determine whether it will produce a short, sharp jolt or a prolonged, undulating rupture that moves the ground for minutes at a time.

**Read time:** 8 min read

---

## [SUB-PAGE 4][SECTION 1]

**H2:** 1. What Is a Fault?

Think of the Earth's crust as a three-dimensional block of layered rock. A fault is a planar surface cutting through that block at some angle — sometimes near-vertical, sometimes nearly horizontal — along which rock has moved. The key word is along: faults are not cracks that simply open up; they are surfaces of displacement, where rock on either side has slipped past or over or under rock on the other side.

Faults can be ancient, locked, and apparently dormant for tens of thousands of years, and then erupt into activity in seconds. They can be kilometres long or thousands of kilometres long. The Alpine Fault in New Zealand runs for 850 kilometres; the East Anatolian Fault in Turkey extends for 700 kilometres. By contrast, the fault that caused the 1989 Newcastle earthquake was estimated to measure only a few kilometres in length — and had never been detected.

The fault plane is the actual surface along which movement occurs. The angle of this surface from horizontal is its dip. Rock above the fault plane is the hanging wall; rock below is the foot wall. These terms, which sound archaic, come directly from the language of deep-shaft mining — miners who worked fault-bearing ore veins stood on the foot wall and hung their lanterns on the hanging wall.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 4][SECTION 2]

**H2:** 2. The Three Fault Types

Normal faults form under extensional stress — where the crust is being pulled apart. The hanging wall slides downward relative to the foot wall along a fault plane that is typically inclined at 50 to 60 degrees. Normal faults are characteristic of diverging plate boundaries and rift zones. In Australia, the Eastern Highlands were partly shaped by normal faulting as the continent drifted away from Antarctica. Visually, normal faults create distinctive stepped landscapes — the raised block (horst) stands above the dropped block (graben), producing fault scarps that can be read directly in the topography long after the last earthquake.

Reverse (thrust) faults are the inverse — they form under compressive stress, where the crust is being squeezed. The hanging wall is pushed upward and over the foot wall. Low-angle reverse faults (dipping at less than 45 degrees) are specifically termed thrust faults and are responsible for building mountain ranges. The collision of the Indian subcontinent with Eurasia has produced a vast zone of thrust faulting that has elevated the Himalayas — and continues to raise them at approximately 5 millimetres per year. Critically for Australia, the compressive stress generated by the northern margin of the Indo-Australian Plate is transmitted southward into the continent's interior, making reverse and thrust faulting the dominant mechanisms of Australian intraplate seismicity. The vast majority of moderate-to-large Australian earthquakes — including Newcastle and the series of events at Tennant Creek in 1988 — are reverse-fault mechanisms.

Strike-slip (transcurrent) faults occur where rock masses slide horizontally past each other, with little or no vertical component. The active fault surface is typically near-vertical. If the block on the far side of the fault moves to the right relative to the observer, it is a right-lateral or dextral fault (the San Andreas, the North Anatolian Fault in Turkey). If it moves to the left, it is left-lateral or sinistral. The Alpine Fault in New Zealand's South Island is a dextral strike-slip fault — it has produced approximately 480 kilometres of lateral displacement over the past 25 million years, the geological equivalent of shuffling two stacked decks of cards apart horizontally.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 4][SECTION 3]

**H2:** 3. Intraplate vs. Interplate Earthquakes

The majority of the world's seismic energy is released at or near tectonic plate boundaries — these are interplate earthquakes. They are often large because the stresses driving plate motion are continuously replenished by mantle convection. The great subduction zone events of 2004 and 2011; the 1906 San Francisco earthquake on the San Andreas transform fault; the 2023 Turkish earthquakes on the East Anatolian Fault — these are all interplate events, and they conform to the intuitive model of earthquakes as a plate boundary phenomenon.

Intraplate earthquakes are far more conceptually challenging. They occur within the rigid interior of a plate, far from any active boundary, on faults that may be entirely invisible at the surface. Their driving mechanism is disputed and likely varies between regions. In Australia, the dominant hypothesis is that compressive stress from the collision at the northern plate margin is transmitted laterally across the rigid continent, reactivating ancient faults that formed under entirely different stress regimes hundreds of millions of years ago.

The geological structure beneath much of Australia is extraordinarily old — the cratons of Western Australia contain some of the oldest exposed rock on Earth, in excess of 4 billion years. These ancient terrains contain innumerable fossil faults and shear zones, many of which are poorly characterised, that can be reactivated if the contemporary stress field aligns with their orientation. This is why Australian earthquake hazard is difficult to forecast: the seismogenic faults are often ancient, deeply buried, and revealed only by the earthquakes themselves.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 4][SECTION 4]

**H2:** 4. Focal Depth and Its Consequences

Beyond fault type and plate setting, one further classification governs an earthquake's potential for destruction: focal depth. Earthquakes are divided into three broad categories based on the depth of their hypocenter.

Shallow-focus earthquakes (0–70 km) are the most destructive class. Because the rupture point is close to the surface, seismic energy has little distance over which to attenuate before reaching buildings and infrastructure. Nearly all historically destructive earthquakes fall into this category. The 22 February 2011 Christchurch earthquake was only 5 kilometres deep — an extreme case of shallow seismicity that maximised the shaking intensity at the surface.

Intermediate-focus earthquakes (70–300 km) are characteristic of the upper portions of subducting slabs, where the descending oceanic plate is still cold and brittle enough to fracture. They can produce significant shaking over a wide area but generally cause less concentrated surface damage than shallow events of comparable magnitude.

Deep-focus earthquakes (300–700 km) are scientifically enigmatic. At depths below approximately 50 kilometres, the pressure and temperature conditions should, in theory, prevent brittle fracture — rock should deform plastically rather than snap. Yet deep earthquakes do occur, and their mechanism remains an area of active research. Candidates include dehydration embrittlement (water released from descending oceanic rocks locally reducing effective pressure) and phase transitions in mineral crystal structure. Whatever their mechanism, deep earthquakes rarely cause major damage at the surface.

# PAGE 5: AUSTRALIA'S SEISMIC LANDSCAPE

`Sub-page: Australian_Seismicity/index.html`

## [SUB-PAGE 5][HERO]

**Breadcrumb:** The Trembling Earth / Feature · Australian Context

**H1:** Australia's Seismic Landscape

**[DECK]:**
Ask most Australians where the world's earthquake zones are, and they will draw you the Ring of Fire. Very few will draw a line through Newcastle, Tennant Creek, or Meckering. They should.

**Read time:** 11 min read

---

## [SUB-PAGE 5][SECTION 1]

**H2:** 1. The Myth of the Stable Continent

Australia is routinely described as geologically stable — and in certain respects, justifiably so. The ancient cratons that underpin much of the continent have not experienced significant tectonic deformation for hundreds of millions of years. Australia has no active composite volcanoes, no convergent plate boundary on its immediate margins, and no analogue of California's San Andreas system running through its cities.

This stability is real. But stability is not the same as safety, and geological quiescence over human timescales does not mean seismic inactivity. Australia has experienced at least six earthquakes of magnitude 6.0 or greater since European settlement, dozens of magnitude 5.0 or greater events, and a continuous background of smaller events recorded by Geoscience Australia's national seismograph network. The continent shakes — it simply does so in ways that feel counterintuitive to the plate-boundary model of earthquake hazard.

The important distinction is between seismic hazard (the probability that shaking of a given intensity will occur at a given location within a given time period) and seismic risk (the probability of loss, accounting for what is built in the hazard zone). Australia's seismic hazard, while lower per unit area than Japan or Chile, is concentrated in regions that are also densely built — Western Australia's Goldfields and Perth basin, the Hunter Valley, Adelaide, and the southeastern highlands. Our relatively low investment in seismic engineering, until recently, meant that the relationship between hazard and risk was poorly managed.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 5][SECTION 2]

**H2:** 2. The Indo-Australian Plate: A Continent Under Compression

Australia's seismicity is inseparable from the dynamics of the Indo-Australian Plate — a geological unit that carries not just Australia, but the Indian subcontinent, the floor of the Indian Ocean, and much of the surrounding region as a single rigid slab. It is one of the faster-moving plates in the world, carrying Australia northward at approximately 67 millimetres per year toward the Eurasian and Pacific plates.

The northern margin of the Indo-Australian Plate is one of the most tectonically complex regions on Earth. In the northwest, the Indian subcontinent is drilling into the underbelly of Eurasia, building the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau. In the northeast, the plate margins become a chaotic mosaic of microplates, island arcs, and back-arc basins across Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, and the southwestern Pacific. The cumulative compressive force generated by these collisions does not simply stop at the plate boundary. It propagates — as internal stress — deep into the rigid interior of the plate, including beneath Australia.

This stress is the engine of Australian seismicity. The mechanism is analogous to pushing on one end of a thick wooden board while the other end is braced against a wall: the board does not bend uniformly — it concentrates stress at pre-existing weaknesses in the wood. For Australia, those weaknesses are the ancient fossil faults of Precambrian and Palaeozoic age buried beneath the surface, periodically reactivated by a contemporary stress field they were never formed to accommodate.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 5][SECTION 3]

**H2:** 3. Where Australia Shakes: The Seismic Zones

Australian seismicity is not uniformly distributed. Geoscience Australia's national seismic hazard maps identify several regions of elevated activity.

Southwestern Western Australia is the most seismically active region in Australia by historical record. The 1968 Meckering earthquake (ML 6.9) — Australia's largest recorded intraplate earthquake in terms of moment release — produced a surface rupture more than 37 kilometres long, displacing the ground by up to 2.5 metres and completely destroying the small town for which it is named. The Yilgarn Craton, which underlies this region, contains an extraordinary density of Neoproterozoic fault structures that have proven susceptible to reactivation. Research at Geoscience Australia has identified multiple large-scale fault scarps across the Yilgarn and adjacent regions, some clearly produced by post-glacial earthquakes with estimated magnitudes of 6.0–7.5, suggesting that the largest earthquakes in Australian history may pre-date European settlement by thousands of years.

The Flinders Ranges and the Mt Lofty Ranges in South Australia sit above a zone of active reverse and thrust faulting associated with the compression of the Adelaide Fold Belt. Moderate earthquakes are relatively frequent in this region; the 1954 Adelaide earthquake (ML 5.4) caused significant damage to unreinforced masonry structures in the city.

Eastern Australia's highlands and basins — including the Hunter Valley, the Otway Basin, and the southeastern highlands — host scattered but significant seismic activity. The presence of major population centres in these regions (Newcastle, the Gold Coast hinterland, regional Victoria) gives this activity particular socioeconomic importance, as the Newcastle 1989 event demonstrated conclusively.

The Northern Territory hosts the extraordinary Tennant Creek sequence of January 1988, when three earthquakes of magnitude 6.3, 6.5, and 6.7 struck within 12 hours of each other along previously unknown faults beneath the Barkly Tableland. These events produced surface ruptures totalling more than 35 kilometres and remain one of the most significant intraplate seismic sequences ever recorded. The area was sparsely populated; had an equivalent sequence struck beneath Darwin or Alice Springs, the consequences would have been severe.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 5][SECTION 4]

**H2:** 4. The Hidden Hazard: Unmapped Faults

Perhaps the most troubling insight from Australian seismology is the one that applies globally but with particular force here: the most dangerous faults are often the ones we cannot see. This is not a failure of geological investigation — it reflects a fundamental constraint of seismic geology in stable continental interiors.

In tectonically active regions, fault activity is frequent enough that surface expression is fresh and readable. The San Andreas Fault is visible from an aircraft as a long linear trace of offset ridges, sag ponds, and deflected stream channels. By contrast, in stable continental regions, faults may slip once every 10,000 to 100,000 years. Their surface expressions, if they had any, are eroded beyond recognition by rainfall, vegetation, and time. The geological return period may be so long that no surface rupture has occurred in the Holocene — and without surface rupture, detection from above is largely impossible.

The Newcastle fault had no mapped surface expression. The fault that caused the devastating 22 February 2011 Christchurch earthquake was not on any pre-existing seismic hazard map. The lesson is not that seismic hazard assessment is futile — it is that probabilistic seismic hazard analysis must incorporate uncertainty in a rigorous and honest way, acknowledging that any given region may contain seismogenic structures that have simply not yet made themselves known.

This has direct implications for Australian urban planning, building codes, and infrastructure design. Geoscience Australia's 2018 National Seismic Hazard Assessment represented a significant revision of previous estimates, increasing design accelerations in a number of Australian cities. The revision was not driven by new earthquakes — it was driven by better understanding of the underlying fault systems and more sophisticated modelling of the stress environment.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 5][SECTION 5 — NEWCASTLE DEEP DIVE]

**H2:** 5. December 1989 — A City That Changed Australia's Building Standards

The Hunter Valley was, in 1989, an industrial and mining region that had grown substantially since the Second World War. Newcastle was — and remains — one of the largest coal export ports in the world. Its built environment reflected its history: a substantial stock of early-to-mid twentieth-century unreinforced brick and sandstock masonry buildings, commercial premises constructed before modern structural engineering codes, and a dense CBD sitting above alluvial sediments deposited over millennia by the Hunter River.

None of this was considered a seismic risk on the morning of 28 December 1989. The earthquake hazard maps that existed at the time either excluded Newcastle or classified it as a negligible risk zone.

At 10:27:34 AM local time, a rupture initiated approximately 11 kilometres below the surface on a northeast-trending reverse fault. The event lasted approximately 20 to 30 seconds. In that time, the ground accelerated at speeds sufficient to throw objects from shelves and fracture the mortar bonds of unreinforced brick walls. The Newcastle Workers Club — a single-storey brick and concrete structure on King Street in which a morning fitness class was in session — partially collapsed, killing nine people. Four more people died in other structural collapses across the CBD and inner suburbs. The total structural failure of masonry buildings designed with no seismic detailing whatsoever demonstrated, in the most direct terms, the danger of what engineers call seismic gap risk — the vulnerability of cities that assume their low historical earthquake frequency translates to low hazard.

The aftermath was as significant as the event itself. A Royal Commission examined the incident. The Standards Australia earthquake loading code (AS 1170.4) was comprehensively revised, extending mandatory seismic design provisions — previously limited to Western Australia and parts of South Australia — to the entire continent. Within a decade, Australia had fundamentally restructured the engineering requirements for new buildings in seismically active regions. The cost of this restructuring was real and ongoing. The cost of not doing it was demonstrated in under a minute on a summer morning in 1989.

# PAGE 6: WHEN THE GROUND SHAKES, WHAT FOLLOWS?

`Sub-page: Secondary_Hazards/index.html`

## [SUB-PAGE 6][HERO]

**Breadcrumb:** The Trembling Earth / Feature · Hazard Science

**H1:** When the Ground Shakes, What Follows?

**[DECK]:**
An earthquake is frequently only the first event in a cascade. The ground moves — and then it liquefies, the sea retreats and returns, the hillside gives way, the fires start, and the smaller shocks continue for months. Understanding the full hazard chain is as important as understanding the earthquake itself.

**Read time:** 10 min read

---

## [SUB-PAGE 6][SECTION 1]

**H2:** 1. Liquefaction — When Solid Ground Becomes Quicksand

Of all the secondary effects of earthquake shaking, liquefaction is among the most visually dramatic and the most consequential for built environments. It occurs when saturated, loosely packed sediments — particularly silts, fine sands, and reclaimed land — are subjected to rapid shaking that temporarily disrupts the grain-to-grain contact holding the sediment together.

The mechanism can be understood through a simple demonstration: fill a container with wet sand and compact it. It feels solid and will support weight. Now shake it vigorously. The shaking causes the water pressure between grains to increase suddenly, the grains lose frictional contact, and the mixture behaves temporarily as a dense liquid. Objects resting on the surface may sink. Buried objects that are lighter than the surrounding slurry — pipes, septic tanks, hollow foundations — may float upward. The ground can eject sand-water slurry through cracks in the surface as sand boils or sand fountains, leaving circular craters after the water drains away.

Critically, liquefaction does not require a large earthquake. It is primarily a function of shaking duration, shaking intensity, and soil saturation. Areas most at risk are those built on young alluvial sediments, coastal reclaimed land, and river delta deposits — precisely the terrains that historically have been favoured for urban development due to their flat topography and easy excavation.

The 2010–2011 Canterbury earthquake sequence produced the most comprehensively studied liquefaction event in the modern era. During the Mw 7.1 Darfield earthquake of September 2010, significant liquefaction occurred across eastern Christchurch suburbs including Bexley, Richmond, and Dallington — areas built on the low-lying swamps and lagoons that had been progressively drained and filled during the city's expansion in the twentieth century. The February 2011 aftershock repeated and amplified the damage. An estimated 400,000 tonnes of material was ejected from the ground as sand boils across the southern suburbs. The cumulative settlement of the ground surface in some areas exceeded 600 millimetres. Entire residential suburbs — thousands of properties — were subsequently demolished and the land designated as permanently uninhabitable, not because the buildings themselves were immediately destroyed, but because the ground beneath them had been fundamentally compromised.

Australia's own vulnerability to liquefaction is not negligible. Much of metropolitan Adelaide is built on alluvial sediments derived from the Mt Lofty Ranges. Parts of coastal Sydney, Melbourne's Docklands, and Brisbane's floodplain suburbs contain fills and saturated deltaic deposits that would be susceptible to liquefaction under a significant local event.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 6][SECTION 2]

**H2:** 2. Tsunamis — The Ocean's Response

The word tsunami is Japanese: tsu (harbour) and nami (wave). It entered international scientific vocabulary after the 2004 Indian Ocean disaster, when a Mw 9.1–9.3 megathrust rupture off the northern coast of Sumatra generated a series of ocean waves that killed more than 230,000 people across 14 countries — the deadliest natural disaster in the recorded history of the Indian Ocean basin.

A tsunami is not a single wave but a train of waves — a wave train — generated by any rapid, large-scale displacement of the water column. The overwhelming majority of tsunamis are caused by submarine earthquakes, specifically those involving significant vertical displacement of the seafloor. The key factor is mechanism: not all submarine earthquakes generate tsunamis. A purely horizontal strike-slip rupture along a vertical fault plane causes lateral ground motion but little vertical seafloor displacement, and therefore generates minimal tsunami. A thrust (reverse) fault rupture, by contrast, lifts one side of the seafloor abruptly upward while the other subsides — displacing the overlying water column like a piston, sending energy outward in all directions.

In the open ocean, tsunami waves are practically undetectable to ships — they may be less than a metre in amplitude while spanning hundreds of kilometres from crest to crest, travelling at speeds of up to 900 km/h (comparable to a commercial aircraft). Their wavelength-to-depth ratio means that they behave as shallow-water waves across the entire depth of the Pacific or Indian Ocean, losing remarkably little energy over vast distances. As they approach the coast and the ocean floor shoals, their wavelength compresses and their amplitude increases dramatically — a process called shoaling. A wave that was imperceptible in 4,000 metres of open water can arrive at the coast as a wall of water 10, 20, or even 40 metres high.

Australia's tsunami exposure is primarily from the Sunda Trench subduction zone to the northwest — the same system that generated the 2004 disaster — and from the limited but real seismic hazard along the Macquarie Ridge and Kermadec Trench to the southeast. The Australian coast's most significant documented tsunami impact came in March 1994, when a magnitude 8.2 earthquake in the Banda Sea generated waves of 7 metres recorded on Babi Island and smaller, non-damaging waves along Western Australia's north coast. Geoscience Australia and the Bureau of Meteorology co-operate through the Joint Australian Tsunami Warning Centre, which operates continuous seismic monitoring and deep-ocean pressure sensors (DART buoys) to provide early warning of tsunamigenic events.

The critical public-education message — still imperfectly absorbed after 2004 — is that the ocean receding dramatically from the shore is not a curiosity. It is the trough of an incoming wave train, and it is among the most reliable natural warning signs of an imminent tsunami. In 2004, many beachgoers at Phuket walked toward the exposed seafloor to collect stranded fish. Minutes later, the first wave arrived.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 6][SECTION 3]

**H2:** 3. Landslides, Rock Falls, and Slope Failure

Earthquake shaking transmits energy through the substrate in all directions, including upward through hillsides and cliff faces. When the ground accelerates rapidly, any slope that is close to the margin of stability — held in place by friction, root systems, and internal cohesion — can be tipped into failure. Seismically triggered landslides are among the most underappreciated hazards associated with large earthquakes.

The relationship between earthquake magnitude and triggered landslide extent is non-linear and strongly influenced by local geology. Poorly consolidated materials — weathered regolith, saturated hillside soils, weak sedimentary layers — are disproportionately susceptible. The 1970 Ancash earthquake in Peru (Mw 7.9) triggered a catastrophic rock avalanche from the north peak of Nevados Huascaran that buried the towns of Ranrahirca and Yungay, killing an estimated 18,000 to 22,000 people — far more than the earthquake's direct shaking casualties.

Australia's topographic relief limits this hazard in the interior, but the eastern escarpment — the steep western face of the Great Dividing Range — hosts numerous areas of pre-existing instability in deeply weathered Triassic and Jurassic sedimentary sequences. A significant earthquake focused beneath this escarpment could trigger rock falls and slope failures across a broad area.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 6][SECTION 4]

**H2:** 4. Aftershocks — The Long Tail of a Rupture

An earthquake does not end when the main rupture stops. The redistribution of stress caused by the mainshock triggers a cascade of smaller earthquakes on the same fault and on adjacent structures — the aftershock sequence. For large earthquakes, this sequence can persist for months, years, or even decades, gradually declining in frequency but never entirely ceasing before blending into the regional background seismicity.

The empirical description of aftershock decay is given by Omori's Law (1894), which states that the frequency of aftershocks decreases inversely with time after the mainshock — rapidly at first, then progressively more slowly. A modified version, the Omori-Utsu law, is still widely used in operational earthquake forecasting. The magnitude of the largest expected aftershock is typically about one unit lower than the mainshock — so a Mw 7.0 mainshock would be expected to generate aftershocks up to roughly Mw 6.0.

The Canterbury sequence in New Zealand illustrated the lethal dimension of this statistical expectation. The Mw 6.3 event of 22 February 2011, which killed 185 people, was technically an aftershock of the September 2010 Mw 7.1 mainshock — occurring on a different but related fault structure activated by the stress redistribution of the larger event. Seismologists globally have debated whether the February event should be classified as a "triggered earthquake" rather than a conventional aftershock, given that it occurred on a separate fault. The terminological distinction matters less than the practical lesson: in the weeks and months following a significant earthquake, the hazard landscape has been fundamentally altered, and structures already damaged are exponentially more vulnerable to subsequent events of far lesser magnitude.

---

## [SUB-PAGE 6][SECTION 5]

**H2:** 5. Fire, Infrastructure Failure, and Cascade Effects

In densely built urban areas, an earthquake's damage to critical infrastructure can trigger secondary disasters entirely unrelated to ground shaking. Gas mains fractured by ground rupture or lateral spreading leak fuel into basements and street-level voids; if ignition sources are present, fire can spread rapidly through a building stock that is already structurally compromised and through streets that are impassable to fire-fighting vehicles due to collapsed buildings and debris.

The 1906 San Francisco earthquake (estimated Mw 7.9) is the definitive historical case: the earthquake itself caused substantial damage, but it was the fires — fed by ruptured gas mains and spread by wind through a city whose water mains had simultaneously failed — that destroyed approximately 28,000 buildings and rendered most of the city uninhabitable. Modern estimates suggest the fires caused three-quarters of the total property loss. In an era before reinforced concrete construction, this was predictable; in a modern city with buried electrical cables, pressurised gas distribution, and dense high-rise development, the cascade potential remains significant.

The February 2011 Christchurch earthquake simultaneously destroyed or damaged most of the CBD's wastewater system. For months after the earthquake, tens of thousands of households relied on portable chemical toilets while the infrastructure was repaired — a public health challenge of significant logistical complexity in a temperate climate, and a reminder that the most damaging long-term consequences of a major earthquake may be measured not in collapsed buildings but in the unravelling of the systems that make modern urban life possible.

### [SIDEBAR-POP] — HISTORICAL POP-OUT — Christchurch Liquefaction 2010-11

**H4:** The Ground That Wouldn't Hold

Of the many consequences of the Canterbury earthquake sequence, none reshaped Christchurch more profoundly than liquefaction. The eastern suburbs of the city — Bexley, Richmond, Dallington, Avonside, and others along the Avon River corridor — were built on the bed of former wetlands, lagoons, and tidal flats that had been progressively reclaimed and filled during the twentieth century. The sediments beneath these suburbs were young, saturated, and loosely consolidated — exactly the conditions that make liquefaction inevitable under sustained ground shaking.

During the Mw 7.1 Darfield earthquake of 4 September 2010, the ground across these suburbs ejected an estimated 400,000 tonnes of silty sand through cracks and fissures in the surface — sand boils, soil ejecta, and silt slurries that turned gardens and streets into grey moonscapes overnight. Buildings settled unevenly, cracking foundations and rendering many uninhabitable. The February 2011 aftershock repeated the process on an even larger scale, on ground already compromised and partially desiccated from the previous event.

The eventual decision by the New Zealand government to designate the worst-affected areas as permanent red zones — purchasing properties and removing residents — displaced approximately 8,000 households. The Avon River corridor that runs through the former residential zone has since been progressively transformed into a linear park and ecological restoration project. It is, in a very real sense, the ground reclaiming what it was always better suited to be.

The Christchurch liquefaction sequence is now used as a global reference dataset for engineers, urban planners, and seismologists studying the built-environment consequences of soil failure. It demonstrated, more comprehensively than any prior event, that the ground on which a city is built can be as much a determinant of earthquake loss as the buildings above it.

# GLOSSARY — Tooltip Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| hypocenter | The actual point underground where an earthquake rupture begins; also called the focus. The epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it. |
| epicenter | The point on the Earth's surface directly above the hypocenter. Ground shaking is typically most intense here, though depth and local geology also govern intensity. |
| elastic rebound | The theory explaining earthquake generation: rock deforms elastically under tectonic stress, then snaps back when the fault ruptures, releasing stored energy as seismic waves. |
| P-waves | Primary or compressional seismic waves — the fastest type, able to travel through solids and liquids. The first waves to arrive at a seismograph after a distant earthquake. |
| S-waves | Secondary or shear seismic waves — travel at roughly 60% of P-wave speed; cannot propagate through liquids. More destructive than P-waves. |
| surface waves | Seismic waves that travel along the Earth's surface rather than through its interior. The slowest but most amplitude-rich waves; responsible for most structural damage in large earthquakes. |
| Moment Magnitude Scale | The standard scale used by modern seismology to measure earthquake energy. Each whole-number increase represents approximately 31.6 times more energy, not the common misconception of 10 times. |
| subduction | The process by which a denser tectonic plate descends beneath a lighter one at a convergent boundary. Responsible for the world's largest earthquakes and most tsunamis. |
| megathrust | A reverse fault along a subduction zone interface, on which the world's largest earthquakes (magnitude 8.5+) occur. |
| intraplate earthquake | An earthquake occurring within the interior of a tectonic plate rather than at its boundary. Australia's earthquakes are primarily intraplate events. |
| liquefaction | The transformation of saturated, loosely packed sediment into a fluid-like state under rapid shaking. Buildings can sink, pipes can float, and the ground surface can eject sand boils. |
| tsunami | A train of ocean waves generated by rapid vertical displacement of the seafloor, usually caused by a thrust-fault submarine earthquake. Travels at up to 900 km/h in deep water. |
| seismic hazard | The probability that ground shaking of a given intensity will occur at a given location within a specified time period. Distinct from seismic risk, which incorporates what is built in the hazard zone. |
| aftershock | A smaller earthquake that follows the main event, caused by stress redistribution along the ruptured fault or adjacent structures. Can continue for months or years. |
| Indo-Australian Plate | The tectonic plate on which Australia rides, moving approximately 67 mm northward per year toward Eurasia. The compressive stresses generated by this motion are the primary driver of Australian seismicity. |
| neotectonic | Relating to tectonic activity in the geologically recent past (typically the past 2 million years). Australian neotectonic faults are responsible for most of the country's significant earthquake hazard. |
| sand boils | Circular vents in the ground surface through which liquefied sediment — a mixture of sand, silt, and water — is ejected during or after earthquake shaking. |
| seismic moment | The physical measurement of earthquake energy used to calculate moment magnitude: the area of fault rupture x average slip distance x rock stiffness. |
| Omori's Law | The empirical relationship describing how aftershock frequency decreases inversely with time after a mainshock. A foundational principle of operational earthquake forecasting. |
| wave shoaling | The process by which a tsunami wave compresses in wavelength and increases in amplitude as it enters shallow coastal water, transforming from an imperceptible open-ocean ripple into a destructive onshore wave. |
