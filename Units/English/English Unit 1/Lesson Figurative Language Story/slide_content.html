Using Figurative Language and Paper Planes

1. The Shape of a Story

A good story is like a rollercoaster.

It needs a structure to take the reader on an exciting journey, slowly building up tension before the thrilling drop!

The Narrative Arc

Orientation

Complication

Climax

Resolution

Who, where, when.

The problem arises.

The point of highest tension.

How the problem is solved.

Structure in "Paper Planes"

Orientation: Dylan and Grandpa in their dusty Outback home.

Complication: Jason destroys Dylan's plane / The bullying begins.

Climax: The final throw at the World Championships in Japan.

Resolution: Dylan makes peace / Grandpa is proud.

2. Descriptive Language

Structure is the skeleton.
Description is the skin and clothes.

We use Noun Groups and Verb Groups to add rich details to our writing.

Upgrading our Sentences

3. Figurative Language

Figurative language is the special effects of your story.

Similes

Metaphors

Personification

Idioms

Simile

Comparing two things using the words like or as.

Paper Planes Example:

"The paper plane shot forward like a silver bullet."

Metaphor

Comparing two things by saying one thing is another.

Paper Planes Example:

"Jason was a thunderstorm of anger when he lost."

Personification

Giving human qualities to animals or objects.

Paper Planes Example:

"The wind snatched the folded paper aggressively from Dylan's hands."

Idioms

Common phrases that have a hidden, figurative meaning—they don't mean exactly what the words say!

Paper Planes Example:

"When his plane crossed the finish line, Dylan was on cloud nine."

4. Bringing Characters to Life

When characters speak, we use Speech Marks (" ") to show their exact words.

Rule 1:

New Speaker, New Line!

Rule 2:

Punctuation goes INSIDE the closing speech mark.

Speech Marks Example

Let's look at how we punctuate conversation:

"Watch this!" yelled Dylan excitedly.

"It's just a piece of paper," muttered Jason.

"You'll see," Dylan replied with a grin.
