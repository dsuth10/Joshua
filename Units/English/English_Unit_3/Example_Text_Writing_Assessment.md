## W1 Text, audience and development

**Grade: B, provisional.** The B descriptor is:

> Creates a sequenced written persuasive text for an identified audience on a topic, developing and expanding on ideas and providing supporting details that draw information from sources.

### Persuasive purpose and position — Demonstrated

P1 states, “we should stop palm oil plantation”. P6 reinforces the position: “We ought to use everything in our power to save the orangutans from this danger.” These establish the desired change and maintain a persuasive purpose through to the ending. The writer consistently argues against plantation expansion/practice, although the precise scope of the proposed action is not clearly distinguished.

**Next step:** Specify exactly what action the audience should take. Is the request to stop clearing rainforest, change purchasing choices or support a particular proposal? Choose one and carry it through the introduction and conclusion.

### Identified audience and adaptation — Partly demonstrated

P1 opens with “Picture this”, drawing readers into a shared imagined scene. P4 appeals to personal consequences through “the forest that feeds everyone in your family”. P6 uses the inclusive “We need to stop this for Indonesia's rainforest, waterways and native animals.” These are purposeful attempts to involve readers emotionally.

However, the text shifts between general readers and people whose families depend directly on the forest. It does not clearly establish whether it addresses classmates, consumers, local residents or decision-makers. This is a limitation within the provisional B judgement, not evidence that a particular audience was definitely identified in planning.

**Next step:** Name or clearly establish the audience, then connect the argument to that audience's concerns and realistic ability to act. Confirm the intended audience with the teacher before finalising W1.

### Text sequence — Demonstrated

P2–P5 progress through “Firstly”, “Secondly”, “Thirdly” and “Fourth”, followed by “In conclusion” in P6. The text has an opening, supporting arguments, an opposing economic viewpoint and a conclusion. Readers can follow the broad stages of persuasion.

**Next step:** Make each stage contribute a distinct reason. Health, habitat and air pollution currently recur across several stages, so the argument sometimes circles back instead of progressing.

### Developing and expanding ideas — Demonstrated

P4 explains a process rather than simply asserting harm: “Also, tractors pack down the earth, which commonly stops water from sinking in and it also increases runoff.” The writer connects an activity to a mechanism and a consequence. P5 extends the argument by considering economic concerns and proposing alternatives: “cocoa, coffee, rubber, spices and so much more”.

The development is uneven. In P3, “our support will be that they are getting hurt badly just because of the getting destroyed” leaves the causal explanation incomplete. P5 lists alternatives without explaining whether they would address the economic concern in this context.

**Next step:** Follow each reason with a clear explanation of how it works and why it supports the requested action. Expand the alternative-crops argument with relevant research rather than a longer list.

### Supporting details — Demonstrated, with uneven quality

P4 gives concrete physical details: “Bulldozers are stripping tree cover and rich topsoil away during heavy tropical rains.” These details help the reader picture the process behind the erosion argument. P2 connects polluted waterways with “animals who rely on the water ways to stay hydrated”, explaining why the claimed pollution matters.

Some claims exceed the support shown. P6 claims “long-term lung failure”, while the text does not provide a specific source or explanation supporting that outcome. P1 first describes oil as “extracted from the fruit of palm trees” and then says “if we extract the oil from the ground”. This is an internal inconsistency in the student's explanation; no external fact-check is needed to identify it.

**Next step:** Check each major claim against the research. Correct the inconsistent explanation in P1 and replace broad health claims with accurately attributed, clearly explained evidence. Do not invent a statistic to make a point sound stronger.

### Information drawn from sources — Demonstrated attribution; accuracy not assessable

P3 states, “Greenpeace says that palm oil plantations are created through clearing tropical rainforest or agricultural land.” P4 begins, “WWF Australia says that, if we keep doing palm oil plantations, the land will slowly fade away.” These show an attempt to draw information from named sources, supporting the B judgement beyond C's general supporting-details requirement.

The text names organisations relevant to environmental advocacy, but the actual articles are absent. The report cannot confirm that the paraphrases accurately represent them or that all subsequent details came from those sources. Naming an organisation alone does not establish authoritative, accurate support throughout an A-level text.

**Next step:** Identify the source article or classroom resource for each researched claim and explain its relevant finding accurately. Formal referencing is not an invented grade requirement, but traceable evidence would make moderation and revision easier.

### Detail and whole-text cohesion at A — Partly demonstrated

P4 provides sustained detail, and P6 returns to the main environmental concerns. However, P1 introduces many arguments before the body develops them, while P3 moves from habitat loss to a partly expressed proposal and then air pollution. P3 ends with “We need to stop oil plantations”, restating the position without completing that paragraph's reasoning.

**Next step:** Give every paragraph a defined contribution and remove repeated claims that add no new explanation. Link each developed reason back to the same precise proposal.

**Why B rather than C or A:** Sequencing, explicit source attribution and developed causal explanations support B beyond C. B is a best fit with an audience qualification, not a finding that every component is secure. A is not sustained because whole-text cohesion, source precision and the quality of supporting detail are inconsistent. Research materials and an identified audience would help the teacher confirm or revise W1.

## W2 Paragraphs and cohesion

**Grade: B.** The B descriptor is:

> Uses sequenced paragraphs and cohesive devices to organise ideas with topic sentences to guide the reader through the text.

### Paragraphing and organisation — Demonstrated

The six paragraphs separate the opening, body arguments and conclusion. P5 starts, “Fourth, the Indonesian government strongly defends palm oil because it is a vital economic engine and renewable sources of energy.” This gives the economic argument its own space rather than burying it in the erosion discussion.

**Next step:** Preserve this separation while making each body's central reason distinct. Shorten the introduction so the body carries the detailed explanations.

### Development within paragraphs — Partly demonstrated

P4 develops a coherent erosion discussion through tree cover, bare soil, roots, compaction and runoff. P2 starts with “serious health problems” but later shifts to polluted water, extinction and environmental protest. Its supporting material is relevant to the overall position but not tightly selected for one paragraph focus.

**Next step:** Keep P2 focused on one health or pollution argument, and move the habitat material to P3. Use a reason, evidence, explanation and link as an editing scaffold rather than a compulsory formula.

### Paragraph sequence — Demonstrated

The numbered transitions and conclusion marker show deliberate order. P5's economic discussion provides a useful change from environmental harms before the final appeal.

**Next step:** Order the body by distinct ideas, then use transitions that explain the relationship between them. Numbered connectives establish order but do not by themselves show why one reason follows another.

### Links between ideas and paragraphs — Partly demonstrated

P5 uses “However, environmentalist groups argue” to shift from the government's view to an objection, and “Alternatively, farmers are more than capable of growing crops” to introduce a proposed response. These links clarify the local argument. Across P2 and P3, similar habitat and pollution concerns recur without a clear statement of what the second paragraph adds.

**Next step:** Make the opening of P3 identify its distinct focus on habitat loss. End each body paragraph by explaining how that reason supports the proposal, rather than repeating only that plantations should stop.

### Cohesive devices — Demonstrated

P2 uses “For example” to introduce support, P5 uses “However” and “Alternatively” to signal argumentative relationships, and P6 uses “In conclusion” to signal closure. Pronouns and repeated topic words also maintain the topic.

Some references are vague: P2's “This to, causes polluted water” makes the reader work to identify what “This” means. Repeated additions such as “additionally” do not always clarify the relationship between claims.

**Next step:** Replace vague references with the precise process or cause being discussed. Select connectives for their meaning: cause, consequence, contrast or example.

### Topic sentences — Demonstrated, uneven in strength

P2 has a clear controlling claim: “Firstly, not only does it destroy the rainforest, but palm oil plantation also causes serious health problems.” P5 likewise announces its economic focus. P3 and P4 begin with source attribution, but their opening claims are less precise; “the land will slowly fade away” in P4 does not identify erosion clearly.

**Next step:** State the paragraph's reason directly before introducing its supporting source. Make the rest of the paragraph fulfil that promise.

### Range of well-structured paragraphs and effective linking at A — Partly demonstrated

The introduction uses an imaginative opening, P4 explains a process, P5 considers a competing view, and P6 concludes. This shows variety of function. The diffuse focus of P2, incomplete development of P3 and repeated material prevent the paragraphs from consistently organising and linking ideas effectively.

**Next step:** Revise paragraph focus before adding more connectives. Clear selection and order of ideas will do more for cohesion than extra transition words.

**Why B rather than C or A:** The evidence goes beyond simply using paragraphs: sequenced openings, topic sentences and a range of cohesive devices guide the reader, supporting B. A's consistently well-structured and effectively linked paragraphs are not yet demonstrated across the text.

## W3 Language features

**Grade: B.** The B descriptor is:

> Uses language features including complex sentences that have expanded noun groups, tenses, topic-specific vocabulary, and literary devices.

### Complex sentences — Demonstrated, with inconsistent control

P1 writes, “If we keep this up, then Indonesia's beautiful rainforest will be gone, and the constant deforestation will cause the air to become more soiled than in the beginning.” The subordinate conditional clause is “If we keep this up”; the main clause begins “then Indonesia's beautiful rainforest will be gone”. The additional coordinated clause extends the predicted consequence. This is evidence of a compound-complex structure, although the final wording is imprecise.

P2 uses a relative clause in “animals who rely on the water ways to stay hydrated”. However, P2's “While environmentalist fight and protest to protect the environment and the animals that live in it as well.” is a dependent clause left as a separate sentence. P4's “In the young palm trees, there are wide gaps between them, they also lack the number of roots to hold the dirt together.” joins independent clauses with a comma and has an awkward opening.

**Next step:** Check that every sentence has a complete main clause. Split overloaded sentences and join independent clauses with suitable punctuation or conjunctions. Complexity should make relationships clear rather than simply make sentences longer.

### Expanded noun groups — Demonstrated

P1's “Indonesia's beautiful rainforest” has the head noun *rainforest*, expanded by the possessive *Indonesia's* and adjective *beautiful*. It appears inside the complex sentence above. P4's “heavy tropical rains” expands the head noun *rains* with two modifiers. P5's “millions of small-scale farmers” uses a quantity expression with further information identifying the people concerned.

These choices provide specificity and help readers picture what is at risk. Some descriptions elsewhere are less precise, such as P1's “different variations”.

**Next step:** Choose modifiers that add useful detail or persuasive meaning. Replace vague additions with specific, accurate descriptions rather than stacking more adjectives.

### Tenses and their effect — Demonstrated; deliberate effect partly demonstrated

P2 uses the present tense in “causes serious health problems” to present a general claim. P1's “will be gone” predicts a consequence, creating urgency. These forms suit the argument's movement between current claims and possible future outcomes. P6's “We ought to use” expresses obligation through a modal; it is not a separate tense.

Agreement errors reduce control: P6's “palm oil plantations destroys the rainforest” mismatches a plural subject and singular verb. This is an agreement problem, not evidence of a different tense. P1's “where it was happening” also introduces an unclear past-time reference into a general explanation.

**Next step:** Keep general explanations in a consistent present-time frame, reserve predictions for consequences, and check subject–verb agreement. Use time shifts deliberately rather than accidentally.

### Topic-specific vocabulary — Demonstrated, with uneven precision

P1 includes “biofuels” and “deforestation”; P4 uses “topsoil” and “runoff”; P5 includes “small-scale farmers”. These help the writer discuss environmental and economic aspects specifically.

P1's “air to become more soiled”, P4's “land will slowly fade away” and P6's “habitational lost” are less clear. Ambitious vocabulary is useful only when it communicates the intended meaning accurately.

**Next step:** Prefer precise familiar terms over unclear elaborate phrasing. Check environmental terms against the research and proofread noun/verb forms in context.

### Literary devices and persuasive effect — Demonstrated

P1's “Picture this” creates an imagined scene, followed by “imagine that same rainforest absolutely demolished”. This contrast invites readers to feel the loss. “All of it just gone” and “All gone because” repeat the idea of disappearance for emphasis. P2's “turn a blind eye” is an idiom portraying inaction as deliberate disregard. P6 asks, “Do you really want to see a world where you starve because of the actions people make?” This rhetorical question directly challenges the reader.

The devices show range and some effective engagement, but the final starvation appeal makes a large claim that the preceding argument has not established. Frequent repeated harm can also dilute the strongest points.

**Next step:** Retain the opening contrast, use repetition selectively, and end with a question or appeal proportionate to the evidence and suited to the identified audience.

### Creative range and sustained engagement at A — Partly demonstrated

The opening imagery, repetition, inclusive language, rhetorical questions and topic vocabulary show promising variety. They are not consistently combined with controlled syntax and precise expression. P3's incomplete explanation interrupts meaning, and P6's long sentence beginning with the government's position is difficult to follow.

**Next step:** Edit for clarity before adding further devices. Aim for deliberate variation between concise emphasis and well-controlled explanatory sentences.

**Why B rather than C or A:** The student demonstrates the C features and also expanded noun groups within complex sentences, supporting the B descriptor. The rubric does not require error-free B writing. A is not yet supported because creative, engaging language is uneven and sentence control and precision frequently weaken the effect. Presence of features is stronger than sustained mastery here.

## Priority improvement plan

### 1 Make every explanation complete and accurate

Begin with P1's conflicting descriptions of extraction, P3's incomplete habitat explanation and the broad health claims. Check the original research, then state one clear cause and consequence. This improves the reader's understanding and confidence in the argument.

Original, P3: “our support will be that they are getting hurt badly just because of the getting destroyed”.

**Teacher-modelled revision:** “The destruction of rainforest threatens orangutans' homes. Protecting their habitat should be a priority.” This models a complete explanation using the student's existing argument; add verified supporting detail from the student's source before treating it as research-supported writing.

### 2 Give each paragraph one clear purpose

Keep the habitat discussion together, give the erosion explanation its own paragraph, and place the economic viewpoint and response together. Remove duplicated material from the introduction and conclusion. This would move the organisation towards A's effective development and linking.

Original, P4: “WWF Australia says that, if we keep doing palm oil plantations, the land will slowly fade away.”

**Teacher-modelled revision:** “Soil erosion is another reason to protect the rainforest.” Follow this topic sentence with an accurate paraphrase of the available source and a clear explanation. The model deliberately leaves the unverified attribution out of the replacement sentence until it can be checked.

### 3 Control sentence boundaries and verb forms

Original, P2: “While environmentalist fight and protest to protect the environment and the animals that live in it as well.”

**Teacher-modelled revision:** “Environmentalists fight to protect the environment and the animals that live in it.” This supplies a main clause and corrects the plural noun while preserving the intended idea.

Original, P6: “In conclusion palm oil plantations destroys the rainforest.”

**Teacher-modelled revision:** “In conclusion, palm oil plantations destroy the rainforest.” This models punctuation and agreement only; the breadth of the claim still needs checking against the research.

### 4 Address a specific audience with an achievable request

Choose the audience first. If it is classmates, explain an action they can realistically take; if it is decision-makers, frame a specific proposal for them. Replace the broad final starvation question with a supported appeal linked to that action. Keep the engaging opening and strongest imagery.
