Paws at School

How can a strong proposal convince the principal?

Pets at School

How can we ask the principal clearly?

THE PROPOSAL Allow a carefully planned Pet Learning Day.
Part A · Write→Plan→Part B · Speak
●●●

DO Ask students to predict two questions the principal would ask before approving the proposal.

WORK Explain that the lesson studies how a model writer earns agreement; it is not a class vote about pets.

RECORD Students write: audience = principal; action = approve one planned Pet Learning Day.

FINISH When students can name the audience and exact action.

CHECK Listen for safety and animal-welfare questions, not only excitement.

1 Open with a picture — then make the request

MODEL INTRODUCTION

Imagine walking into school and seeing a calm dog beside its owner, a rabbit resting in a safe pen and a fish tank ready for observation. I believe our principal should allow students to bring approved pets to school for a special Pet Learning Day. With clear rules, pets could help us learn about care, responsibility and living things.
Imagine seeing a calm dog, a safe rabbit pen and a fish tank at school. Our principal should allow an approved Pet Learning Day. We could learn about animals and responsibility.

MINI-WHITEBOARDS FIRST

Which part tells the principal exactly what should happen?

Look for the sentence that names the audience's decision: should allow.

HOOK+OPINION+REASON PREVIEW

DO Read the whole introduction once before analysing it. Collect all-student choices on mini-whiteboards.

WORK Students decide which highlighted section states the proposal and justify with its modal verb.

RECORD Hook + opinion + preview.

FINISH When students explain why the stance, not the hook, contains the decision.

CHECK Misconception: the most exciting sentence is automatically the opinion.

2 Turn a reason into a supported reason

REASON 1 · RESPONSIBILITY

First, a Pet Learning Day could teach us to care for animals. Students could prepare information cards about what their pet eats, needs and how to keep it safe. The Queensland Department of Education says schools have a duty of care and must protect animals from hunger, discomfort, pain and fear. This means the day would be a real lesson, not just playtime.
First, we could learn how to care for animals. We could explain what each pet eats and needs. Queensland school guidance says people must keep animals safe and comfortable. This would be learning, not only play.

CHOOSE, THEN EXPLAIN

Which detail makes this reason more trustworthy?

A strong supporting detail tells us where the information came from.

REASON→NAMED SOURCE→THIS MEANS…

DO Contrast the bare claim “Pets teach responsibility” with the complete evidence chain.

WORK Students identify the named source and explain what it proves: schools can plan animal learning responsibly.

RECORD Reason → supporting detail → explanation.

FINISH When students can say that the source supports responsible planning, not a promise that every student will improve.

CHECK Misconception: a named source automatically proves every claim in the paragraph.

3 Explain how the reason could work

REASON 2 · MEMORABLE LEARNING

Second, pets could make learning memorable and help students share knowledge. A student might explain how a bird uses its beak or how a guinea pig communicates. We could listen, ask questions and learn from one another. What better way to study living things than observing them carefully?
Second, pets could help us share what we know. A student could explain how a bird eats or how a guinea pig communicates. The class could observe, listen and ask questions.

BUILD THE EXPLANATION

Put the thinking chain in order

Start with the action students do. Finish with the learning result.

DO Ask students to predict the causal order aloud before touching the screen.

WORK Select the three cards in order, then join them orally with because, so and this means.

RECORD One complete explanation chain.

FINISH When students can explain the middle step rather than jumping from pet attendance straight to learning.

CHECK Misconception: an enjoyable activity automatically produces learning without a teaching action.

4 Treat a concern fairly — then solve it

THE PRINCIPAL MAY ASK: What about allergies, bites, mess and frightened animals?

The best response respects the concern and gives the principal rules that can be checked.

“These are fair concerns. However, the school could…”
“That worry is fair. However, clear rules could keep people and pets safe.”

DO Have students vote before selecting. Require: “The plan is strongest because…”

WORK Test each response against three criteria: respects the concern, protects people, protects animals.

RECORD Concern + however + workable rule.

FINISH When students reject both dismissal and total surrender.

CHECK The official boundary is stricter than “bring any pet”: approval, risk planning and supervision are essential.

5 Finish with a request the principal can act on

In conclusion, our principal should allow a carefully planned Pet Learning Day. It would teach responsibility, make learning memorable and let students share knowledge. With strong safety and welfare rules, we can protect people and pets. Please give us one trial day and let us show that paws, claws and fins can belong in learning!
Our principal should approve one planned Pet Learning Day. We could learn about animals while clear rules keep everyone safe. Please let us try one day.

A strong call to action is specific: approve one safe trial day.

DO Read the conclusion without labels. Ask students to name each sentence's job before revealing.

WORK Reveal restatement, summary and action in order.

RECORD Students draft a precise call to action for their own audience.

FINISH When calls to action name an audience decision, not only “help now”.

CHECK Ask: Could the principal tell exactly what to approve?

Allergies and safety + approval, handler, hygiene, no-contact option.

Restate, summarise and request one trial day.

8 Assessment-ready: can your audience follow the case?

Part A · Written

□ Clear audience and opinion

□ Hook and reason preview

□ Two reasons with supporting details

□ Fair concern and workable response

□ Specific call to action

Part B · Spoken

□ Use writing as a guide, not a script

□ Look at the audience

□ Pause at an important moment

□ Change pace, tone or volume for meaning

□ Rehearse, receive feedback and revise
