# Exemplar Lesson: Paws at School

### Pedagogical Contemplation

1. **Cognitive goal:** Students identify how a persuasive model moves from an opinion to reasons, supporting details, a fair concern and a workable call to action, then transfer those moves into their own assessment planning.
2. **Interactive alignment:** The lesson uses short compare, sequence and choose tasks because students must distinguish the *job* of each persuasive move, not merely remember labels.
3. **Visible thinking:** Mini-whiteboard choices, evidence selection, causal sequencing and rebuttal justification show whether students can connect a claim to a reason and respond to the principal's likely concerns.
4. **Pedagogy and engagement:** The school-pet proposal creates a familiar, appealing decision. The learning remains assessment craft: audience, structure, support, responsible persuasion and spoken delivery.

## Lesson purpose

Students deconstruct an assessment-style exemplar that persuades the principal to permit a carefully planned **Pet Learning Day**. The model is deliberately pitched with short, concrete sentences and accessible evidence chains. No student-facing material names a year level or ability group.

The proposition is not unrestricted access for any pet. The exemplar proposes pets approved in advance, an adult handler, supervision, hygiene routines, a no-contact option and immediate removal of any animal showing stress.

## Learning intention

We are learning how a persuasive writer uses reasons, supporting details and a fair response to concerns to convince a specific audience.

## Success criteria

- I can find the hook, opinion and preview in an introduction.
- I can explain how a detail supports a reason.
- I can connect a reason to a likely result using **because**, **so** or **this means**.
- I can acknowledge a fair concern and answer it with a workable rule.
- I can identify the restated opinion and call to action in a conclusion.
- I can mark a spoken section for pace, pause, volume or emphasis.

## Finished student evidence

Students complete a six-part assessment plan for their own negotiated topic:

1. audience and clear opinion;
2. hook;
3. first reason plus supporting detail;
4. second reason plus supporting detail;
5. fair concern plus response;
6. conclusion and specific call to action.

They then annotate and rehearse one 30–45 second section, using at least two purposeful voice choices.

## Interaction design matrix

| Learning moment | Cognitive demand | Mode | Why it exposes thinking | Tier 2 hint | Placement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Distinguish the writer's position from scene-setting | Compare and choose | Students must identify the sentence that tells the principal exactly what should happen | Look for **I believe** and the named action | Guided noticing |
| Reason and evidence | Select the detail that makes a reason more trustworthy | Evidence choice | Reveals confusion between another opinion and support from a named source | Which option tells us **who says it** and what schools must do? | Model paragraph 1 |
| Explanation chain | Connect action, learning and result | Sequencing | Shows whether students can explain *how* the proposed day could support learning | Begin with what students do; finish with what the class learns | Model paragraph 2 |
| Concern and response | Judge a rebuttal against audience needs | Compare and justify | Reveals whether students dismiss a concern or solve it fairly | The principal needs a rule that protects both people and animals | Counterargument |
| Conclusion | Identify structural purpose | Annotated reveal | Connects restatement, summary and action to the independent conclusion | What exactly is the principal being asked to approve? | Model conclusion |
| Spoken delivery | Apply voice features to meaning | Rehearsal timer | Teacher hears whether emphasis and pauses make the argument clearer | Emphasise the request; pause before the final action | Part B rehearsal |

## Core sequence — approximately 55 minutes

1. **The principal's decision — 4 minutes:** Students predict the rules a principal would need before approving pets at school.
2. **Introduction blueprint — 6 minutes:** Read the opening and identify hook, opinion and preview.
3. **Reason 1: responsibility — 7 minutes:** Compare a bare assertion with a reason supported by Queensland Department of Education guidance.
4. **Reason 2: memorable learning — 6 minutes:** Build the explanation chain from observation to sharing to learning.
5. **Fair concern, workable response — 8 minutes:** Evaluate responses to allergies, safety, hygiene and animal stress.
6. **Conclusion — 5 minutes:** Locate the restated opinion, summary and trial-day request.
7. **Planning map — 8 minutes:** Transfer the six moves to an individual assessment topic.
8. **Voice coach — 7 minutes:** Mark and rehearse a 30–45 second section with a partner.
9. **Exit evidence — 4 minutes:** Improve one bare claim by adding a reason, supporting detail or audience-aware action.

## Model persuasive text

### Paws at School: Let Us Hold a Pet Learning Day

Imagine walking into school and seeing a calm dog beside its owner, a rabbit resting in a safe pen and a fish tank ready for observation. I believe our principal should allow students to bring approved pets to school for a special Pet Learning Day. With clear rules, pets could help us learn about care, responsibility and living things.

First, a Pet Learning Day could teach us to care for animals. Students could prepare information cards about what their pet eats, needs and how to keep it safe. The Queensland Department of Education says schools have a duty of care and must protect animals from hunger, discomfort, pain and fear. This means the day would be a real lesson, not just playtime.

Second, pets could make learning memorable and help students share knowledge. A student might explain how a bird uses its beak or how a guinea pig communicates. We could listen, ask questions and learn from one another. What better way to study living things than observing them carefully?

Some people may worry about allergies, bites, mess or frightened animals. These are fair concerns. However, the school could approve pets before the day, require an adult handler, provide a no-contact area and make everyone wash their hands. Dangerous, sick or unsuitable pets would stay home, and any animal showing stress would leave immediately. Queensland guidance says animal activities need risk planning, safe handling, good hygiene and principal approval for classroom dog visits.

In conclusion, our principal should allow a carefully planned Pet Learning Day. It would teach responsibility, make learning memorable and let students share knowledge. With strong safety and welfare rules, we can protect people and pets. Please give us one trial day and let us show that paws, claws and fins can belong in learning!

## Support and access

- Use the unlabelled language switch to show shorter paired wording.
- Read the exemplar aloud before asking students to identify craft moves.
- Provide the six planning prompts as movable cards or desk strips.
- Allow pointing, oral explanation, speech-to-text or adult scribing.
- Accept one developed reason plus a fair concern before adding a second reason.
- Keep the same destination: an audience-aware opinion supported by details and a workable response.

## Extension

- Test whether the proposal is precise enough for a real principal to approve.
- Distinguish evidence that proves feasibility from evidence that proves a learning benefit.
- Add a monitoring and review condition after the trial day.
- Revise one exaggerated statement into a cautious, defensible claim.

## Accuracy and welfare boundaries

- Do not promise that pets will make every student calm, happy or successful.
- Do not claim that any pet may attend school.
- Treat allergies, fear, hygiene, injury and animal stress as genuine concerns.
- Dogs are not suitable as permanent classroom pets under the Queensland guidance used here, except assistance or support contexts; short classroom visits require principal approval and supervision.
- A real activity requires school-specific approval, risk planning and animal-welfare arrangements.
