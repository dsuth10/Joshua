## Assessment conventions and resources

Purpose: To create a written persuasive text developing and expanding on ideas with supporting details. To present an opinion to an audience.

Technique: Extended response (Part A); Observed demonstration (Part B)

Conditions

Individual work.

Provide the task to students in a way that is age appropriate.

Suggested length: 200–400 words (informed by QCAA guidelines) (Part A).

Suggested length: 1–3 minutes (informed by QCAA guidelines) (Part B).

Provide lesson time for drafting with access to teacher feedback and conferencing.

May be completed over multiple lessons.

## Implementation advice

## Part A: Writing and creating

Students plan, create and edit a written persuasive text expanding on ideas with supporting details for an identified audience.

Instructions

Negotiate a topic with students which is of interest to them and/or relevant to the school context. For example, they may choose to persuade an identified audience about:

Actions in the school or community

an action to be taken (for example: walk to school day, fundraising for a cause)

an item or activity they would like to see included in a school community (for example: Storytime dog, kitchen garden, chess club, yoga class, cold water bubbler)

a response to an issue.

Guide students to plan by reflecting on the identified audience and action/item/activity.

Guide students to research their identified topic and gather differing ideas, evidence or reasons from a variety of sources, including authoritative sources (print, digital, online, interviews and/or the opinions of others), to support their argument.

<br>

Guide students to organise information and create their text using:

text structures appropriate to the task

language features appropriate to the audience.

Students may use the **Sheet — Planning (optional)** to plan their ideas and arguments.

Prompt students to review and edit their text for spelling (monitoring strategy), punctuation and meaning.

## Part B: Speaking and listening

Students present an opinion on a topic with supporting arguments to an identified audience using features of voice.

Instructions

Guide students to:

use their written text as a guide to the structure of their spoken text and annotate and adapt it, identifying where they could use emphasis, literary devices, persuasive techniques, figurative language, and features of voice such as variations in pitch, tone, pace and volume for effect

rehearse their spoken text with a partner or small group and provide feedback to each other including asking and answering questions about the topic

include any new, relevant and impromptu ideas that develop and expand on their ideas

use visual and digital features (optional), as appropriate, for effect.

Provide opportunities for students to present their spoken text to the identified audience.

Teachers may use the Sheet — Presentation: Teacher recording sheet (optional) to observe and record evidence against presentation skills.

## Part A: Writing and creating

**P****lan**, **create** and **edit** a persuasive text developing and expanding on ideas with supporting details for an audience.

Choose a topic with your teacher.

Plan your text.

Identify your audience and purpose.

Decide what your opinion is on the topic. Consider describing your opinion. Ask *yourself**,* *‘Why should this happen?’, ‘Who will benefit?’*

Identify your supporting arguments.

Research your topic and gather differing ideas from a variety of sources. For example:

Ask for community opinions.

Consider differing opinions.

Research the benefits of your opinion on the topic, for example: health benefits, social benefits.

Use authoritative sources to support your topic.

Identify your sources and use facts or quotes (for example, ‘According to Storytime Org research, story dogs are great for wellbeing, as noted Storytime expert, Ima Hound said, “ … ”.’)

You may choose to use a planning sheet to organise your research and ideas.

Create your persuasive text. Think about how you will appeal to and persuade your audience. Include:

- *Text structure*

The stages of an argument.

Introduction to engage the audience.

Supporting arguments, developing and expanding on ideas in paragraphs with topic sentences to guide the reader and sequence ideas.

Supporting information, including research, to justify your argument.

Conclusion.

- *Language features*

A range of sentences including compound and complex sentences with strong starting points to guide the reader.

Expanded noun groups for fuller descriptions.

Relevant verb tense.

Subjective language to reflect opinions and objective language to support arguments.

Direct speech and quotations.

Topic-specific vocabulary.

Cohesive devices including using the starting point of a sentence or paragraph to give prominence to the message and to guide the reader through the text.

Literary devices and vocabulary including persuasive techniques such as rhetorical questions and repetition and figurative language to persuade the reader.

Review and edit your text for spelling, punctuation and meaning.

## Part B: Speaking and listening

**Present and justify your opinion **

Your written work is a great starting point for your presentation. Think of it as your guide, not a script. Instead of reading every word, try to talk to your audience. Speak in a way that feels natural and convincing, like you’re really trying to get them to agree with your opinion.

- Prepare your presentation.

- Look for ways to make your presentation even more convincing and engaging. Annotate your written speech to identify where you could include:

details and elaborations to expand ideas

literary devices, such as persuasive techniques and figurative language

variations in tone, pitch, pace, volume and emphasis.

You may like to include multimodal elements such as images, animations, costumes, props, sound effects, and music to make your speech engaging.

Practise presenting to a partner or small group.

Respond to your partner’s opinions about the topic and feedback about your presentation, making any changes that will improve your speech.

Deliver your opinion to the audience. Make sure you include:

a structured spoken text with an introduction that states your opinion, arguments and a conclusion

sequenced arguments with supporting details that share, develop and expand on ideas from your research, and take into account a variety of views including authoritative sources and the opinions of others

objective and subjective language

modal verbs

literary devices (for example: persuasive techniques, exaggeration, repetition, figurative language)

topic-specific vocabulary

appropriate formality

voice effects such as tone, pitch, pace, volume and expressions to emphasise key points and engage the audience

appropriate multimodal elements to support the persuasive effect of your speech (optional).
