# Teacher Guide: The Borrow Box

## At a glance

| Item | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Audience | Years 5-6 |
| Core time | Approximately 55 minutes |
| Final product | 7-9 sentence letter to a fictional principal proposing a four-week equipment-library trial |
| Explicit move | ACK bridge: Acknowledge, Connect, Keep the claim |
| Core misconception | A counterargument is an insult, or acknowledging a concern means abandoning a claim |
| Print | Reading Pack + ACK Organiser; Concise Access Pack where appropriate |
| Projected language | Red = extended class language; green = concise language |

## Central teaching point

A persuasive writer becomes more credible when they represent a concern their audience could actually hold. The response must meet that exact concern with a reason, relevant detail or safeguard. Repeating the original claim is not a rebuttal; attacking the audience is not a counterargument.

Keep these distinctions visible:

1. **Acknowledge is not agree.**
2. **A fair concern is not a straw man.**
3. **A rebuttal answers the concern; it does not change the subject.**
4. **A trial is a measured action, not a promise of perfect results.**

## Red-green presentation control

The unlabelled switch at the top right changes projected language on the current slide immediately.

- **Red:** extended disciplinary language and a 7-9 sentence letter.
- **Green:** concise plain language and a 4-6 sentence response.
- Both routes use the same claim, fair concern, response and returned claim.

## Reading answers

### The main claim

Riverbank Primary should run a four-week Borrow Box trial at lunch, offering shared equipment such as skipping ropes, chalk, balls, card games and drawing boards.

### Fair concerns

- equipment may be lost, damaged or taken by one group;
- a collection queue may reduce valuable lunch play time;
- a busy system could add work for staff or student helpers;
- students who prefer quiet or independent activities could be forgotten;
- the trial could use money or space that another school need could use.

### Responses and safeguards

- use a simple sign-out card, labelled tub and equipment count;
- trial one short collection window and record waiting time;
- use a rotating volunteer roster rather than expecting one person to manage it;
- include quiet, active and choice-based equipment;
- begin with existing donated or low-cost equipment and review use before expanding.

### Unfair language to mark X

- "People who worry about lost things do not care about fun."
- "Teachers always make simple ideas difficult."
- "Anyone who says no wants students to be bored."

These statements assume motives and make disagreement easier to dismiss. They do not respond to the concern.

## ACK bridge model

| Move | What the writer does | Model wording |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | States the proposal | Riverbank Primary should trial The Borrow Box for four weeks. |
| Acknowledge | Names a real concern and why it matters | Some people may worry that shared equipment will be lost or that the queue will take up lunch time. Those are sensible concerns because a system that is frustrating or wasteful will not be fair to students. |
| Connect | Answers the exact concern | However, a labelled tub, quick sign-out card and end-of-lunch count would let us test a simple routine instead of guessing. |
| Keep | Returns to the claim with an action | Therefore, the school should run a small trial, record waiting times and missing items, then decide what to improve. |

## Rebuttal choice answer

Concern: "The Borrow Box may take too long to collect at the start of lunch."

- "Fun is more important than waiting." - repeats the opinion and ignores the concern.
- "People who complain about queues never support good ideas." - attacks people.
- "A five-minute collection window and a class roster could limit waiting time; the trial can record whether that works." - direct rebuttal: it offers a relevant, testable safeguard.

## Conference prompts

- What would a careful listener genuinely worry about?
- Have you stated that concern in words they would accept?
- Which part of your response answers that exact worry?
- Does your safeguard happen during the trial, or is it only a wish?
- Where do you return to your claim after the concern?
- Which word makes your response fair rather than dismissive?

## Formative coding

- **FC+** - fair concern accurately represented
- **ACK+** - acknowledge, connect and keep moves are present
- **MATCH+** - rebuttal answers the actual concern
- **SAFE+** - action includes a usable safeguard or measure
- **REV+** - visible revision improves fairness or precision

Group exit evidence by straw-man language, incomplete response, mismatched safeguard or secure transfer.

## Pacing fallback

If reading takes longer than planned:

1. read the highlighted claim, two concerns and the model together;
2. preserve the fair-concern sort and ACK model;
3. offer one selected concern rather than a choice of three;
4. keep at least eight minutes for writing and four minutes for revision.

The non-negotiable learning is that the rebuttal must answer a genuine concern.

## Concise access facilitation

- Read the short scenario aloud while the student tracks.
- Use four cards: **our idea**, **real worry**, **helpful action**, **say the idea again**.
- Offer two concerns and two safeguards; allow pointing or eye-gaze selection.
- Scribe exactly what the student communicates, then offer one fairness choice such as **"people are negative" / "some people worry about lost things"**.
- Accept a spoken, typed or scribed 4-6 sentence response where this matches authoritative adjustments.

## Fiction and discussion boundary

The school, proposal, people and quotations are original fictional classroom material. The lesson does not require a real-school decision, nor does it invite students to disclose experience of exclusion or conflict. The goal is rhetorical fairness: students can disagree strongly while treating an opposing concern accurately.
