# Lesson 23 Alternative: The Borrow Box

### Pedagogical Contemplation

This lesson makes the cognitive work of counterargument visible: persuasive writers do not invent a weak opposing view to knock down; they anticipate the strongest fair concern their audience may genuinely hold, then answer that exact concern with a reason, example or practical safeguard. A fictional school decision gives every student enough shared context to take part without relying on *Berani*, personal disclosure or outside research.

The reading, annotation code and short interactives fit the thinking: students first notice that a proposal can have a good purpose and real implementation risks, then sort objections from dismissals, before explicitly modelling the **ACK** move: **Acknowledge, Connect, Keep the claim**. The final letter makes the thinking observable in a claim -> fair concern -> reasoned response -> returned claim sequence. Engagement comes from deciding what would make a real school trial fair and workable; it does not replace the deliberate teaching of the rhetorical move.

## Lesson purpose

Students read an original fictional school feature about a proposed lunchtime equipment library, *The Borrow Box*. They learn that a counterargument is a fair concern an audience may raise, and that a rebuttal must answer that concern rather than attack the people who hold it. Students then compose and revise a short persuasive letter proposing a four-week trial.

This is a supplemental alternative. It does not replace or modify the original Lesson 23.

## Transfer from the original lesson

The original lesson asks students to anticipate objections and draft a counterargument and rebuttal. This alternative preserves that thinking through a new shared text:

- **claim:** the school should trial The Borrow Box at lunch;
- **counterargument:** equipment could be lost, the queue could take time, or some students may be left out;
- **rebuttal:** acknowledge the concern accurately, then offer a relevant safeguard and reason;
- **return:** restate the trial claim with a measured, workable action.

## Curriculum alignment

- **Year 5 - AC9E5LA02:** acknowledge differing ideas and opinions.
- **Year 6 - AC9E6LA02:** identify bias in persuasive texts.

The lesson also rehearses persuasive creation described in **AC9E5LY06** and **AC9E6LY06**, while its primary focus remains on anticipating and responding to differing viewpoints.

## Learning intention

We are learning to anticipate a fair counterargument and answer it with a reasoned rebuttal.

## Success criteria

- I can state a clear claim and identify a fair concern an audience may hold.
- I can distinguish a counterargument from an insult, a different claim or a repeated opinion.
- I can acknowledge a concern without giving up my position.
- I can answer the exact concern with a reason, text detail or practical safeguard.
- I can return to my claim using a careful, workable action.
- I can revise a response that dismisses people or ignores the concern.

## Finished workbook response

A **7-9 sentence letter to the school principal** that:

1. states a position about a four-week Borrow Box trial;
2. names one fair counterargument;
3. acknowledges why that concern matters;
4. supplies a rebuttal that directly answers it;
5. recommends at least one safeguard or measure;
6. returns clearly to the claim; and
7. includes one visible revision after feedback.

Students may argue for a changed version of the trial, or against it, provided they represent the opposing concern fairly and provide a reasoned response.

## Core sequence - approximately 55 minutes

### 1. A good idea - until someone asks a fair question (4 minutes)

Display the Borrow Box proposal. Students hear a genuine concern and decide what makes it fair.

- **Student action:** Think-pair-share: "A fair question would be..."
- **Finished when:** One concern is stated without blaming a person.
- **Teacher look-for:** Students separate a practical concern from a put-down.

### 2. The job of a counterargument (3 minutes)

Teach the pathway: **claim -> fair concern -> answer -> returned claim**. Explain that acknowledgement is not surrender.

- **Student action:** Rehearse the pathway aloud.
- **Finished when:** Students can name each move.

### 3. Read the proposal and the objections (8 minutes)

Students read *The Borrow Box* and mark:

- **C** = claim or proposed action;
- **?** = genuine concern or question;
- **R** = response, safeguard or reason;
- **X** = language that unfairly dismisses a person or group.

- **Student action:** Independent or partnered annotation.
- **Finished when:** At least two C, two ?, two R and one X marks.
- **Teacher note:** The feature, students and quotations are fictional. Use text details as reading evidence, not as claims about the real school.

### 4. Sort fair concern from straw man (6 minutes)

Students classify three possible objections. They must explain why the strongest fair concern deserves an answer.

- **Student action:** Choose, justify, retry when needed.
- **Finished when:** Accurate classification plus one why statement.
- **Misconception exposed:** Every disagreement is an attack, or a writer should make the other side sound foolish.

### 5. Explicit model: ACK (6 minutes)

Model four sentences, one move at a time:

1. **Acknowledge** the genuine concern.
2. **Connect** it to a reason, evidence or safeguard.
3. **Keep** the claim with a practical next action.

The claim is stated first and returned to at the end; ACK names the bridge through the difficult middle of the paragraph.

- **Student action:** Label the model and underline the words that make the response fair.
- **Finished when:** Students can explain which sentence answers the concern.

### 6. Which rebuttal actually replies? (6 minutes)

Students compare a repeating opinion, a personal attack and a direct rebuttal. They select the rebuttal that answers the exact concern.

- **Student action:** Individual decision then partner explanation.
- **Finished when:** Correct selection and evidence from the wording.
- **Feedback:** First incorrect choice asks students to reread the concern; second reveals a targeted hint.

### 7. Build an ACK bridge (5 minutes)

Students choose a fair concern about lost equipment, a slow queue or inclusion. They plan the reason/safeguard that responds to it.

- **Student action:** Complete organiser sections 1-3 or rehearse orally.
- **Finished when:** Concern and response match precisely.

### 8. Write the principal letter (10 minutes)

Students write the 7-9 sentence response. The concise route may compose 4-6 clear sentences with the same conceptual components.

- **Student action:** Independent, typed, spoken or scribed response as adjusted.
- **Finished when:** Claim, fair concern, answer, return and action are all present.

### 9. Stress-test and revise (5 minutes)

Partners ask one fair question and use feedback to improve one sentence.

- **Student action:** Partner feedback then visible revision.
- **Finished when:** One revision and a spoken explanation of how it now answers the concern.

### 10. Exit: fairness makes an argument stronger when... (2 minutes)

Students complete: "A counterargument makes my writing stronger when I ___, because ___."

## Optional depth

### Depth: Change the audience, keep the fairness (7 minutes)

Students rewrite the same counterargument for a student council, a playground duty teacher and a student who prefers quiet play. They identify what must stay fair even when the audience changes.

## Support

- Preteach: **claim, counterargument, rebuttal, acknowledge, safeguard, trial, measure**.
- Provide a claim and a choice of fair concerns before independent planning.
- Rehearse orally with: "Some people may worry that ___. This matters because ___. However, ___. Therefore, ___."
- Reduce the classification task to two cards initially and read the statements aloud.
- Allow a 5-6 sentence response while retaining claim, real concern, direct answer and action.

## Concise access / ICP

- Use `Lesson_23_Borrow_Box_Concise_Access_Pack.docx`.
- Read the short scenario aloud or with a partner.
- Preserve the same destination: hear a real worry, say why it matters, choose a helpful action and state the proposal again.
- Students may point, choose, speak, copy or use a partner scribe.
- The concise route never removes the counterargument; it reduces language and writing load, not conceptual demand.

## Extension

- Ask students to distinguish a counterargument from an adjacent issue that does not affect the decision.
- Require two counterarguments and explain why one is more important for this audience.
- Revise a rebuttal by adding a measurable safeguard, such as a sign-out rule, a roster, an equipment count or an inclusion choice.
- Explain why "some people are just negative" is bias rather than a rebuttal.

## Formative assessment and next teaching response

- **Opening concern:** identifies whether students can voice a fair concern.
- **Annotation:** checks that students can locate claim, concern and response in a persuasive feature.
- **Sort:** exposes straw-man thinking.
- **Rebuttal choice:** shows whether students answer the actual concern rather than repeat their opinion.
- **Organiser:** reveals mismatches between concern and safeguard.
- **Visible revision and exit:** show whether students can make their own persuasion more credible.

Reteach with one shared claim and one audience question if students label personal attacks as counterarguments. Extend secure students by asking them to prioritise counterarguments according to audience and consequence.

## Safety and accuracy boundaries

- The Borrow Box, Riverbank Primary, all characters, quotations and school details are fictional teacher-created material.
- Do not present the feature as a report about the students' real school.
- Keep discussion school-safe. Students are not required to disclose experience of exclusion, loss, conflict or financial circumstances.
- A fair counterargument can be acknowledged without being accepted. A rebuttal must address an idea or concern, never insult a person or group.
