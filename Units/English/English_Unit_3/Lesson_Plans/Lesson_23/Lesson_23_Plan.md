# Lesson 23: Anticipating Counterarguments

## Lesson purpose

Students learn that a persuasive argument becomes stronger when it acknowledges and answers a fair opposing view. They use the connected Malia and Ari chapters to distinguish a textual obstacle from an inferred concern, then draft a counterargument and rebuttal on a school-safe issue.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 — AC9E5LA02:** acknowledge differing ideas and opinions.
- **Year 6 — AC9E6LA02:** identify bias in persuasive texts.

## Learning intention

We are learning to anticipate a fair counterargument and answer it with a reasoned rebuttal.

## Success criteria

- I can state a clear claim and a fair opposing concern.
- I can distinguish a counterargument from a rebuttal.
- I can use a connector to acknowledge and answer a concern.
- I can return to my claim with a reason or responsible action.
- I can revise a rebuttal that attacks a person or merely repeats my opinion.

## Finished workbook response

A 4–6 sentence persuasive paragraph containing a claim, a fair counterargument, an evidence-based rebuttal, and a return to the claim.

## Preparation

- Open Lesson_23_Presentation.html in a modern browser and use fullscreen.
- Print the standard or Lucas organiser.
- Students need their English workbooks.

## Core sequence — approximately 50 minutes

1. **Does your argument survive a fair question? — 4 min.** Students respond to a meaningful objection.
2. **Make your argument harder to knock over — 2 min.** Establish the claim → counterargument → rebuttal pathway.
3. **Malia prepares before acting — 5 min.** Retrieve the request for help and infer a concern responsibly.
4. **Ari names the obstacle — 5 min.** Map issue, objection and response needed from pp. 143–145.
5. **Not every “but” is a rebuttal — 5 min.** Classify the language moves and explain their jobs.
6. **Acknowledge. Answer. Return to the claim. — 6 min.** Deconstruct the model.
7. **Which rebuttal actually answers the concern? — 6 min.** Choose and justify the strongest response.
8. **Choose a school-safe issue — 4 min.** Students select or gain approval for a topic.
9. **Build the bridge before you write — 4 min.** Plan claim, fair concern, answer and return.
10. **Draft your counterargument and rebuttal — 10 min.** Write the 4–6 sentence response.
11. **Give the argument a stress test — 4 min.** Rehearse, receive feedback and revise.
12. **One fair concern. One strong answer. — 2 min.** Exit evidence.

## Reading evidence and accuracy

In Malia’s pp. 139–141 chapter, she responds to Ari’s request, compares rescue-organisation notes and offers support. In Ari’s pp. 143–145 chapter, he expects Uncle to be angry about the rescue. These are text details. Reasons for Uncle’s opposition and the model’s economic-impact scenario are explicitly taught as possible inferences/practice examples, not claims made by the novel.

## Differentiation

**Support:** provide the claim and a choice of fair concerns; rehearse orally with “Some people may worry… However…”; use the organiser before drafting.

**Lucas (ICP):** use the large-print, low-writing organiser. Students may point, speak, copy or use a partner scribe to identify one care concern and one helpful action. This preserves the core learning: another view can be heard and answered respectfully.

**Extend:** revise a vague rebuttal by adding a specific, responsible action; explain why attacking a person is not a rebuttal.

## Formative assessment

The classification task reveals whether students confuse a counterargument with a rebuttal. The choice task and exit pair show whether students can answer a fair concern rather than dismiss it. Use this evidence to reteach fair representation before later persuasive composition.

## Safety and teacher guidance

Keep debate topics school-safe and do not ask students to disclose personal financial, family or animal-harm experiences. Accept different positions where the counterargument is fair and the rebuttal genuinely responds to it.
