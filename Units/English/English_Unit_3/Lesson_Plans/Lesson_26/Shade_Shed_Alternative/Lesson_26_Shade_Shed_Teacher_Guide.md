# Teacher Guide: The Shade Shed

## Teaching stance

Treat the model as a crafted example, not a formula to copy. Students should leave knowing that a persuasive text makes decisions for an audience: it does not merely collect techniques.

## Model answers

- **Hook:** “At lunch, the strip beside the library is often empty ...” creates a scene and reason to care.
- **Thesis:** “Harbourview should trial a Shade Shed ...” states the action, purpose and scope.
- **Topic sentence:** “First, the shed would give students a calm, usable choice ...” states the paragraph’s reason.
- **Other view:** “Some people may worry ...” acknowledges a practical concern.
- **Conclusion:** returns to a measured trial and review, rather than promising every outcome.

## Conferencing prompts

- “Who is your audience, and what decision can they influence?”
- “Could a reasonable person disagree with this claim?”
- “Which word will you need evidence to support?”
- “What question will help you find that evidence?”

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Response |
|---|---|
| “My topic is recycling.” | Ask for an action, audience and reason: “What should happen, and who needs to act?” |
| “A hook is my opinion.” | Compare the scene-setting opening with the thesis that makes the position explicit. |
| “I can use the Shade Shed facts.” | Re-state the fiction boundary; model a research question rather than a borrowed claim. |
| “Everyone will agree.” | Replace absolute language with a claim that can be researched and tested. |

## Safe stopping point

Collect the Assessment Direction Organiser when students have an audience, a provisional claim and at least one genuine research question. Do not approve a topic involving personal disclosures, family finances or unsafe local action without an appropriate alternative and teacher guidance.
