The Shade Shed

A model persuasive proposal: clear enough for an audience to decide.

An unused corner can still be a choice

At lunch, the strip beside Harbourview's library is often empty. It is too bright for reading and too narrow for a game, so students pass it on their way to the oval. Maia sees something different: a small covered space with benches, a noticeboard and a crate of drawing materials that students can use during lunch.

Harbourview should trial a Shade Shed beside the library for one term, then review whether it gives students a calm and useful lunchtime choice.

First, the shed would give students a place to read, draw, talk quietly or wait for a friend without having to join a noisy game. A school playground should offer more than one way to belong. The proposal does not ask students to stop playing sport; it adds one small option to an unused space.

A careful proposal anticipates questions

Some people may worry that a shed could cost too much, become messy or need extra supervision. Those are reasonable concerns. A space that staff cannot manage fairly would not solve the problem it is meant to address.

However, a short trial could begin with existing benches, a simple use-and-return routine and a weekly check-in with the duty teacher. The school could record which materials are used, what needs repair and whether the space is manageable before deciding whether to continue.

Mark the model

Hhook: invites a reader into the situation
Tthesis: states a position and action
Rreason: explains why the action matters
Oother view: fair concern
Cconclusion: measured next step
Find the thesis. Underline one reason. Put a box around the fair concern.

What the model does

A persuasive writer makes a claim, then gives the audience reasons to consider it.

Different sentences, different jobs

The proposal returns to its main point at the end: Harbourview should trial the Shade Shed, collect information during the term and decide what to do next. This conclusion is not a promise that every student will enjoy the shed. It is a request for a limited action that an audience can evaluate.

The model also avoids pretending that one feeling is evidence. Maia's observation creates a reason to investigate a need; it does not prove that the school should spend money. A writer would still need to find reliable information, listen to different views and explain where the information came from.

Three beginnings

Only a topic
“Shade and lunch.”
Vague wish
“The school needs something better outside.”
Researchable claim
“The principal should approve a one-term Shade Shed trial beside the library.”
Overclaim
“The Shade Shed will fix every playground problem.”

Before you choose your own idea

Name an audience with a real decision to make. State what should happen. Then list the questions you will need to research. Your claim can change after you learn more.
