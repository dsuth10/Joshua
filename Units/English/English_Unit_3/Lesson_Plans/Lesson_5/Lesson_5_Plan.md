# Lesson 5: The Persuasion Mission

## Lesson purpose

Students plan and rehearse the opening of a persuasive presentation connected to Malia’s palm oil campaign in *Berani*. The lesson deliberately connects audience engagement (the hook) with audience trust (credible evidence and explanation). Students finish with a usable 30-second opening rather than a collection of disconnected exercises.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 — AC9E5LA02:** move beyond bare assertions by using information from authoritative sources.
- **Year 6 — AC9E6LA02:** examine how objective and subjective language influence an audience.

## Learning intention

We are learning to create a persuasive opening that hooks attention, earns trust and leads an audience towards action.

## Success criteria

- I can explain the job of each part of PEEL.
- I can judge whether a source is relevant and credible.
- I can select and justify a hook for a particular audience and purpose.
- I can draft and rehearse a 30-second opening using Hook + PEEL.

## Preparation

- Open *Berani* to pages 21–25.
- Open `Lesson_5_Presentation.html` in a modern browser and use fullscreen mode.
- Check that the embedded Greenpeace video is available. If not, use the title image and the novel pages for the Notice–Feel–Prove discussion.
- Mini-whiteboards or the existing PEEL handout may be used for students who prefer handwriting.

## Sequence (approximately 55–65 minutes)

### 1. Cold open: the 8-second challenge — 5 minutes

Display two contrasting openings. Students choose which makes them “lean in” and defend the choice with a language reason. Establish that engaging language must be deliberate, specific and appropriate for the audience.

### 2. Mission briefing — 3 minutes

Introduce the three jobs of a persuasive opening:

1. Hook attention.
2. Earn trust.
3. Build action.

Students restate the lesson goal in their own words.

### 3. Real-world context: Notice–Feel–Prove — 7 minutes

Watch the short palm oil video. Students identify:

- one memorable image or fact;
- the emotion the creators intend to produce;
- something Malia could use as evidence rather than opinion.

Explicitly distinguish emotional impact from factual proof.

### 4. Text detectives: Malia’s evidence file — 7 minutes

In pairs, scan pages 21–25. Reveal a clue card only after students locate or infer the matching detail. Include the “evidence or emotion?” decoy to surface the misconception that a strong feeling is proof.

### 5. Argument Lab: PEEL case build — 10 minutes

Students place six statements into Point, Evidence, Explanation and Link. After checking, discuss the most common confusion: evidence tells us what supports the claim; explanation tells us how or why that evidence matters.

### 6. Source Check — 6 minutes

Compare an anonymous comment, advocacy video, current research report and petition total. Students select the strongest source for the specific habitat claim and justify the decision using author, date, method, relevance and purpose.

### 7. Hook Showdown — 7 minutes

Audience: classmates who can listen, respond and act. Purpose: make them care enough to listen to the claim.

Evaluate four openings using the 3-second test:

- specific;
- audience-aware;
- purposeful.

Students read the hooks without technique labels, click their strongest choice, then complete: “This hook works because it is ___ (specific / audience-aware / purposeful).” Click other hooks to reveal technique labels for comparison. Emphasise that more than one hook can be effective if the justification is sound.

Challenge: rewrite hook A so it passes the 3-second test — keep the topic.

### 8. Mentor opening — 5 minutes

Read the model opening once for fluency. Then reveal the function of each sentence: Hook, Point, Evidence, Explanation and Link. Notice that the hook and PEEL paragraph work as one spoken argument.

### 9. Build Studio — 10–12 minutes

Students draft a 55–80 word opening. Optional sentence stems are available in the presentation, but students should adapt them. Require:

- a purposeful hook;
- a clear claim;
- a named or traceable source;
- an explanation of why the evidence matters;
- a link to the message or action.

### 10. Rehearsal room — 6 minutes

Partners deliver their openings for 30 seconds. Listeners respond with:

- “I leaned in when…”
- “I believed you when…”
- “One phrase to strengthen is…”

Students revise one phrase immediately.

### 11. Exit ticket — 3 minutes

Students submit two lines:

1. Their strongest hook.
2. The evidence they will use and why it is trustworthy.

Use the confidence check to group students for the next lesson.

## Differentiation

### Support

- Provide the sequence strip: **Hook → Point → Evidence → This matters because… → Therefore…**
- Allow students to select and adapt a hook stem from the Build Studio.
- Reduce the PEEL sort to four cards before adding the two additional cards.
- Rehearse orally with a partner before writing.

### Lucas (ICP)

- Use three visual sequence cards: forest → clearing/logging → orangutan needs help.
- Offer two spoken choices for the hook: “Help orangutans” or “Orangutans need a safe home.”
- Match one evidence picture to the sentence: “The forest is being cut down.”
- Rehearse the two-sentence presentation with an adult or peer: “Orangutans need a safe home. Help orangutans.”

### Extend

- Compare objective evidence with subjective/emotive framing in the mentor opening.
- Revise the opening for a different audience, such as a supermarket manager or younger students.
- Identify the advocacy purpose of the Greenpeace video and propose one independent source that could corroborate its claim.

## Formative assessment

- Cold-open justification reveals students’ current hook criteria.
- The PEEL sort exposes Evidence/Explanation confusion.
- Source Check reveals whether students equate popularity or emotion with authority.
- Partner feedback surfaces the difference between attention and credibility.
- The exit ticket determines readiness for independent presentation planning.

## Pedagogical rationale

The lesson follows a complete persuasive-writing cycle: experience an audience response, retrieve evidence, analyse structure, evaluate sources and hooks, study a model, compose, rehearse, receive feedback and revise. Interaction is used to make students explain decisions, not simply move objects. The final product is directly reusable in the unit’s presentation task.
