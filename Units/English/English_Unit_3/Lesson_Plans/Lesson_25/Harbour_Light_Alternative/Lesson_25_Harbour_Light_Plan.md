### Pedagogical Contemplation

The cognitive goal is to move students from recounting a dramatic moment to explaining how a character's deliberate choice changes what is possible, then to make an evidence-grounded persuasive claim about courage. The original fictional reading gives students a complete, low-stakes narrative arc; the turning-point choice, event-to-claim model and revision check each expose a different part of the reasoning needed for the final paragraph. Thinking becomes visible in the turning-point justification, organiser, oral rehearsal, draft and exit clauses. The harbour setting provides narrative tension, but the lesson is not an emergency-safety lesson: engagement serves close reading and persuasive transfer.

# Lesson 25 Alternative: Harbour Light - Climax to Claim

## Lesson purpose

Students read an original fictional community feature, identify its turning point and trace its consequence. They develop a precise, evidence-grounded claim about courage and explain why the claim matters to a reader. This preserves Lesson 25's bridge from climax and resolution to assessment-ready persuasive ideas without using *Berani*.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 - AC9E5LY06:** Plan, create and edit written persuasive texts.
- **Year 6 - AC9E6LY06:** Plan, create and edit persuasive texts using paragraphs.

## Learning intention

We are learning to explain how a turning point develops an idea about courage that is worth sharing with a reader.

## Success criteria

- I can identify a precise turning point and its consequence.
- I can explain how Nia's choice involves care and risk without being careless.
- I can use a story detail as evidence for a claim about courage.
- I can explain why that claim matters to a reader.

## Finished student product

A 6-8 sentence climax-to-claim paragraph about *When the Lantern Went Dark*. It names a precise event, explains a decision and consequence, uses a detail as evidence, makes a defensible claim about courage and ends with why a reader should care. The concise route may be spoken, scribed or composed as three connected clauses while preserving the same event-to-claim reasoning.

## Preparation

- Open `Lesson_25_Harbour_Light_Presentation.html` and choose fullscreen.
- Print the Reading Pack and the appropriate organiser; students need English workbooks.
- The original text is fictional. Do not treat it as emergency advice or as factual evidence for a real proposal.

## Core sequence (50 minutes)

1. **The lantern went dark - 4 min.** Predict what might change, then distinguish excitement from a turning point.
2. **Today’s path - 3 min.** Establish event -> decision -> consequence -> persuasive claim.
3. **Encounter the text - 8 min.** Read *When the Lantern Went Dark* and mark danger, decision and consequence.
4. **Turning-point decision - 5 min.** Justify the event that redirects the response.
5. **Explicit model - 6 min.** Trace Nia's call to a consequence, courage claim and reader purpose.
6. **Care, not carelessness - 4 min.** Select and justify the explanation that fits the evidence.
7. **Plan - 5 min.** Build four linked notes in the organiser.
8. **Write - 10 min.** Construct the climax-to-claim paragraph.
9. **Revise - 3 min.** Make one visible revision using the check.
10. **Exit evidence - 2 min.** Submit event, claim and reader-purpose clauses.

## Interaction matrix

| Interaction | Cognitive demand | Evidence/rule | Misconception exposed | Teacher signal | Tier 2 hint |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turning-point choice | Evaluate and justify | It must redirect the response and make a new outcome possible. | The most dramatic event is always the climax. | Student can name an outcome that follows. | Find the event that changes what can happen next. |
| Care, not carelessness | Compare explanations | Use Nia's feeling, safe action and consequence. | Bravery means no fear or acting alone. | Student cites at least one detail. | Use what Nia felt, did and what happened next. |
| Draft/revise | Construct and improve | Event + consequence + evidence + claim + purpose. | A claim is plot summary. | Student underlines a generalisable claim. | What does this event suggest to a reader? |

## Explicit model and answer guidance

Model: Nia calls for trained help -> the crew can safely free the dinghy -> courage can mean taking the responsible next step even when afraid -> readers may rethink bravery as safe care, not a dramatic risk.

The strongest turning-point answer is Nia's emergency call because it redirects the response. Accept another answer only if a student explains its causal effect. Claims are interpretations, not a single correct moral. Require a precise event and a linked consequence.

## Support, concise access and extension

The green presentation route pairs all projected learning with concise wording; use the concise organiser for pointing, speaking, copying, drawing arrows or partner scribing. Keep the shared destination: a character action linked to a consequence and a courage claim. Support students to orally rehearse: “When Nia ___, this changed ___. This suggests courage can ___.”

Extension: require cautious language (“can” or “may”) and ask students to explain why the proposed sign is a persuasive proposal rather than proof that every community member will act safely.

## Formative assessment and next step

The two choices identify whether students can distinguish climax from excitement and courage from recklessness. The paragraph and exit clauses reveal who can move from story event to persuasive idea, the required bridge into Lesson 26's assessment model deconstruction.

## Discussion and factual boundary

Do not invite students to share personal emergency experiences. The harbour scenario, names and proposed sign are fictional. Accept varied claims when a student links a precise textual detail to a defensible interpretation.
