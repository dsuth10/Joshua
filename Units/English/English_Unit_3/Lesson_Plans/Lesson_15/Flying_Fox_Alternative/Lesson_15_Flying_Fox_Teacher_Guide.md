# Teacher Guide: Living With Flying-Foxes

## At a glance

This supplemental Lesson 15 replaces literary analysis with an authentic council-communication problem. Students use a shared evidence briefing to audit two deliberately positioned messages, revise a difficult classification and write advice that is persuasive without distortion.

**Core time:** approximately 60 minutes  
**Final product:** 7-9 sentence Persuasive Language Audit and Advice Brief  
**Audience:** fictional Rivergum Council  
**Key transfer:** checkable information versus judgement, mixed statements, reader positioning and evidence-responsive revision

## Essential teacher framing

Say:

> We are not deciding whether people or wildlife matter more. We are examining how language can acknowledge real impacts, communicate safe information and still position a community towards a course of action.

Keep these distinctions visible:

- **Objective:** mainly checkable information; not automatically proven.
- **Subjective:** judgement, preference, emotion or loaded evaluation.
- **Mixed:** checkable content plus evaluation or framing.

## Evidence briefing answer guidance

### Community impacts (C)

Accept:

- camps can produce noise, odour and droppings;
- residents may experience interrupted sleep, mess or property impacts;
- urban habitat fragmentation can bring camps closer to homes and parks.

### Ecological importance (E)

Accept:

- flying-foxes feed on fruit, nectar and blossoms;
- they move pollen and seeds between feeding areas;
- some species travel long distances at night;
- two Australian flying-fox species are nationally listed as threatened;
- habitat loss, extreme heat and conflict with people can affect populations.

### Safe or lawful responses (S)

Accept:

- never touch or attempt to rescue a bat;
- contact a trained, vaccinated wildlife rescuer;
- seek urgent medical advice after a bite or scratch;
- bring washing in at night, cover vehicles or outdoor items and plan ahead;
- council or landholder management must follow current law and codes;
- management must not harm the animals.

### Misconception correction

Strong boxed choices include:

- living, walking or playing near a camp does not expose a person to Australian Bat Lyssavirus;
- ABLV requires direct exposure such as an untreated bite or scratch from an infected bat;
- there is no evidence that Hendra virus passes directly from flying-foxes to people;
- objective wording still requires checking.

## Classification answer guidance

| Statement | Suggested classification | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Flying-foxes leave their daytime camps around dusk to search for food. | Objective | Behaviour that could be observed and checked. |
| Their unbearable racket ruins every nearby family’s day. | Subjective | “Unbearable”, “racket”, “ruins” and “every” load the statement with judgement and exaggeration. |
| The camp beside Rivergum Park has become a serious neighbourhood problem. | Mixed | The camp/location can be checked; “serious problem” evaluates its significance. |
| Flying-foxes can spread pollen and seeds as they travel between feeding areas. | Objective | Ecological function that can be checked against research. |
| These wonderful night gardeners deserve a warm welcome from everyone. | Subjective | Positive metaphor, moral judgement and universal demand. |
| Living near a camp can involve noise, odour and mess, but disease risk is extremely low when people do not handle bats. | Mixed preferred | Contains checkable impacts and official risk advice, while “extremely low” evaluates scale and the sentence compresses several claims. Accept objective if a student precisely explains how every part could be verified. |

## Message audit guidance

### Message A: “Clear the Camp Now”

Likely checkable content:

- the fictional camp is beside Rivergum Park;
- residents have reported noise, odour or droppings;
- flying-foxes leave at dusk.

Positioning choices:

- “deafening invasion” creates alarm and loss of control;
- “ruining our neighbourhood” generalises one viewpoint;
- “clear the camp now” makes immediate removal sound simple and unquestionably desirable;
- “dangerous animals” implies a broad risk unsupported by the shared evidence.

### Message B: “Welcome Our Night Gardeners”

Likely checkable content:

- flying-foxes move pollen and seeds;
- camps may use urban trees;
- protected species and roost-management rules are relevant.

Positioning choices:

- “wonderful night gardeners” creates affection and value;
- “tiny inconvenience” minimises genuine impacts;
- “everyone should welcome them” dismisses variation in experience;
- “nothing to worry about” overstates reassurance and removes the important no-touch safety message.

## Model analysis

The model succeeds because it:

1. identifies the purpose and frame of both messages;
2. quotes short, exact wording;
3. separates checkable content from evaluation;
4. explains positioning with precise verbs;
5. corrects misleading implications using the evidence briefing;
6. gives safe and lawful actions;
7. recommends communication principles;
8. remains persuasive about coexistence without dismissing residents.

## Feedback prompts

Use:

> Which exact word creates the effect you named?
>
> What part of this sentence could be checked?
>
> Does your category describe the whole sentence or only one part?
>
> What source would the council need before publishing this?
>
> Have you acknowledged the strongest legitimate concern?
>
> Is your action safe, lawful and possible for this audience?

## Common misconceptions and responses

### “Objective means true”

Respond:

> Objective means the claim can be checked. What evidence would prove or disprove it?

### “Subjective means a lie”

Respond:

> A judgement can be sincere and reasonable. Which words show evaluation rather than checkable information?

### “The whole sentence must be one type”

Respond:

> Underline the claim. Circle the framing. If both are present, what label captures the combination?

### “Fear makes persuasion stronger”

Respond:

> Fear can create urgency, but what happens to trust when the wording exaggerates the evidence?

### “Fairness means saying nothing strongly”

Respond:

> A fair message can still recommend action. It earns trust by accurately representing concerns and evidence.

## ICP pathway

- Read the one-page evidence briefing aloud.
- Use the labels **fact**, **feeling/judgement** and **both**.
- Offer two items at a time.
- Permit pointing, speaking, copying or scribing.
- Keep the destination: identify one loaded phrase, replace it with calmer wording and recommend one safe council action.
- Do not reduce the task to naming animals or copying facts.

## Optional depth

Use **Preserve the fact, change the frame** for students ready to manipulate viewpoint. Use **Audit the evidence behind an objective claim** for students ready to examine source credibility and proof.

## Safety script

If students ask what to do with an injured flying-fox:

> Do not touch it. Keep people and pets away and tell an adult. An adult should contact a trained, vaccinated wildlife rescuer. If anyone is bitten or scratched, seek medical advice immediately and follow school procedures.

Do not improvise capture, disposal or first-aid instructions beyond current school and Queensland Health guidance.
