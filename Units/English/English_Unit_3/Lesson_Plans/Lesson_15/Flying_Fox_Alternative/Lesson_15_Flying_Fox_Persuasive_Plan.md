# Supplemental Lesson 15: Living With Flying-Foxes

## Lesson purpose

Students read a concise evidence briefing about flying-fox camps, audit competing community messages for objective, subjective and mixed language, then advise a fictional local council how to communicate fairly and persuasively. This lesson preserves Lesson 15's cognitive focus while moving completely away from the novel.

## Central teaching diagnosis

Environmental persuasion can slip into a false choice: either dismiss residents' concerns or portray native wildlife as a frightening threat. Students need to recognise that checkable information and judgement can appear in the same sentence, and that a persuasive message becomes more trustworthy when it labels uncertainty, avoids loaded wording and still makes a clear recommendation.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 - AC9E5LA06: understand how vocabulary choices add precision and shape viewpoint.**
- **Year 6 - AC9E6LA06: understand how language choices expand ideas and position a reader.**

## Learning intention

We are learning to distinguish checkable information from subjective positioning and use that knowledge to advise a council on fair, persuasive communication.

## Success criteria

- I can identify information that could be checked independently.
- I can identify judgement, emotion and loaded wording.
- I can explain why a statement may be mixed.
- I can explain how wording positions a community audience.
- I can revise one unsupported classification after checking the exact wording.
- I can recommend language that recognises legitimate concerns without exaggerating risk or dismissing wildlife.

## Finished workbook response

A 7-9 sentence **Persuasive Language Audit and Advice Brief** to the fictional Rivergum Council.

The brief must:

- identify the purpose and likely effect of two community messages;
- accurately classify at least four examples as objective, subjective or mixed;
- quote or precisely copy at least two short language choices;
- explain how those choices position readers;
- correct or qualify one misleading or loaded claim;
- recommend two communication principles and one practical council action;
- include one visible revision after peer or self-checking.

## The three-way language test

### Objective

The statement mainly gives information that could be checked independently. It may still turn out to be inaccurate; **objective does not automatically mean proven or true**.

### Subjective

The statement mainly expresses judgement, preference, emotion or loaded evaluation.

### Mixed

The statement combines a checkable claim with language that evaluates, exaggerates, minimises or frames that information.

## Core sequence

### 1. Same camp, different message - 4 minutes

Display two fictional notices:

> A flying-fox camp has formed beside Rivergum Park.
>
> A deafening invasion has taken over our peaceful park.

Students decide what each message makes a reader notice and feel.

- **Student action:** Think, pair, justify.
- **Finished when:** One wording-effect comparison.
- **Teacher note:** Do not label the categories yet. The first sentence may be checkable; the second adds judgement and metaphor.
- **Misconception revealed:** Different emotional reactions must come from different events.

### 2. Your mission: advise the council - 2 minutes

Introduce the fictional scenario:

> Rivergum Council must publish a community update. Residents deserve accurate safety advice, acknowledgement of noise and mess, and information about protected wildlife. Your job is to audit two draft messages before advising the council.

- **Student action:** Complete orally: “The council’s message must be persuasive, but it must not ___.”
- **Finished when:** One boundary named.
- **Teacher note:** Likely boundaries include exaggerate, dismiss, frighten or promise what cannot be delivered.

### 3. Read for common ground - 8 minutes

Students read the evidence briefing using:

- **C** = community impact or concern;
- **E** = ecological importance;
- **S** = safe or lawful response.

They box one sentence that corrects a common misconception.

- **Student action:** Independent or paired reading.
- **Finished when:** At least two C, two E and three S annotations, plus one boxed correction.
- **Teacher note:** Insist that students use the curated reading rather than invented internet-style claims.

### 4. Build the shared evidence bank - 4 minutes

Students record:

1. one checkable fact about flying-fox behaviour or ecology;
2. one checkable fact about community impacts;
3. one checkable fact about health or safety;
4. one checkable fact about lawful management.

For each, they note what would need to be checked: source, date, place, species, measurement or exact wording.

- **Student action:** Independent; organiser.
- **Finished when:** Four facts and four verification notes.
- **Teacher note:** A statement can sound calm and still require verification.

### 5. Read two competing community messages - 5 minutes

Students read the two fictional messages in the reading pack.

**Message A: “Clear the Camp Now”** foregrounds noise, odour, mess and urgency.  
**Message B: “Welcome Our Night Gardeners”** foregrounds pollination, conservation and coexistence.

Students underline one checkable claim and circle one judgement or loaded phrase in each.

- **Student action:** Pairs; reading pack and organiser.
- **Finished when:** Four language annotations.
- **Teacher note:** Both messages contain legitimate information and positioning. Neither is the official council answer.

### 6. Classify the language - then defend the boundary - 7 minutes

Students classify six statements:

1. “Flying-foxes leave their daytime camps around dusk to search for food.”
2. “Their unbearable racket ruins every nearby family’s day.”
3. “The camp beside Rivergum Park has become a serious neighbourhood problem.”
4. “Flying-foxes can spread pollen and seeds as they travel between feeding areas.”
5. “These wonderful night gardeners deserve a warm welcome from everyone.”
6. “Living near a camp can involve noise, odour and mess, but disease risk is extremely low when people do not handle bats.”

Suggested classifications:

1. objective;
2. subjective;
3. mixed;
4. objective;
5. subjective;
6. mixed.

- **Student action:** Independent first, then pair comparison; organiser.
- **Finished when:** Six labels, exact wording marked and one difficult boundary defended.
- **Teacher note:** Sentence 6 combines checkable claims and a risk evaluation grounded in official advice. “Extremely low” is a judgement about scale, but it can also be evidence-based. Accept objective or mixed if the reasoning identifies what must be verified. The strongest answer is mixed because it compresses several facts and an evaluative risk description.

### 7. What job does the wording perform? - 4 minutes

Students complete:

> The phrase “___” positions residents to feel/think ___ because ___.

They analyse one choice from each message.

- **Student action:** Independent, then compare.
- **Finished when:** Two word-effect explanations.
- **Teacher note:** Move beyond “it is emotive”. Useful effect verbs include alarms, reassures, minimises, dignifies, blames, invites, pressures and oversimplifies.

### 8. Revise an unsupported classification - 3 minutes

Students return to the classification they were least certain about. They:

1. underline the checkable part;
2. circle the judgement or framing part;
3. retain or revise the label;
4. write “I changed/kept my label because ___.”

- **Student action:** Independent.
- **Finished when:** One visible reasoning revision.
- **Teacher note:** Revision is required even when the category stays the same; the reasoning must become more precise.

### 9. Model a fair but persuasive language audit - 5 minutes

Read the complete model before annotating:

> **Advice to Rivergum Council**
>
> Both draft messages use real community concerns, but each pushes readers towards a different judgement. Message A calls the camp a “deafening invasion”, which exaggerates a checkable issue - noise - and positions residents to expect danger and loss of control. Message B accurately explains that flying-foxes move pollen and seeds, but “welcome from everyone” dismisses people who experience odour, mess or interrupted sleep. The council should replace loaded labels with specific, checkable descriptions. It should state that people must not touch bats and should contact a trained wildlife rescuer about an injured animal. It should also explain that flying-foxes and their roosts are protected and that management must be lawful and not harm the animals. A fair update can recommend practical steps, such as bringing washing in at night, while promising to monitor local impacts. This balanced wording is more persuasive because it informs residents, recognises their concerns and gives them safe actions.

Students identify:

- classification;
- quoted wording;
- effect on the audience;
- correction or qualification;
- communication principles;
- practical action.

- **Student action:** Whole-class annotated model; star one move to imitate.
- **Finished when:** Six moves identified and one starred.
- **Teacher note:** “Balanced” does not mean neutral mush. The model still recommends coexistence and specific council communication choices.

### 10. Plan the council advice brief - 4 minutes

Students select:

- one example from Message A;
- one example from Message B;
- one fact that corrects or qualifies a claim;
- two communication principles;
- one practical, safe and lawful action.

- **Student action:** Independent; organiser.
- **Finished when:** Complete advice chain.
- **Teacher note:** Conference first with students who classify accurately but have not explained reader positioning.

### 11. Write the Persuasive Language Audit and Advice Brief - 10 minutes

Students write 7-9 sentences to Rivergum Council.

- **Student action:** Independent and silent; English workbook.
- **Finished when:** Complete evidence-based advice brief.
- **Teacher note:** Students may use the model’s architecture but must make their own language choices and explanations.

### 12. Run the fair-message test - 3 minutes

Reviewers respond:

> Your clearest language-effect link is ___ because ___.
>
> One claim that needs checking, qualifying or softening is ___ because ___.

Writers immediately revise one sentence.

- **Student action:** Partner feedback, then independent revision.
- **Finished when:** Two feedback statements and one visible revision.
- **Teacher note:** Reviewers diagnose; writers retain control of their recommendation.

### 13. Exit: persuasive without distortion - 1 minute

Students complete:

> A council can persuade responsibly by ___ instead of ___ because ___.

- **Student action:** Independent; organiser or workbook.
- **Finished when:** One complete principle-and-effect sentence.
- **Teacher note:** Sort exit responses by classification confusion, effect-language weakness or factual inaccuracy.

## Optional depth activities

### Depth A: Preserve the fact, change the frame - 7 minutes

Students rewrite the same checkable event for:

- a resident advocacy group;
- a wildlife conservation group;
- the council’s public information page.

They explain what changed and which version carries the least visible judgement.

- **Student action:** Pairs; workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** Three versions and one comparison.
- **Teacher note:** Objective wording can reduce viewpoint, but choices about inclusion, order and emphasis still shape meaning.

### Depth B: Audit the evidence behind an “objective” claim - 8 minutes

Students choose one calm-sounding statement and list what a council would need before publishing it:

- named source;
- location and date;
- definition or measurement;
- corroboration;
- limitation or uncertainty.

- **Student action:** Small groups.
- **Finished when:** A five-part verification note.
- **Teacher note:** This extends the original lesson’s key distinction: objective does not mean automatically proven.

## Support

- Preteach: **camp/roost, nocturnal, pollination, seed dispersal, coexist, protected, mitigation, loaded language**.
- Allow paired reading and alternate paragraphs.
- Colour-code checkable information and judgement.
- Provide only four classification items initially, then add the mixed examples.
- Offer the optional writing frame:

> Message A uses “___”, which is ___ because ___.
>
> This positions residents to ___.
>
> Message B uses “___”, which ___.
>
> The council should say ___ because the evidence shows ___.
>
> It should also ___.
>
> This would persuade responsibly because ___.

- Permit a 5-6 sentence brief while preserving both messages, one correction and one recommendation.

## ICP

- Use `Lesson_15_Flying_Fox_Support_Pack.docx`.
- Read the simplified evidence aloud or with a partner.
- Preserve the same conceptual destination with three accessible choices: **fact**, **feeling/judgement**, **both**.
- Use large print and permit pointing, choosing, speaking, copying or scribing.
- Build one message analysis and one council recommendation before attempting the full response.
- Suggested final response:

> Rivergum Council should use calm and clear words. Flying-fox camps can be noisy and messy, but people should not call them an “invasion”. Flying-foxes help forests by moving pollen and seeds. People must not touch bats. The council should listen to residents, give safe advice and protect the animals.

## Extension

- Require students to explain how selection and order can position a reader even without obviously loaded words.
- Ask students to distinguish a risk statement from fear-based framing.
- Require a qualification using **although**, **while** or **however**.
- Adapt the advice for a wildlife carer, affected resident or council communications officer without changing the evidence base.

## Formative assessment and next teaching response

- **Opening contrast:** reveals sensitivity to framing.
- **Reading codes:** show whether students can identify shared evidence.
- **Evidence bank:** reveals whether calm-sounding claims are being treated as automatically proven.
- **Classification:** exposes confusion at the objective-subjective boundary.
- **Language-effect links:** show whether students can explain positioning.
- **Visible revision:** demonstrates evidence-responsive reasoning.
- **Advice brief:** transfers analysis into authentic persuasion.
- **Exit sentence:** samples responsible persuasive control.

Respond next by:

- reteaching the checkability test if students label accurate-sounding claims as objective without naming what could verify them;
- reteaching mixed statements if students classify only whole sentences and ignore embedded wording;
- modelling precise effect verbs if explanations stop at “makes the reader interested”;
- correcting health misinformation immediately and returning students to the official reading;
- extending secure students to source credibility and omission.

## Accuracy, safety and respect boundaries

- Do not ask students to approach, handle, feed, rescue or disturb flying-foxes.
- If a bat is injured or on the ground, people should keep away and contact a trained, vaccinated wildlife rescuer.
- If a person is bitten or scratched, they should seek urgent medical advice. Teachers should follow school procedures and current Queensland Health guidance.
- Do not imply that simply living, walking or playing near a camp transmits Australian Bat Lyssavirus.
- Do not imply that Hendra virus passes directly from flying-foxes to people.
- Recognise that noise, odour, droppings, sleep disruption and property impacts can be genuine concerns.
- Do not describe flying-foxes as dirty, invading, evil or diseased.
- Do not present dispersal as an easy, harmless or universally effective solution.
- Do not encourage unauthorised interference with protected animals or roosts.
- Students are not required to disclose health history, family property concerns, fears or political views.
