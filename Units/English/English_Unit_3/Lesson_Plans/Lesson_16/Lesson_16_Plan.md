# Lesson 16: The Character Verdict

## Lesson purpose

Students formulate a defensible literary opinion about a major character's choice, using precise evidence, explanation and a fair counterview.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 - AC9E5LE03: recognise how point of view influences reader response to characters and events.**
- **Year 6 - AC9E6LE01: identify and explain responses to characters drawn from social and ethical contexts.**

## Learning intention

We are learning to form a literary opinion that is arguable, evidence-based and fair to another interpretation.

## Success criteria

- I can turn a reaction into a precise judgement.
- I can select evidence that directly supports my judgement.
- I can explain how the evidence proves my point.
- I can acknowledge a reasonable counterview.
- I can write and revise a coherent 100-word literary opinion.

## Finished workbook response

A 100-word Character Verdict paragraph judging one significant choice made by Malia, Ari, Uncle Kus or Mrs Harwono in pages 1-91.

## Agreed delivery model

- Teacher controls the projected HTML presentation.
- Students complete the organiser and all substantial composition in physical English workbooks.
- The deck contains no student composition fields.
- The chapter is assumed to have been read. Use the fallback summary only when required.
- Core route: approximately 50 minutes.
- Both optional depth slides are included and may extend the lesson towards 70 minutes.
- Revision is primarily independent through the projected self-check.

## Core sequence

### 1. Reaction—or literary judgement? - 4 minutes

Rank the three statements from weakest to strongest and name what improves.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: verdict test.
- **Finished when:** A ranking and two improvement features.
- **Teacher note:** Avoid presenting the final wording as the only legitimate judgement. Its strength comes from precision and evidence.

### 2. Make a case a thoughtful reader could challenge - 2 minutes

Select one character and one choice worth judging.

- **Student action:** Independent; Organiser: case file heading.
- **Finished when:** Character + choice.
- **Teacher note:** Offer Malia's apology dilemma as the common pathway, while allowing other well-evidenced choices from pages 1-91.

### 3. What did the character choose—and under what pressure? - 5 minutes

Complete the three-part case file using accurate chapter details.

- **Student action:** Pairs with same character; Organiser: case file.
- **Finished when:** Choice, pressure and consequence.
- **Teacher note:** Students choosing Malia should distinguish delaying a signature at page 91 from later decisions not yet read.

### 4. Choose a position with room for nuance - 5 minutes

Place your choice on the continuum and define the criterion you used.

- **Student action:** Four corners / line debate; Organiser: provisional verdict.
- **Finished when:** Position + criterion + spoken reason.
- **Teacher note:** Accept defensible positions. Ask what new evidence would cause a student to move.

### 5. Which detail actually proves the verdict? - 5 minutes

Select your strongest evidence and explain why it beats a second option.

- **Student action:** Independent, then partner challenge; Organiser: evidence trial.
- **Finished when:** One selected detail and comparison.
- **Teacher note:** The displayed statements are teacher paraphrases. Students may use careful paraphrase rather than extended quotation.

### 6. A fair objection strengthens the verdict - 4 minutes

Draft one fair counterview and a response that keeps your judgement nuanced.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: counterview.
- **Finished when:** Counterview + response.
- **Teacher note:** The counterview may modify the verdict. Revision is a sign of stronger thinking.

### 7. Verdict, evidence, reasoning, counterweight - 5 minutes

Identify the four moves and decide which sentence does the most reasoning.

- **Student action:** Whole class; Star one move to imitate.
- **Finished when:** Four moves identified.
- **Teacher note:** This is an original teacher model. It judges only the delay visible by page 91.

### 8. Lock the case before writing - 3 minutes

Complete every box in the verdict chain.

- **Student action:** Independent; Organiser: 100-word plan.
- **Finished when:** A complete five-move plan.
- **Teacher note:** Confer with students whose evidence is relevant to the character but not to the chosen choice.

### 9. Write the 100-word Character Verdict - 12 minutes

Write the paragraph from your completed case file.

- **Student action:** Independent and silent; Facing workbook page.
- **Finished when:** One complete literary opinion.
- **Teacher note:** Keep the word count approximate. Coherent reasoning is more important than landing on exactly 100.

### 10. Test the verdict - 4 minutes

Mark every check and revise one sentence that only summarises.

- **Student action:** Independent; Workbook paragraph.
- **Finished when:** One visible reasoning revision.
- **Teacher note:** Collect or photograph a sample across different verdict positions, not only agreement with the model.

### 11. The verdict in one sentence - 2 minutes

Write your final one-sentence verdict after revision.

- **Student action:** Independent; Workbook.
- **Finished when:** One precise, defensible sentence.
- **Teacher note:** Safe stopping point. Use these sentences to group students for future evidence-and-reasoning support.


## Optional depth slides

### Switch the criterion - 8 minutes

Rejudge the same choice using a different criterion.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** A second verdict and changed reasoning.
- **Teacher note:** Optional depth. This demonstrates why literary judgements can differ without becoming arbitrary.

### Compare two character choices - 10 minutes

Judge two choices with the same criterion and write a comparative verdict.

- **Student action:** Independent; Workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** One comparative judgement.
- **Teacher note:** Optional depth for students with secure knowledge of the first 91 pages.


## Support

- Reduce the number of initial choices without removing the central decision.
- Permit oral rehearsal before workbook writing.
- Provide optional sentence launches on the projected writing brief.
- Conference first with students whose organiser contains details but no relationship or interpretation.
