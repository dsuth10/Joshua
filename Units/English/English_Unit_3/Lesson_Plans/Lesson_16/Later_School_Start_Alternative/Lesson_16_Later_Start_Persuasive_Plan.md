# Lesson 16 Alternative: The Bell-Time Verdict

## Lesson purpose

Students form a defensible policy judgement about whether secondary school should start later. They move from an immediate reaction to a precise verdict, select directly relevant evidence, explain how it supports a named criterion, acknowledge a fair counterview, respect the evidence's age boundary, and revise summary into reasoning.

This is a supplemental, non-novel alternative. The original Lesson 16 and all of its files remain unchanged.

## Curriculum connection

The lesson preserves the original Lesson 16 cognitive work:

- turn a reaction into a precise arguable judgement;
- select evidence that directly supports the judgement;
- explain the reasoning rather than merely summarising;
- state the criterion used to judge the policy;
- acknowledge and answer a fair counterview;
- revise one summary sentence into reasoning.

The final product is persuasive rather than literary: an approximately 100-word policy verdict for a school decision-maker.

## Learning intention

We are learning to form a policy verdict that is arguable, evidence-based, fair and honest about the limits of the evidence.

## Success criteria

- I can turn a reaction into a precise policy judgement.
- I can choose evidence that directly supports my criterion.
- I can explain how the evidence supports my verdict.
- I can distinguish evidence about teenagers from evidence about primary-aged students.
- I can acknowledge a fair practical counterview.
- I can revise summary into reasoning.

## Finished workbook response

An approximately 100-word **Bell-Time Verdict** addressed to a school principal or school council. It recommends, rejects or qualifies a later start-time policy and includes:

1. a precise verdict and named group;
2. a stated criterion;
3. two directly relevant evidence points;
4. reasoning that links evidence to the verdict;
5. a fair counterview and response;
6. an honest age/evidence boundary;
7. one visible revision from summary into reasoning.

## Evidence boundary

Most strong start-time research and the 8:30 am professional recommendation concern **adolescents in middle or secondary school**, not every child in every school. Australian guidance says children aged 5-13 generally need 9-11 hours of sleep, while young people aged 14-17 generally need 8-10 hours. Sleep need alone does not prove that a particular bell time is best for every age group.

Students must not write, “Research proves all schools should start at 8:30.” A defensible alternative is: “Evidence supports considering a later start for secondary students, but a primary-school decision would need age-specific local evidence.”

## Agreed delivery model

- Teacher controls the standalone HTML presentation.
- Students annotate the Reading Pack and complete the Verdict Organiser.
- Substantial composition is completed in English workbooks.
- The deck contains no required student composition fields.
- Core route: approximately 60 minutes.
- Two optional depth screens can extend the lesson towards 75 minutes.
- The final decision is open: support, oppose or propose a trial, provided it is well evidenced.

## Core sequence - approximately 60 minutes

### 1. The 8:25 bell problem - 4 minutes

Students choose an immediate response to a proposed later secondary-school start: support it, reject it, or request a trial.

- **Student action:** Silent choice, then one-sentence partner reason.
- **Finished when:** Every student has a provisional position.
- **Teacher note:** Do not settle the issue. Record the criteria hidden inside reactions: sleep, learning, family routines, transport, sport or fairness.

### 2. Reaction or policy judgement? - 4 minutes

Rank three statements from weakest to strongest and identify what improves.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: Verdict Test.
- **Finished when:** Ranking plus two improvement features.
- **Teacher note:** Stronger wording names a group, action and criterion; it does not merely sound more forceful.

### 3. Read like a policy adviser - 9 minutes

Students annotate the original evidence brief.

- **Student action:** Independent or paired reading.
- **Record:** `E` for evidence, `L` for limitation and `P` for practical consideration.
- **Finished when:** Four evidence marks, two limitations and two practical considerations.
- **Teacher note:** Preteach circadian rhythm, adolescent, association, systematic review, policy trial and criterion.

### 4. Build the evidence clock - 5 minutes

Place the strongest ideas under health/sleep, learning/attendance, practical operation and evidence limits.

- **Student action:** Independent, then partner comparison; Organiser: Evidence Clock.
- **Finished when:** Four evidence notes with source and meaning.
- **Teacher note:** A number without an explanation is not yet useful reasoning.

### 5. The age-boundary test - 5 minutes

Classify four claims as supported, reasonable inference or overclaim.

- **Student action:** Individual vote, class check, retry.
- **Finished when:** Four classifications and one boundary explanation.
- **Teacher note:** The deliberate misconception is transferring adolescent findings directly to all primary students.

### 6. Choose the criterion before the verdict - 5 minutes

Students select the most important decision criterion: health, readiness to learn, practical feasibility or fairness.

- **Student action:** Position line; Organiser: Criterion and Provisional Verdict.
- **Finished when:** Criterion, affected group and provisional verdict are recorded.
- **Teacher note:** Ask, “What evidence would make you move?” A criterion makes disagreement reasoned rather than arbitrary.

### 7. Which evidence actually proves your point? - 5 minutes

Students select two evidence points and reject one attractive but less relevant detail.

- **Student action:** Evidence trial with a partner challenge.
- **Finished when:** Two selected points and one relevance explanation.
- **Teacher note:** “Teenagers need sleep” is relevant but too general. Strong evidence names the group, finding and limit.

### 8. A fair objection strengthens the policy - 4 minutes

Students construct a counterview about transport, family schedules, after-school activities or uncertain primary-school transfer, then answer it without pretending the concern disappears.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: Counterview.
- **Finished when:** Counterview plus proportionate response.
- **Teacher note:** A trial, staged change or evaluation plan may be stronger than an absolute verdict.

### 9. Read the model like a decision-maker - 5 minutes

Students identify verdict, criterion, evidence, reasoning, counterview and limitation in the annotated model.

- **Student action:** Whole class; star one move to imitate.
- **Finished when:** Six moves are located.
- **Teacher note:** The model recommends a secondary-school trial. Other verdicts remain possible.

### 10. Lock the case before writing - 3 minutes

Students complete the verdict chain in note form.

- **Student action:** Independent; Organiser: Policy Verdict Plan.
- **Finished when:** Every move has usable notes.
- **Teacher note:** Confer first with students whose evidence concerns sleep generally but not the named group or policy.

### 11. Write the 100-word Bell-Time Verdict - 10 minutes

Students write to the principal or school council.

- **Student action:** Independent and silent; workbook.
- **Finished when:** One coherent verdict of about 90-120 words.
- **Teacher note:** Coherent reasoning matters more than an exact word count.

### 12. Turn summary into reasoning - 4 minutes

Students find one sentence that only reports information and revise it using “This matters because…”, “Therefore…” or “For this decision…”.

- **Student action:** Independent self-check, then one visible revision.
- **Finished when:** One reasoning revision is underlined.
- **Teacher note:** Collect examples across different verdicts, not only agreement with the model.

### 13. The verdict in one sentence - 2 minutes

Students write a final one-sentence verdict that names group, policy and criterion.

- **Student action:** Exit slip or workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** One precise sentence plus confidence code.
- **Teacher note:** Sort responses by evidence relevance, reasoning and age-boundary control.

## Optional depth

### Depth A: Change the audience - 7 minutes

Rewrite the verdict for a parent group rather than a school council.

- **Decision:** Which practical concern needs more space?
- **Transfer:** Preserve the evidence but alter emphasis, tone and call to action.

### Depth B: Design a fair trial - 8 minutes

Design a one-term secondary-school start-time trial.

- name the group and proposed time;
- name two outcomes to measure;
- name one family/transport safeguard;
- state what result would support keeping or reversing the change.

## Original teacher model - approximately 105 words

Our school council should trial an 8:45 am start for secondary students because student health and readiness to learn should guide the decision. A 2022 meta-analysis found later start times were associated with longer sleep and less negative mood, while sleep specialists recommend 8:30 am or later for middle and high schools. This matters because the evidence concerns adolescents whose body clocks tend to shift later. Families may reasonably worry about transport and afternoon activities, so the trial should monitor attendance, sleep and scheduling problems. The same evidence does not prove that primary students need a later bell. A measured secondary trial is therefore stronger than an all-school change.

## Answer guidance

### Verdict test

Weakest: “Later starts are awesome.”  
Stronger: “Schools should start later because students are tired.”  
Strongest: “The school council should trial a later start for secondary students because adolescent sleep and readiness to learn outweigh manageable scheduling costs.”

The strongest statement names the decision-maker, action, group, criterion and qualified scope.

### Age-boundary classifications

1. “Later start times are associated with longer sleep and less negative mood across the reviewed youth studies.” - **Supported**
2. “An 8:30 am recommendation applies specifically to middle and high schools.” - **Supported**
3. “Therefore every primary school should start at 8:30 am.” - **Overclaim**
4. “A secondary-school trial may be justified if the school also checks local transport and activity effects.” - **Reasonable inference**

## Support

- Allow paired reading and oral rehearsal.
- Provide the evidence cards already reduced to one finding plus one limit.
- Offer optional sentence launches without making one structure compulsory.
- Reduce the initial policy options to “trial” or “do not trial” while preserving the evidence judgement.
- Conference first on group, criterion and evidence relevance.

## Extension

- Compare health and academic criteria using the same evidence.
- Evaluate whether “associated with” is strong enough to justify a permanent policy.
- Design an evaluation measure that could distinguish improved sleep from improved test scores.
- Write a verdict for primary school and explain why the evidence is less secure.

## Formative assessment

- Entry position reveals reaction and implicit criteria.
- Ranking reveals whether students recognise precision rather than force.
- Evidence selection reveals relevance.
- Age-boundary classifications expose overgeneralisation.
- Counterview reveals fairness and policy realism.
- Visible revision reveals whether students can turn reporting into reasoning.
- Exit sentence diagnoses readiness for later persuasive writing.

## Source trail and research limits

Research checked 29 July 2026.

- Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing, **24-hour movement guidelines for all Australians**: https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians
- American Academy of Sleep Medicine, **School Start Times Health Advisory**: https://aasm.org/advocacy/position-statements/school-start-times-health-advisory/
- Watson et al. (2017), **Delaying Middle School and High School Start Times Promotes Student Health and Performance**: https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.6558
- Yip et al. (2022), **School Start Times, Sleep, and Youth Outcomes: A Meta-analysis**: https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2021-054068
- Marx et al. (2022), **School start times and academic achievement - a systematic review on grades and test scores**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2021.101582
- Sleep Health Foundation (Australia), **Teenage Sleep**: https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-topics/teenage-sleep

The evidence brief deliberately distinguishes association from proof, adolescent evidence from primary-school transfer, sleep outcomes from academic achievement, and a professional recommendation from a guaranteed result in every local context.
