# Lesson 16 Alternative Teacher Guide

## The Bell-Time Verdict

This supplemental lesson replaces novel content with a current policy question: **Should secondary school start later?** The original Lesson 16 remains unchanged.

## Teaching in one sentence

Students research the issue, choose a criterion, test the relevance and limits of evidence, then write and revise an approximately 100-word verdict for a school decision-maker.

## Core misconception

“Teenagers benefit from later starts” does **not** automatically mean “every primary student should start later.”

Make the evidence boundary visible:

- sleep recommendations cover broad age bands;
- puberty changes sleep timing for many adolescents;
- the professional 8:30 am recommendation names middle and high schools;
- the strongest review evidence is weighted towards adolescent/secondary settings;
- local transport, family and activity effects still require local investigation;
- later starts are more consistently linked with sleep and mood than with improved grades or test scores.

## Evidence brief at a glance

| Evidence | What it can support | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Australian guidance: ages 5-13 generally need 9-11 hours; ages 14-17 generally need 8-10 hours | Sufficient sleep matters across childhood and adolescence | One universal school start time |
| Adolescent body clocks commonly shift later through puberty | Early starts can conflict with adolescent sleep timing | Every teenager has the same sleep pattern |
| AASM recommends 8:30 am or later for middle and high schools | A later secondary start is a credible policy option | The same recommendation applies to all primary schools |
| 2022 meta-analysis: later starts associated with longer sleep, less negative mood and better overall developmental outcomes | A secondary trial has an evidence base | A guaranteed causal benefit for every student |
| 2022 academic review: no generalisable grade/test-score improvement, but achievement was generally maintained | Avoid “later starts definitely raise grades” | That later starts have no value |

## Suggested teacher language

### At the hook

“Today, strength does not come from choosing the popular side. Strength comes from naming the group, the criterion, the evidence and the limit.”

### At the age boundary

“A source may be trustworthy and still be the wrong source for your exact claim. Relevance includes the age group.”

### At evidence selection

“Which detail most directly proves your verdict under your criterion? Which detail is interesting but not decisive?”

### At the counterview

“A fair objection is not a straw person. State the strongest practical concern a thoughtful family or school leader could raise.”

### At revision

“If your sentence only tells me what the source said, add the policy thinking: why does it matter for this group, criterion and decision?”

## Interaction answer guidance

### Verdict test

1. “Later starts are awesome.” - reaction only.
2. “Schools should start later because students are tired.” - arguable but vague group, evidence and criterion.
3. “The school council should trial a later start for secondary students because adolescent sleep and readiness to learn outweigh manageable scheduling costs.” - precise group, decision-maker, action, criterion and qualification.

### Evidence-boundary check

1. Later start times are associated with longer sleep and less negative mood across the reviewed youth studies. - **Supported**
2. The professional 8:30 am recommendation applies specifically to middle and high schools. - **Supported**
3. Every primary school should therefore start at 8:30 am. - **Overclaim**
4. A secondary trial may be justified if the school also checks local transport and activity effects. - **Reasonable inference**

### Evidence trial

For a health/readiness criterion, the meta-analysis and adolescent circadian explanation are more direct than a claim about test scores. For a feasibility criterion, local transport and activity data would be essential; international health research alone is insufficient.

### Fair counterviews

Accept:

- later finishing times could affect sport, work, care arrangements or transport;
- family schedules may not shift easily;
- students may simply stay up later;
- primary-school evidence is less direct;
- local effects need measurement.

A proportionate answer may propose a trial, staged implementation, sleep education, consultation, transport safeguards or explicit evaluation. Do not require students to pretend the concern vanishes.

## Model annotation

> Our school council should trial an 8:45 am start for secondary students **[verdict + scope]** because student health and readiness to learn should guide the decision **[criterion]**. A 2022 meta-analysis found later start times were associated with longer sleep and less negative mood **[evidence]**, while sleep specialists recommend 8:30 am or later for middle and high schools **[evidence + source scope]**. This matters because the evidence concerns adolescents whose body clocks tend to shift later **[reasoning]**. Families may reasonably worry about transport and afternoon activities **[fair counterview]**, so the trial should monitor attendance, sleep and scheduling problems **[response]**. The same evidence does not prove that primary students need a later bell **[limitation]**. A measured secondary trial is therefore stronger than an all-school change **[reasoned conclusion]**.

## Quick conference prompts

- Who exactly is affected by your verdict?
- What criterion are you using?
- Which sentence contains your strongest evidence?
- Where do you explain why it matters?
- Is your source about children, adolescents or both?
- What would a reasonable opponent say?
- Which sentence can you revise from summary into reasoning?

## Lucas / ICP delivery

- Read the four evidence cards aloud.
- Let the student choose “trial” or “do not trial”.
- Ask for one criterion: health or practical routines.
- Co-construct one sentence using “I recommend ___ because ___.”
- Add one fair concern using “Some families may worry ___.”
- Finish with “This evidence is mainly about teenagers, not every primary student.”
- Accept spoken, pointed, copied, typed or scribed responses.

## Optional local adaptation

Before teaching, substitute the school's actual current bell time only if it is publicly known or supplied by the teacher. Do not invent local transport routes, family patterns or attendance results. These are exactly the missing local evidence students should identify.

## Accuracy note

All student-facing prose is an original synthesis, not copied source text. The reading uses “associated with” where the evidence is associational, keeps recommendations attached to their named age/school group, and avoids promising improved grades.
