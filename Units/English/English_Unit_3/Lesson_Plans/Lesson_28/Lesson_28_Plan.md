# Lesson 28: Outlining the Persuasive Plan

## Lesson purpose

Students map arguments, counterarguments and traceable evidence on the planning sheet for their individually selected assessment topic. Ari’s pp. 187–189 return home provides a literary model of a position supported by reasons, anticipated objections and planned responses.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 — AC9E5LY06:** plan, create and edit written persuasive texts.
- **Year 6 — AC9E6LY06:** plan, create and edit persuasive texts using paragraphs.

## Learning intention

We are learning to organise our opinion, reasons, evidence and response to a fair concern before we draft.

## Success criteria

- I can identify an audience, an action and a clear opinion.
- I can organise two or more reasons in a helpful order.
- I can attach accurate evidence notes and source details to my reasons.
- I can plan a fair counterargument and a response.
- I can plan a conclusion that asks my audience to act.

## Assessment requirements preserved

Part A is an **individual** written persuasive text, planned, created and edited for an identified audience, with a suggested length of **200–400 words**. Students research differing ideas and supporting information, including authoritative sources, identify their sources, use paragraphs to develop and link ideas, and review for spelling, punctuation and meaning. Part B is an **individual 1–3 minute** presentation that justifies an opinion for an identified audience using features of voice. The written text is a guide for, not merely a script read in, the spoken presentation.

## Preparation

- Open the standalone presentation in a browser; teacher logistics are in the Notes popup only.
- Print the appropriate Lesson 28 organiser and ensure Lesson 27 research notes / checked sources are available.
- Read *Berani* pp. 187–189. This lesson builds the plan; drafting follows in later lessons.

## Core sequence (50 min)

1. A decision needs more than a wish — 4 min
2. Plan ideas before drafting — 2 min
3. Ari’s claim with stakes — 5 min
4. Anticipated questions — 5 min
5. Paragraph jobs — 6 min
6. Evidence, not invented proof — 5 min
7. Ready-to-draft comparison — 4 min
8. Map arguments — 5 min
9. Planning studio — 11 min
10. Peer plan audit — 4 min
11. Exit plan check — 2 min

## Accuracy boundary

Ari plans for Suni to attend middle school, accepts he will find paid work for his fees, help in the fields and continue studying chess; the administrator’s condition is that both students enrol the following year (pp. 187–189). This is a literary example of planning—not evidence for students’ own topics and not a universal claim about education, work or family decisions. Model responses demonstrate structure only; their generic research language is not verified evidence for student use.

## Differentiation

**Support:** offer an approved topic menu, a labelled planning sheet and verbal rehearsal. Prioritise a precise opinion, two source-linked reasons and one fair concern.

**Lucas (ICP):** students may point, speak, copy or use a partner scribe to select an audience, a “should” opinion, one reason, one helpful action and a simple response to a concern. The destination remains a purposeful plan for an audience.

**Extend:** sequence arguments deliberately for the audience; use a source that complicates the position and plan a respectful, evidence-based response.

## Formative assessment

The paragraph-job sort reveals structural confusion. The planning studio and peer audit show whether students have a traceable evidence trail, a fair concern and a clear audience before drafting. The exit directs conferences for Lesson 30.

## Discussion guidance

Use negotiated, school/community-safe topics. Do not ask students to disclose family finances, education access, gender discrimination or personal hardship. Record “needs checking” rather than inventing evidence.
