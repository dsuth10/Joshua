# Teacher Guide: Letters from Seabreeze Square

## Central teaching point

Bias is created by more than emotional words. It also appears when a writer generalises, assumes motives, selects only favourable detail, omits a relevant concern or presents prediction as certainty. Balanced writing can still take a firm position; it becomes more trustworthy by attributing claims, acknowledging limits and representing opposing concerns accurately.

The middle letters can be ordered differently when students justify their placement using the published criteria.

## Verified fictional briefing

The only shared local facts are:

- Seabreeze Council is considering a trial, not a permanent program;
- the proposed trial lasts six Friday evenings;
- operating hours are 5:00 pm to 8:00 pm;
- no more than eight trucks may attend;
- the plan includes waste stations and an accessible path;
- council will review the trial afterwards.

Nothing in the briefing proves future noise, profit, parking failure, community renewal or universal support.

## Bias fingerprints and answers

- **Loaded evaluation:** circus, invasion, lifeless, rescue, selfish, reckless.
- **Sweeping generalisation:** every resident, nobody asked, everyone knows, only sensible choice.
- **Assumed motive:** councillors only want photographs; critics want young people to leave.
- **Omission / selection:** C foregrounds twelve unavailable bays but omits trial duration and safeguards; D foregrounds social opportunity but omits noise, access and waste concerns.
- **Unsupported certainty / false choice:** the trial will destroy the square; food trucks will rescue the town; either support it or accept a dead town centre.

## Fingerprint teaching popups

Clicking a fingerprint card projects a short teaching letter written to isolate that one move. These letters are not A-F.

1. **Loaded evaluation:** “Council's disgraceful food-truck scheme would dump a miserable circus on our peaceful square...” Notice judging words such as disgraceful, reckless, foolish and ugly.
2. **Sweeping generalisation:** “Every resident of Seabreeze wants the trucks gone...” Notice every, nobody, all, always and anyone.
3. **Assumed motive:** “Councillors support the trucks because they want to look popular in photographs...” Notice guessed reasons, not observed facts.
4. **Omission / selection:** “A Friday food-truck trial would offer affordable meals and a place to meet...” Notice what is missing: hours, cap, waste, access, parking, noise and review.
5. **Unsupported certainty / false choice:** “Either council approves the full trial tonight or Seabreeze will stay a dead town forever.” Notice will, either...or and forever.

## Additional classification slide bank

Use the five extra CFU slides flexibly. Each one follows the same routine: students commit on a mini-whiteboard, justify with exact wording, then the teacher allows a click, hint or answer reveal. The slides are deliberately short so they can be used as a 3-minute check, or run as a 15-minute retrieval block when the class needs more practice.

1. **Loaded evaluation:** “The council's reckless plan will turn our peaceful square into a miserable mess.” The strongest fingerprint is loaded evaluation because “reckless”, “peaceful” and “miserable” judge the plan and its setting.
2. **Sweeping generalisation:** “Every sensible resident knows the trucks will ruin Friday nights.” The strongest fingerprint is sweeping generalisation because “every sensible resident” claims to speak for a whole group and excludes dissent.
3. **Assumed motive:** “The councillors support the trucks because they want flattering photographs.” The strongest fingerprint is assumed motive because the writer claims to know a private reason without evidence.
4. **Omission / selection:** “Food trucks offer affordable meals and a place to meet.” The strongest fingerprint is omission / selection because the sentence foregrounds benefits while leaving relevant concerns out of view.
5. **Unsupported certainty / false choice:** “Either council approves the full trial tonight or Seabreeze will stay a dead town forever.” The strongest fingerprint is unsupported certainty / false choice because “either...or...” removes middle options and “will” / “forever” present predictions as facts.

For any answer, ask students to name the exact word or clause first. If a student identifies a secondary feature, accept it as an observation but return to the question: **which problem is doing the most persuasive work here?**

## Balanced-language moves

- report rather than absorb claims: **The writer argues...**;
- concede accurately: **Nearby residents may reasonably be concerned about...**;
- qualify predictions: **may, could, if the trial shows...**;
- state boundaries: **The proposal does not yet show...**;
- recommend concretely: hours, cap, accessible path, waste stations, measures and review.

## Model Editorial Fairness Briefing

Seabreeze Council is considering a six-Friday food-truck trial from 5:00 pm to 8:00 pm, with no more than eight vendors. Letters A and C oppose or question the proposal, while B, D and E support some form of trial; F recommends a smaller, carefully managed test. Letter A calls the proposal a "food-truck circus", which frames an untested trial as disorder before evidence is considered. Letter B describes critics as a "whining few", assuming that disagreement is selfish rather than reporting the concern. Letter C uses calmer wording, but it foregrounds parking loss and does not mention the proposed vendor cap, waste stations or later review. A neutral version of Letter A's claim would be: "The writer argues that the trial may increase evening noise and asks council to reconsider the location." Letter E is the most trustworthy because it states the limits of current knowledge, acknowledges residents' concerns and proposes measures rather than guaranteed outcomes. Its support is conditional, but its position remains clear.

## Neutral rewrite examples

- **Biased:** Reckless councillors are surrendering our square to a noisy invasion.  
  **Neutral:** The writer opposes the proposed trial because they are concerned about noise and use of the square.
- **Biased:** Only selfish complainers could oppose this brilliant rescue plan.  
  **Neutral:** The writer supports the trial and argues that it could increase evening activity in the town centre.
- **Biased:** The trial will make parking impossible.  
  **Neutral:** The writer predicts that the trial may increase parking pressure, although the briefing does not provide a traffic forecast.

## Red-green presentation control

The unlabelled switch changes projected language on the current slide. Red uses extended analytical language and an 8-10 sentence balanced article rewritten from Letter A, B, C or D. Green uses concise language and a 5-7 sentence article rewritten from Letter A or B, while retaining viewpoint, missing facts, qualification and a recommended action. The slide, timer, reveal and answer states remain in place. Do not describe the colours as ability levels or student groups.

## Feedback codes

- **WORD+** - exact wording identified
- **EFFECT+** - positioning effect explained
- **OMIT+** - relevant omission identified
- **ATTR+** - viewpoint attributed rather than stated as fact
- **FAIR+** - neutral rewrite preserves the real position
- **BOUND+** - claim and evidence limit distinguished

## Pacing fallback

If reading takes longer than planned, compare A, B, E and F only, teach the five fingerprints, and still require a rewrite of Letter A or B as a balanced article. Keep the final revision.

## Fiction boundary

Every letter and local detail is teacher-created fiction. The texts are deliberately engineered to reveal bias. Do not present a letter's prediction as an actual outcome or ask students to disclose personal business, parking or neighbourhood disputes.
