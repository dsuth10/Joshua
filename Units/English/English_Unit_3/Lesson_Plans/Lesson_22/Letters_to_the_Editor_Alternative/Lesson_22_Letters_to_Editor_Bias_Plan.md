# Lesson 22 Alternative: Letters from Seabreeze Square

### Pedagogical Contemplation

The cognitive goal is to identify how word choice, certainty, assumed motives and omission position a reader, then to report competing viewpoints fairly. A sequence of letters to the editor is ideal because every writer addresses the same decision while selecting different details and tones. Students can therefore hold the topic steady and compare the language choices that create bias.

The lesson moves from obvious bias to subtler slant, then to balanced persuasion. This matters: balanced language is not positionless or weak. A balanced writer can recommend firmly while naming limits, attributing claims and acknowledging reasonable concerns. Annotation, spectrum placement, targeted classification and neutral rewriting make student thinking visible. The newspaper setting supplies an authentic editorial purpose; the engagement treatment serves close language analysis and revision.

## Lesson purpose

Students read six original letters to the editor about a fictional six-Friday food-truck trial at Seabreeze Square. The letters range from heavily biased to balanced but clearly opinionated. Students locate exact language choices, identify what each writer foregrounds or omits, and prepare a neutral editorial briefing that reports the debate fairly.

This supplemental alternative is independent of *Berani* and does not replace the original Lesson 22.

## Curriculum alignment

- **Year 5 - AC9E5LA02:** acknowledge differing ideas and opinions.
- **Year 6 - AC9E6LA02:** identify bias in persuasive texts.

The lesson also rehearses precise vocabulary analysis and persuasive revision without changing the lesson's primary language-analysis destination.

## Learning intention

We are learning to identify how language and omission position a reader, and to report competing viewpoints fairly.

## Success criteria

- I can identify loaded or evaluative words and explain their likely effect.
- I can recognise sweeping generalisations, assumed motives and unsupported certainty.
- I can explain what a letter foregrounds and what it leaves out.
- I can distinguish a strong opinion from unfair or misleading positioning.
- I can recognise balanced language that still recommends a clear action.
- I can rewrite a biased statement in neutral, attributed language.

## Finished workbook response

An **8-10 sentence Editorial Fairness Briefing** that:

1. states the shared decision accurately;
2. reports at least two different positions using neutral reporting verbs;
3. quotes or precisely copies three short language choices;
4. explains how two choices position readers;
5. identifies one important omission or unsupported certainty;
6. rewrites one biased sentence neutrally;
7. judges which letter is most trustworthy and explains why; and
8. includes one visible revision.

## Shared fictional decision

Seabreeze Council is considering a six-Friday trial in which up to eight food trucks could operate at Seabreeze Square from 5:00 pm to 8:00 pm. The draft plan keeps an accessible path open, requires waste stations and ends with a council review. All local details, writers and quotations are fictional.

## Delivery model

- Teacher controls the standalone HTML presentation.
- Students annotate the reading pack and use the Bias Spectrum Organiser.
- Substantial writing occurs in English workbooks or an approved device.
- The red-green switch changes projected language only; it never labels students or pathways.
- Red uses extended analytical language. Green reduces sentence and output load while retaining word-effect, omission and fair-reporting reasoning.
- Teacher logistics stay in the Notes popup.
- Core route: approximately **60 minutes**.

## Core sequence

### 1. Same proposal, opposite headlines - 4 minutes

Compare **Food-Truck Circus Threatens Our Square** with **Food Trucks Will Rescue Our Dying Town**.

- **Student action:** Identify what each headline invites readers to feel before evidence appears.
- **Finished when:** One exact word and one likely reader effect are named.
- **Misconception exposed:** Bias exists only when a writer disagrees with me.

### 2. The editor's problem - 3 minutes

Introduce the shared fictional briefing and final Editorial Fairness Briefing.

- **Student action:** State what an editor must preserve and remove.
- **Finished when:** Students name viewpoint accuracy and neutral reporting as separate requirements.

### 3. Read the editor's verified briefing - 4 minutes

Students establish the limited facts that all six letters may use.

- **Student action:** Box the proposed duration, hours, vendor cap and safeguards.
- **Finished when:** Four accurate proposal details are available as a comparison baseline.

### 4. Bias leaves five fingerprints - 6 minutes

Explicitly teach:

1. **loaded evaluation** - labels that praise, shame, alarm or minimise;
2. **sweeping generalisation** - everyone, nobody, always, every;
3. **assumed motive** - claiming to know why a person or group acts;
4. **omission / selection** - foregrounding one side while hiding relevant detail;
5. **unsupported certainty or false choice** - presenting prediction as fact or allowing only two possibilities.

- **Student action:** Click each fingerprint card, read the plain example letter, then name the wording and its job.
- **Finished when:** Five labels and at least two effect explanations.

### 5. Read the loudest letters - 7 minutes

Read Letter A, **Stop the Friday Food-Truck Circus**, and Letter B, **Let the Town Wake Up**.

- **Student action:** Underline loaded wording, circle generalisations and box assumed motives.
- **Finished when:** At least three marks per letter and one explanation of how bias operates in opposite directions.

### 6. Classify the fingerprint - 5 minutes

Use the interactive statement challenge. Students classify a phrase before the teacher clicks.

- **Student action:** Individual decision, pair justification, then class check.
- **Finished when:** Accurate category plus a word-level explanation.
- **Feedback:** First error prompts a reread; second error reveals a precise hint.

### 6A. Additional fingerprint check bank - optional 3-15 minutes

Five additional classification slides provide a short, repeatable practice bank. Use one or two when the class is ready to move on, or use all five when students need more evidence before reading the quieter letters. The sequence deliberately revisits each fingerprint: loaded evaluation, sweeping generalisation, assumed motive, omission / selection, and unsupported certainty / false choice.

- **Student action:** Commit to a category, point to the exact words, then explain the reader effect or missing information.
- **Finished when:** Students can distinguish the main fingerprint even when a phrase contains more than one persuasive feature.
- **Feedback:** First error prompts a targeted reread; second error reveals a concise hint; teacher reveal marks the defensible category.

### 7. Find the quiet slant - 6 minutes

Read Letter C, **Parking First, Please**, and Letter D, **A Chance for Young People**. Neither uses obvious insults, yet each selects one side and predicts beyond the verified briefing.

- **Student action:** Mark foregrounded detail, omitted detail and certainty words.
- **Finished when:** One omission and one unsupported prediction are explained.
- **Misconception exposed:** Calm language is automatically balanced.

### 8. Balanced does not mean neutral mush - 6 minutes

Read Letter E, **Trial It, Measure It, Adjust It**, and Letter F, **Protect the Square While Testing the Idea**.

- **Student action:** Identify concession, qualification, attribution, evidence boundary and clear recommendation.
- **Finished when:** Students can explain how each writer remains persuasive without distorting the other view.

### 9. Place the letters on a bias spectrum - 5 minutes

Students place A-F from most heavily positioned to most balanced and justify two placements using exact wording. On the projected slide, click a card to reread the letter and drag the cards to test a class order.

- **Student action:** Independent placement in the organiser, then compare with a partner and drag the shared order.
- **Finished when:** Six placements and two text-based justifications.
- **Teacher note:** Accept different middle placements where evidence and criteria are sound.

### 10. Model a neutral editorial report - 5 minutes

Deconstruct a complete model that attributes viewpoints, distinguishes proposal facts from claims and comments on positioning.

- **Student action:** Label reporting verb, accurate detail, quoted wording, effect, omission and neutral rewrite.
- **Finished when:** Six model moves are located.

### 11. Write the Editorial Fairness Briefing - 10 minutes

- **Student action:** Write independently using the organiser and reading pack.
- **Finished when:** All required elements are present.

### 12. Precision check and exit - 4 minutes

Partners identify one loaded, vague or mind-reading phrase. Writers revise immediately, then complete: **A letter can sound calm and still be biased when...**

- **Finished when:** One visible revision and one accurate exit principle.

## Support

- Preteach: **biased, balanced, loaded, generalisation, motive, omission, certainty, attribute, qualify**.
- Begin with Letters A, B, E and F before adding the subtler C and D.
- Colour-code exact word, reader effect and missing detail.
- Offer neutral reporting stems: **The writer argues...**, **The letter claims...**, **The proposal states...**, **The writer does not address...**.
- Permit a 6-7 sentence briefing while retaining two viewpoints, two quoted choices, one omission and one neutral rewrite.

## Concise access / ICP

- Use the Concise Access Pack with four shortened letters: two strongly biased and two balanced.
- Preserve the same destination: identify strong judging words, notice what is missing and create one fair report.
- Students may point, highlight, speak, copy, type or use a scribe.
- Use the accessible labels **strong judging words**, **words about everyone**, **guessing people's reasons**, **missing information** and **too-certain words**, alongside the technical terms.

## Extension

- Compare bias created through omission with bias created through overt loaded language.
- Rewrite a balanced letter to make it subtly slanted without adding an obvious insult, then explain the changes.
- Explain why Letter E may be more credible than Letter D even though both support the trial.
- Distinguish a justified prediction from unsupported certainty.

## Formative assessment and next teaching response

- Reteach word-effect links if students only label language as "biased".
- Reteach omission if calm letters are automatically labelled balanced.
- Reteach attribution if students reproduce a letter's claim as fact.
- Extend secure students into the relationship between source selection and bias.

## Accuracy and safety boundaries

- Seabreeze Council, the square, proposal, writers, quotations and local details are fictional.
- The editor's briefing is the only shared fact base. Letters may make claims beyond it; students must identify those as claims, not facts.
- Do not ask students to disclose family income, business ownership, neighbourhood conflict or political views.
- Balanced language does not require equal agreement with every claim. It requires accurate representation, relevant qualification and fair reasoning.
