# Lesson 30: Drafting — Introduction & Thesis

## Lesson purpose

Students transfer their Lesson 28 plan into an original persuasive introduction. They use the Year 5 and Year 6 Storytime Dog model introductions as structural examples only: a hook engages an audience, brief background establishes context, and a thesis states the intended action and key directions.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 — AC9E5LY06:** plan, create and edit written persuasive texts, developing and expanding on ideas with supporting details.
- **Year 6 — AC9E6LY06:** plan, create and edit persuasive texts using paragraphs, including complex sentences and expanded noun groups.

## Learning intention

We are learning to draft an introduction that engages our audience, gives relevant background and clearly states our opinion.

## Success criteria

- I can choose a hook that suits my audience and purpose.
- I can include only the background a reader needs.
- I can write a clear thesis that says what my audience should do.
- I can preview the reasons I will develop with checked information.
- I can revise my introduction when a reader cannot identify my action.

## Assessment alignment

Part A is an **individual** persuasive text for an identified audience, suggested at **200–400 words**. It must develop and expand ideas with supporting details, organise ideas in paragraphs and use appropriate language features. Part B is an **individual 1–3 minute** presentation; the written text is a guide for the spoken presentation, rather than a script merely read aloud. This lesson drafts only the opening and thesis; students will develop evidence-based body paragraphs in Lesson 31.

## Preparation

- Open Lesson_30_Presentation.html; all DO/WORK/RECORD/FINISH/CHECK logistics are in the Notes popup.
- Print the appropriate organiser and provide students’ Lesson 28 plans and checked Lesson 27 research notes.
- Display the Year 5 and Year 6 Storytime Dog introductions from the model-response materials. Treat their research language as a structural model, not a source for student evidence.

## Core sequence (50 min)

1. Why an opening matters — 3 min
2. Three introduction jobs — 3 min
3. Compare openings — 4 min
4. Annotate the Year 6 structural model — 6 min
5. Compare Year 5 and Year 6 elaboration — 5 min
6. Sentence-job sort — 5 min
7. Build a thesis — 4 min
8. Plan hook, background and thesis — 5 min
9. Independent drafting studio — 12 min
10. Reader test and revision — 5 min
11. Exit readiness check — 3 min

## Accuracy boundary

The Storytime Dog model introductions are used only to analyse persuasive structure. Students must not present their generic statements about reading, wellbeing or animal-assisted learning as verified evidence, nor copy the model as their own assessment response. Their later reasons and supporting details must come from their own identified, checked sources.

## Differentiation

**Support:** provide a three-line introduction planner, a choice of hook types, oral rehearsal and word-processing spelling support. Conference on audience, action and one precise thesis before students elaborate.

**Lucas (ICP):** students may point, speak, copy or use a partner scribe to choose an audience, a “should” statement and one reason. They can record one short opening sentence followed by their clear action statement. The core destination remains an introduction with a purposeful opinion for an audience.

**Extend:** shape a nuanced hook for a particular decision-maker, use an expanded noun group or carefully controlled complex sentence, and preview the argument’s ordering without adding unsupported claims.

## Formative assessment

The opening comparison and sentence-job sort show whether students distinguish engagement, context and thesis. During the studio, the underlined thesis makes audience/action clarity visible. The peer reader test and exit identify students needing an introduction conference before Lesson 31 body-paragraph drafting.

## Discussion and safety guidance

Use negotiated school or community-safe topics. Do not require personal disclosures. Students should label a claim as needing checking instead of inventing a fact, statistic, quotation or source.
