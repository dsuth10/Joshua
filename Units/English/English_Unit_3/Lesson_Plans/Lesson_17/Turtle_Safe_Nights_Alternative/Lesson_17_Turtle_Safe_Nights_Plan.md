# Lesson 17 Alternative: Turtle-Safe Nights

## Lesson purpose

Students use a current Queensland coastal issue to analyse how an opportunity can lead to an action and a chain of consequences. They consider how stakeholder viewpoint shapes a response, then write and revise an evidence-based recommendation about strengthening beachfront-lighting rules during locally verified marine turtle nesting and hatching periods.

This is a supplemental alternative. It does not replace or modify the original Lesson 17.

## Transfer from the original lesson

The original lesson distinguishes opportunities, choices and consequences; examines how viewpoint shapes response; and uses a chessboard as a cautious interpretive metaphor. This alternative transfers the same thinking:

- **opportunity:** a coastal council can review seasonal lighting rules;
- **action:** the council can strengthen, target or retain current requirements;
- **consequences:** changes may affect turtle behaviour, beach darkness, property lighting, safety planning, costs and community routines;
- **viewpoint:** different stakeholders notice different risks, responsibilities and practical needs;
- **interpretive metaphor:** a **light pathway** or **ripple map** can show a defensible chain, but it cannot prove the exact size of an effect.

## Curriculum alignment

The alternative retains the original lesson’s endorsed curriculum focus:

- **Year 5 — AC9E5LE03:** recognise that point of view influences how readers interpret and respond to events and characters. Here, students transfer the concept to how stakeholder viewpoint shapes response to an environmental decision.
- **Year 6 — AC9E6LE01:** identify responses drawn from social, ethical and cultural contexts. Here, students examine responses to a public decision involving wildlife protection, human safety, local livelihoods and Sea Country stewardship.

It also rehearses persuasive creation described in **AC9E5LY06** and **AC9E6LY06** without replacing the lesson’s primary literature focus.

## Learning intention

We are learning to trace a defensible chain of consequence, explain how viewpoint shapes a response and use evidence fairly in a recommendation.

## Success criteria

- I can distinguish an opportunity, an action and a consequence.
- I can build a consequence chain using **because**, **therefore** and **which means**.
- I can represent at least two stakeholder viewpoints fairly.
- I can separate official evidence, reasonable inference and overclaim.
- I can use a light pathway as an interpretive model and state its limitation.
- I can write and visibly revise an evidence-based council recommendation.

## Finished workbook response

An **8–10 sentence recommendation to a coastal council** that:

1. states a clear position on stronger seasonal beachfront-lighting rules;
2. uses at least three accurate evidence points from the reading;
3. represents a reasonable stakeholder concern fairly;
4. explains a defensible chain of consequence;
5. recommends one concrete, workable action;
6. uses the light pathway cautiously rather than claiming it proves an exact outcome; and
7. includes one visible revision after feedback.

## Core proposition

> A coastal council should strengthen lighting rules in mapped turtle-sensitive areas during locally verified nesting and hatching periods, while using purpose-specific lighting design to meet human safety needs.

Students may qualify or disagree with this proposition if they use accurate evidence and propose a workable alternative.

## Delivery model

- Interactions are designed to expose causal and evidentiary reasoning, not guessing.

## Core sequence — approximately 60 minutes

### 1. Which horizon wins? — 4 minutes

Display the night-coast scene. Students silently predict which horizon a hatchling might follow, then explain what evidence would be needed before making a strong claim.

- **Student action:** Think–pair–share; Organiser: first prediction.
- **Finished when:** One prediction plus one evidence need.
- **Teacher look-for:** Students distinguish an observation about the visual from a factual claim about turtle behaviour.

### 2. The council has a choice before turtle season — 2 minutes

Introduce the council decision and the final product.

- **Student action:** Copy or paraphrase the decision question.
- **Finished when:** Students can name the audience, purpose and decision.
- **Teacher note:** The issue is not “lights or no lights”. It is how to provide necessary light while reducing avoidable wildlife impacts.

### 3. Read like a council evidence adviser — 8 minutes

Students annotate the reading pack with:

- **E** — evidence about turtles or light;
- **A** — a possible council action;
- **V** — a stakeholder value, responsibility or concern;
- **?** — a claim that needs a boundary or more local information.

- **Student action:** Independent annotation, then compare one mark with a partner.
- **Finished when:** At least two E marks, one A, one V and one ?.
- **Teacher note:** The article is an original classroom synthesis based on official sources. It does not present invented stakeholder quotations.

### 4. Build the opportunity–action–consequence chain — 6 minutes

Use the projected chain challenge. Students decide which council move creates the most defensible pathway and explain the causal links.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: O–A–C chain.
- **Finished when:** Three linked stages and two because/therefore explanations.
- **Misconception exposed:** A desired outcome is not automatically a guaranteed consequence.
- **Feedback:** Retry prompts name the missing causal link rather than revealing the whole answer.

### 5. One coast, several legitimate viewpoints — 6 minutes

Students compare stakeholder lenses:

- turtle researcher or ranger;
- beachfront resident;
- local business or accommodation operator;
- Traditional Owner ranger or Sea Country representative;
- council safety and infrastructure officer.

- **Student action:** Choose two lenses and complete: “This stakeholder may prioritise ___ because ___.”
- **Finished when:** Two fair representations and one shared value.
- **Teacher note:** These are analytical viewpoint lenses, not quotations from named people. Do not ask a student to speak on behalf of a cultural group. A real council should consult the culturally appropriate Traditional Owners for that Sea Country.

### 6. Test the light pathway — 5 minutes

Students use a light pathway or ripple map to connect:

**council rule → lighting design/behaviour change → beachward light conditions → possible turtle response**

They label the evidence-supported steps and mark one limitation.

- **Student action:** Organiser: pathway map.
- **Finished when:** One four-step chain plus “This model cannot prove…”.
- **Teacher note:** The pathway is an interpretive organising device. It does not calculate how many turtles will survive or predict every site.

### 7. Evidence, inference or overclaim? — 5 minutes

Use the three-part projected classification.

- **Student action:** Vote, justify, test and retry.
- **Finished when:** Students correctly classify one official-evidence statement, one reasonable inference and one overclaim.
- **Teacher look-for:** Appropriate modal language such as **can**, **may**, **is likely to** and **would help**, rather than **will always** or **guarantees**.

### 8. Read the model recommendation as a writer — 5 minutes

Reveal and annotate the model’s moves:

- recommendation;
- official evidence;
- causal explanation;
- fair stakeholder concern;
- qualified response;
- concrete action;
- metaphor boundary;
- monitoring and review.

- **Student action:** Star one move to imitate and circle one cautious verb.
- **Finished when:** One chosen move plus its purpose.

### 9. Plan the council recommendation — 5 minutes

Students complete the organiser:

1. position;
2. strongest evidence;
3. consequence chain;
4. stakeholder concern;
5. response or qualification;
6. concrete action;
7. metaphor limitation.

- **Student action:** Independent planning; optional oral rehearsal.
- **Finished when:** Every move has a note.
- **Teacher conference priority:** Students with a list of facts but no consequence relationship.

### 10. Write the 8–10 sentence recommendation — 13 minutes

Students write for the council audience.

- **Student action:** Independent; workbook or approved device.
- **Finished when:** A complete recommendation meeting the criteria.
- **Optional sentence launches:**  
  “The council should…”  
  “Official guidance explains…”  
  “Because…, this may…”  
  “A reasonable concern is…”  
  “However, the council can…”  
  “The light pathway suggests…, although it cannot prove…”

### 11. Trace one ripple and revise — 4 minutes

Partners use two feedback stems:

- “Your clearest evidence-to-consequence link is…”
- “Your recommendation would be more accurate/workable if…”

Writers immediately revise one sentence.

- **Student action:** Partner feedback and visible revision.
- **Finished when:** One sentence is changed and the improvement is identifiable.

### 12. Exit evidence — 2 minutes

Complete:

> If the council ___, then ___ may happen because ___. This is a defensible claim, not a guarantee, because ___.

- **Student action:** Independent.
- **Finished when:** One causal claim plus a boundary.
- **Use:** Sort for the next lesson into secure causal reasoning, evidence-but-no-link and overclaim support groups.

## Optional depth

### Depth A — Design a safety-and-wildlife compromise — 8 minutes

Students combine three guideline principles into a location-specific lighting rule for a path, car park or beachfront business entrance. They must explain how the design serves both safety and turtle protection.

### Depth B — Stress-test the recommendation — 8 minutes

Students add a monitoring clause:

> The council should review the rule if monitoring shows…

They identify what local evidence should be collected and what result could justify revising the policy.

## Model recommendation

> The council should strengthen seasonal beachfront-lighting rules in mapped turtle-sensitive areas while keeping necessary public spaces safely lit. Queensland guidance explains that most nesting and hatching activity occurs at night and that artificial light can disturb nesting turtles and disorient hatchlings. Hatchlings normally use the brighter ocean horizon as a direction cue, so beachfront glow can draw them away from the sea. Therefore, the council should require purpose-specific lights to be shielded, directed, low-intensity and controlled by suitable timers or adaptive systems during locally verified nesting and hatching periods. A beachfront business may reasonably worry that changed lighting could affect safe access and evening trade. However, national guidance recognises human safety needs and calls for creative lighting solutions that meet both safety and wildlife objectives. The light pathway suggests how a council rule can lead to property changes and then to a darker beach horizon, although it cannot prove the exact number of turtles that would be protected. The council should consult the appropriate Traditional Owners, lighting specialists, residents and businesses, then monitor beachward light and turtle behaviour so the rule can be improved.

- Accept careful paraphrase; extended quotations are not required.

## Extension

- Compare a universal “lights off” rule with a targeted, outcomes-based rule.
- Decide which is more defensible for a mixed-use coastline and justify using the national guidelines.
- Add a monitoring trigger and review condition.
- Evaluate whether a chosen visual pathway oversimplifies a feedback loop or cumulative skyglow.

## Accuracy, cultural respect and privacy boundaries

- Do not claim that every artificial light affects every turtle in the same way.
- Do not claim that one rule guarantees survival or population recovery.
- Distinguish direct light, reflected light and collective skyglow where useful.
- Do not present amber or red light as harmless; lower-impact lighting still requires appropriate intensity, placement, shielding and timing.
- Do not treat safety concerns as an excuse to dismiss wildlife protection, or wildlife protection as a reason to ignore statutory safety requirements.
- Do not invent stakeholder quotations.
- Do not ask students to speak for Traditional Owners. Refer to Traditional Owners’ continuing rights, responsibilities and Sea Country management, and the need for direct, culturally appropriate consultation.
- Do not ask students to disclose personal experiences of conflict, cultural identity or community controversy.
