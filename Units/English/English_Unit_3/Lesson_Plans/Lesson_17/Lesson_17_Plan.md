# Lesson 17: The Board of Consequences

## Lesson purpose

Students analyse how Ari's point of view connects opportunity, guilt and responsibility, using chess as an interpretive metaphor rather than claiming the chapter states the metaphor directly.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 - AC9E5LE03: recognise how point of view influences feelings and reader response.**
- **Year 6 - AC9E6LE01: identify responses to characters drawn from social and ethical contexts.**

## Learning intention

We are learning to explain how Ari's first-person viewpoint reveals guilt and to use the chessboard as a defensible metaphor for his choices.

## Success criteria

- I can distinguish Ari's opportunities, choices and consequences.
- I can identify how his narration reveals guilt.
- I can connect a chess feature to an accurate chapter detail.
- I can signal when a metaphor is my interpretation rather than a direct statement by the novel.
- I can write and revise an evidence-based literary analysis.

## Finished workbook response

An 8-10 sentence analysis explaining how the chessboard can represent Ari's choices, guilt and growing responsibility in pages 93-97.

- Teacher controls the projected HTML presentation.

## Core sequence

### 1. Every move changes the board - 4 minutes

Predict one chapter detail that could fit each part of the board.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: prediction row.
- **Finished when:** Three predicted details.
- **Teacher note:** Do not present chess as an explicit metaphor in pages 93-97. It is today's interpretive lens, supported by Ari's recurring chess thinking.

### 2. Turn guilt into an evidence-based interpretation - 2 minutes

Copy the interpretation boundary and complete: The chessboard can represent ___.

- **Student action:** Independent; Organiser: metaphor claim.
- **Finished when:** One cautious metaphor claim.
- **Teacher note:** This protects accuracy while allowing genuine literary interpretation.

### 3. Map Ari's emotional position - 5 minutes

Add one accurate detail to each category and draw the causal arrows.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: position map.
- **Finished when:** Three details and two arrows.
- **Teacher note:** Students may paraphrase. If quoting 'baggage', keep the quotation brief and discuss the metaphor already present in the narration.

### 4. Guilt is not one event—it is accumulated weight - 5 minutes

Build a four-link chain. Label fact from the story and Ari's interpretation differently.

- **Student action:** Independent, then compare; Organiser: consequence chain.
- **Finished when:** Four linked ideas.
- **Teacher note:** Draw on previously read Ari chapters to explain the guilt, while keeping the main analysis anchored in pages 93-97.

### 5. Match the feature by meaning—not appearance - 6 minutes

Place each chess feature, then connect it to one Ari detail.

- **Student action:** Small groups; Organiser: metaphor map.
- **Finished when:** Four placements and one defended connection.
- **Teacher note:** Some connections may be defensibly rearranged with strong reasoning. The interaction supplies a starting map, not a universal code.

### 6. What does first person let the reader carry? - 4 minutes

Explain what would disappear if this scene were told only from outside Ari.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Organiser: viewpoint effect.
- **Finished when:** One evidence-based effect.
- **Teacher note:** This directly addresses the Year 5 point-of-view focus and supports the ethical response required in Year 6.

### 7. Interpret without overclaiming - 5 minutes

Find the inference signal, evidence and metaphor reasoning.

- **Student action:** Whole class; Star one move to imitate.
- **Finished when:** Three analytical moves.
- **Teacher note:** This is an original teacher model. Explain that the later novel makes chess symbolism more explicit, but today's claim remains limited to current reading.

### 8. Set the analysis in motion - 3 minutes

Complete all five planning moves in note form.

- **Student action:** Independent; Organiser: analysis plan.
- **Finished when:** A complete five-move plan.
- **Teacher note:** Conference with students whose chess comparison does not yet clarify Ari's ethical conflict.

### 9. Write the Board of Consequences analysis - 13 minutes

Write the analysis from your completed metaphor map.

- **Student action:** Independent and silent; Facing workbook page.
- **Finished when:** One complete literary analysis.
- **Teacher note:** Students can reject part of the chess metaphor if they explain its limitation and offer a stronger reading.

### 10. Look beyond the next move - 4 minutes

Mark every check and revise one sentence that overclaims or only retells.

- **Student action:** Independent; Workbook analysis.
- **Finished when:** One visible analytical revision.
- **Teacher note:** Use revisions to identify who needs support distinguishing evidence from interpretation.

### 11. One move, one consequence, one insight - 2 minutes

Complete the sentence using one accurate detail and one insight about guilt or responsibility.

- **Student action:** Independent; Workbook.
- **Finished when:** One evidence-to-insight chain.
- **Teacher note:** Safe stopping point. This exit evidence previews readiness for the ethical dilemmas in the next lessons.


## Optional depth slides

### Test a competing metaphor - 8 minutes

Compare the chessboard with Ari's baggage metaphor and judge which is stronger here.

- **Student action:** Pairs; Workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** One comparative judgement.
- **Teacher note:** Optional depth. Students should recognise that 'baggage' is textually explicit while the chessboard is interpretive.

### Predict the next responsible move - 8 minutes

Predict one responsible move for Ari and justify it with his current conflict.

- **Student action:** Independent; Workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** Prediction + evidence-based reason.
- **Teacher note:** Optional depth. Preserve uncertainty and do not confirm later plot outcomes.
