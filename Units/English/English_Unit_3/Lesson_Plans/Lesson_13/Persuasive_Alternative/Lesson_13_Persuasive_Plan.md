# Alternative Lesson 13: Make the Detail Do the Persuading

## Lesson purpose

Students use purposeful expanded noun groups to turn a vague school-improvement idea into a precise, convincing proposal for the principal or student council. This lesson teaches the grammatical concepts from Lesson 13 without using the novel.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 - AC9E5LA06: understand how noun groups can be expanded for description.**
- **Year 6 - AC9E6LA06: understand how ideas can be expanded through choices of verbs and adverbs.**

## Learning intention

We are learning to add precise detail that helps an audience understand and support a persuasive proposal.

## Success criteria

- I can identify the head noun in a noun group.
- I can expand a noun group to specify exactly who, what or which one I mean.
- I can explain how a detail strengthens a persuasive reason.
- I can remove impressive-sounding detail that does not help the argument.
- I can write a 6-8 sentence persuasive proposal with at least three purposeful expanded noun groups.
- I can independently revise one vague or overloaded noun group.

## Finished workbook response

A 6-8 sentence persuasive proposal titled **One Change Our School Should Make**, addressed to the principal or student council.

- **Year 5 evidence:** at least three purposeful expanded noun groups, with the head noun underlined in each.
- **Year 6 evidence:** at least three precise verbs and two purposeful adverbial details that clarify an action, consequence or implementation.
- **Common evidence:** one visible revision and one annotation explaining how a language choice helps persuade the audience.

## Agreed delivery model

- Teacher controls projected examples and class discussion.
- Students complete planning and substantial composition in their English workbooks.
- No knowledge of *Berani* is required.
- Students choose a low-stakes school improvement; personal disclosure is not required.
- Core route: approximately 55 minutes.
- Both optional depth activities may extend the lesson towards 70 minutes.
- Revision is independent first, followed by brief peer testing.

## Teacher knowledge: the grammatical focus

An expanded noun group is built around a **head noun**. Detail can be added before or after it.

> those two neglected garden beds beside the library

- **Head noun:** beds
- **Before the noun:** those, two, neglected, garden
- **After the noun:** beside the library

Expansion is purposeful when it helps the audience identify, picture, distinguish or value the person, place, object or idea. Length alone is not quality.

In persuasive writing, expanded noun groups can:

- identify the people affected: **students who need a calm place at lunch**;
- make a problem precise: **the uncovered metal seats near the oval**;
- make a solution concrete: **three durable shade sails above the eating area**;
- name a benefit clearly: **a safer, more comfortable place to meet**.

Avoid treating every word after a noun as part of the noun group without checking its function. Keep the teaching emphasis on the head noun and the detail attached to it.

## Core sequence

### 1. Which proposal would you support? - 4 minutes

Display:

> A. Our school needs a better area.
>
> B. Our school needs a calm, shaded lunchtime area beside the library.

Students choose the stronger proposal and complete:

> B is easier to support because the added detail tells us ___.

- **Student action:** Think, pair, justify; workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** One choice and one reason.
- **Teacher note:** Do not accept “because it has more adjectives” as the final explanation. Press for what the audience now understands.
- **Misconception revealed:** Longer noun groups are automatically more persuasive.

### 2. Detail must earn its place - 3 minutes

Introduce the lesson test:

> Keep a detail only if it helps the audience identify, understand or value the idea.

Display the noun **area** and build the model **a calm, shaded lunchtime area beside the library**. Underline **area** as the head noun. Ask students what each addition contributes.

- **Student action:** Whole class noticing; copy and label the model.
- **Finished when:** Head noun underlined and two details labelled by job.
- **Teacher note:** Use the terms *head noun* and *expanded noun group*. Explain that detail may come before or after the noun.

### 3. Build three versions for three different jobs - 5 minutes

Start with the head noun **students**. Pairs build one noun group for each job:

1. **Identify:** Which students?
2. **Explain need:** What do they need?
3. **Create value:** Why should the audience care?

Possible teacher models:

- **the students in the crowded eating area**
- **younger students who need a quieter place to sit**
- **every student seeking a safe, welcoming place at lunch**

- **Student action:** Pairs; write three alternatives.
- **Finished when:** Three noun groups with the head noun underlined.
- **Teacher note:** Compare the effects rather than selecting one universal winner. A defensible choice depends on the sentence and purpose.

### 4. Useful detail—or adjective pile? - 5 minutes

Display:

> We need an amazing, wonderful, fantastic, beautiful new space.

Students apply the lesson test. They cross out additions that do not make the proposal more specific, then replace them with useful detail.

Possible revision:

> We need a quiet, weather-protected space near the junior playground.

- **Student action:** Independent edit, then partner comparison.
- **Finished when:** One clean revision and one explanation of a deletion.
- **Teacher note:** Accept different revisions when students can explain what the retained details help the audience know.
- **Misconception revealed:** Positive-sounding adjectives count as evidence or precision.

### 5. Make the reason more exact - 5 minutes

Display the vague reason:

> It would help students.

Model a three-question expansion:

1. **Which students?**
2. **Help them do what?**
3. **When or where would this matter?**

Shared model:

> The quiet lunchtime zone would give students overwhelmed by the noisy oval a calmer place to talk or reset.

Identify:

- noun group: **students overwhelmed by the noisy oval**;
- precise verb: **give**;
- benefit: **a calmer place to talk or reset**.

- **Student action:** Whole class model, then independently improve one second vague reason.
- **Finished when:** One revised reason that names a specific group and benefit.
- **Teacher note:** Make clear that grammatical detail supports a reason; it does not replace the need for a reason.

### 6. Choose a proposal worth improving - 4 minutes

Students choose one of the three prepared prompts or propose another small, appropriate school improvement:

- a quiet lunchtime zone;
- more shaded seating;
- a weekly student-choice activity.

They record:

> Our school should ___ because ___.

Then they identify the real audience: **the principal** or **student council**.

- **Student action:** Independent choice and claim; workbook.
- **Finished when:** One claim, one reason and one named audience.
- **Teacher note:** Approve alternatives quickly. Redirect proposals involving individual complaints, named people, unsafe actions or spending claims students cannot support.

### 7. Plan the people, problem and proposal - 5 minutes

Students create three purposeful expanded noun groups:

| Job | Head noun | Useful expansion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People affected | students / classes / families | Which ones, and what matters to them? |
| Problem now | area / equipment / routine | Which exact problem? |
| Proposed change | zone / seats / activity | What would it actually be like? |

They underline the head noun in each group and write beside it:

> This detail helps my audience ___.

- **Student action:** Independent planning; partner oral rehearsal when helpful.
- **Finished when:** Three noun groups and three brief purpose notes.
- **Teacher note:** Conference first with students whose additions express approval but not precision.

### 8. Read the model like a writer - 5 minutes

Read the whole model before analysing it:

> **A Quiet Place at Lunch**
>
> Our school should create a calm, shaded lunchtime zone beside the library. At present, students who find the crowded oval overwhelming have few comfortable places to pause. A clearly marked quiet area would give these students a safer place to talk, read or reset. Two sturdy benches beneath the existing trees could make the idea practical and easy to supervise. The space would not replace active play; it would give students one additional choice. Please consider trialling this small, inclusive change for four weeks. A more flexible lunchtime environment would help more students return to class ready to learn.

Ask students to find:

1. one noun group that makes the **problem** precise;
2. one noun group that makes the **solution** concrete;
3. one noun group that names a **benefit or value**;
4. one sentence that anticipates a concern.

- **Student action:** Whole class annotated model; star one move to imitate.
- **Finished when:** Three language choices identified and one move starred.
- **Teacher note:** This is an original teacher model. Discuss each choice’s persuasive job before labelling its grammar.

### 9. Write One Change Our School Should Make - 12 minutes

Students write a 6-8 sentence proposal for their chosen audience.

The proposal must include:

- a clear claim;
- a precise description of the current problem or opportunity;
- at least two reasons or benefits;
- a concrete version of the proposed change;
- a respectful call to action;
- at least three purposeful expanded noun groups.

- **Student action:** Independent and silent; workbook.
- **Finished when:** One complete 6-8 sentence persuasive proposal.
- **Teacher note:** Optional sentence launches may remain visible, but students need not follow a fixed formula.

### 10. Audience test and immediate revision - 5 minutes

Partners exchange proposals. The reviewer brackets one noun group and responds using both stems:

> This detail helps me understand ___.
>
> I still need more precise detail about ___.

Writers then revise one noun group. They may add, replace or remove detail.

- **Student action:** Partner feedback, then independent revision.
- **Finished when:** One visible revision prompted by audience feedback.
- **Teacher note:** The reviewer diagnoses clarity; the writer retains control of the final wording.

### 11. Prove that the detail has a job - 2 minutes

Students box their strongest expanded noun group, underline its head noun and complete:

> This is persuasive because it helps the audience ___.

- **Student action:** Independent exit evidence; workbook.
- **Finished when:** One boxed noun group, underlined head noun and explanation.
- **Teacher note:** Use the explanation—not the number of describing words—as the strongest evidence of understanding.

## Optional depth activities

### Change the audience - 10 minutes

Students rewrite one sentence for two audiences:

- the principal, who may focus on feasibility and supervision;
- other students, who may focus on fairness and usefulness.

They then explain which noun-group detail changed and why.

- **Student action:** Independent, then compare.
- **Finished when:** Two versions and one audience explanation.
- **Teacher note:** This extends control and transfer rather than increasing writing volume.

### Cut ten words without weakening the case - 8 minutes

Students exchange one deliberately overloaded sentence or use:

> The extremely wonderful, truly fantastic, very special new lunchtime area would be an absolutely amazing and brilliant improvement for all of our many different students.

They cut at least ten words, add any missing precise detail and justify two changes.

- **Student action:** Pairs; workbook margin.
- **Finished when:** One precise revision and two reasons.
- **Teacher note:** Reward sharper meaning, not merely the largest word reduction.

## Support

- Offer the three prepared proposal topics so topic generation does not consume the lesson.
- Use colour or simple marks to separate the head noun from detail before and after it.
- Provide this optional planning frame:

> Our school should create/change ___.
>
> At present, **[specific people or place]** ___.
>
> **[Concrete proposed change]** would ___.
>
> This would give **[specific group]** ___.
>
> Please consider ___.

- Permit oral rehearsal, speech-to-text, scribing or a shorter 4-5 sentence proposal while preserving the claim, reason and purposeful noun-group decisions.
- Reduce the initial number of language choices; do not reduce the task to copying adjectives into blanks.

## Lucas (ICP)

- Present one shared proposal: **a quiet lunchtime zone**.
- Use three large-print picture-supported choices: **who needs it**, **what it includes**, and **where it could be**.
- Build three noun groups orally or by pointing and arranging phrase cards.
- Permit Lucas to dictate, copy, select or have a response scribed.
- Preserve the conceptual destination: the chosen detail must help an audience understand the proposal.
- Suggested final response:

> Our school should make a quiet lunchtime zone. Students who need a break could sit there. The shaded space beside the library could have two benches. It would give students a calm place to read or talk. Please try this helpful change.

## Extension

- Require students to anticipate one reasonable concern and respond without dismissing the audience.
- Ask students to tailor one noun group for a second audience and explain the change.
- Require a deliberate contrast between one concise noun group and one more fully expanded group.
- Judge precision, audience awareness and control rather than volume.

## Formative assessment

- Opening comparison reveals whether students equate length with quality.
- Head-noun identification checks grammatical understanding.
- The adjective-pile edit reveals whether students can distinguish evaluation from precision.
- The planning table makes the persuasive job of each noun group visible before drafting.
- Peer feedback tests whether the intended audience can identify the people, problem and proposal.
- The exit explanation shows whether students understand why the detail persuades.

## Suggested next teaching response

- **If students cannot identify the head noun:** reteach noun-group boundaries with short oral examples before further composition.
- **If students add only positive adjectives:** contrast evaluative words with details that identify, locate, quantify or explain.
- **If noun groups are precise but arguments remain weak:** teach the difference between elaborating a noun and developing a reason with evidence or consequence.
- **If students overload every noun group:** model sentence rhythm and require them to choose only one place where fuller expansion is worth the reader’s attention.
- **If students show control:** transfer the same language decisions into the unit’s assessed persuasive topic.

## Accuracy and privacy boundaries

- Do not present invented statistics, costs or survey results as evidence.
- Students may make reasonable proposals without claiming that unnamed groups agree with them.
- Avoid requiring disclosure about disability, anxiety, exclusion, family finances or conflict.
- Keep proposals focused on systems, spaces and routines rather than criticism of named people.
- Accept different proposals and language choices when students can explain how the detail serves the audience and purpose.
