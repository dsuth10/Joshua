# Lesson 21: Evaluating Authoritative Sources

## Lesson purpose

Students use Malia’s letter (pp. 125–129) and Ari’s chapter (pp. 131–133) to distinguish a character’s commitment from evidence for a claim. They create and revise a practical evidence-source evaluation checklist for persuasive writing.

## Curriculum focus

- **Year 5 — AC9E5LA02: acknowledge differing ideas and opinions.**
- **Year 6 — AC9E6LA02: identify bias in persuasive texts.**

## Learning intention

We are learning to evaluate whether a source is useful for a persuasive claim and to decide what further checking is needed.

## Success criteria

- I can identify a claim, evidence and limit in a source or speaker.
- I can ask about creator, evidence, purpose/relevance and corroboration.
- I can explain why commitment or confidence alone does not prove a claim.
- I can write a checklist item that tells a writer what to do.
- I can revise a checklist item after feedback.

## Finished product

A four-part evidence-source evaluation checklist plus one applied example from Malia’s or Ari’s chapter.

## Delivery model

- The teacher controls the projected HTML presentation; all DO/WORK/RECORD/FINISH/CHECK logistics stay in its Notes popup.
- Students use the organiser for planning and complete the finished checklist in their English workbooks.
- Core route: 51 minutes. One optional depth slide can extend the lesson.

## Preparation

- Open Lesson_21_Presentation.html in a modern browser and use fullscreen.
- Print the standard or Lucas organiser as required.
- Students need their English workbooks and access to the named *Berani* pages.

## Core sequence

1. **Would you trust it yet? — 4 min.** Generate source-checking questions from two text-connected claims.
2. **Build a source-evaluation checklist — 2 min.** Establish the finished tool.
3. **What does Malia’s letter actually claim? — 5 min.** Separate commitment, claim and limitation.
4. **What does Ari check before deciding? — 5 min.** Identify discovery, uncertainty and consequence.
5. **Four questions before you use a source — 5 min.** Develop creator, evidence, fit/purpose and corroboration lenses.
6. **Which evidence is ready to use? — 6 min.** Sort text-based source judgements and retry.
7. **Turn a judgement into a checklist item — 5 min.** Analyse an explicit model.
8. **Create your evidence-source checklist — 10 min.** Independently build four actionable checks and apply one.
9. **Make one check sharper — 4 min.** Peer feedback and immediate revision.
10. **Is this a usable evaluation tool? — 3 min.** Self-check.
11. **One claim. One next check. — 2 min.** Exit evidence.

## Optional depth

**When two sources disagree — 7 min.** Students list the specific further evidence they would need before drawing a conclusion. The task practises inquiry; it does not require students to settle the issue.

## Support

- Give the four lens headings first, then allow oral rehearsal before writing.
- Offer stems: “Before I use this source, I will check ___” and “This needs checking because ___.”
- Reduce the sort to two cards initially and read the cards aloud.

## Lucas (ICP)

- Use the large-print organiser with familiar, text-connected ideas: kind, worried, help and safe.
- Preserve the destination: identify a helpful claim and choose one check before sharing it.
- Lucas may point, speak, copy or use a partner scribe.

## Formative assessment

The sort exposes the misconception that passionate language, a familiar adult or an online result automatically proves a claim. The exit sentence shows whether a student can name a relevant next check and justify it.

## Teacher reading fallback

Malia’s apology letter accepts responsibility for her actions while maintaining that she will continue to learn about environmental awareness and the impact of palm oil agriculture on orangutans. Ari reports finding rescue organisations through an online search, notes facts he does not know about Ginger Juice, and weighs consequences for his family, school and Uncle.

## Accuracy and discussion boundaries

- Use only verified details from *Berani* and the approved sequence.
- Avoid asking students to disclose personal family experience, political views or animal-harm experiences.
- Do not treat any source as authoritative merely because it appears official; evaluate evidence, purpose, relevance and corroboration.
