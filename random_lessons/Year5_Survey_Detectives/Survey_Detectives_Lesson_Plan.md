# Survey Detectives: Two Questions, One Finding

**Year level:** Year 5  
**Learning area:** Mathematics — data investigation  
**Duration:** 75–90 minutes  
**Class:** 19 students  
**Digital tool used by students:** Microsoft Forms  
**Recommended grouping:** eight pairs and one trio, producing nine forms

## Lesson outcome

Students formulate a question about a possible relationship between two preferences or behaviours, build one Microsoft Form containing two linked survey questions, collect anonymous class responses, organise the paired answers in a two-way table, and write a cautious evidence-based conclusion.

The central conceptual move is:

> A relationship cannot be found from two separate totals. The two answers from each respondent must stay linked as a pair.

## Learning intention

We are learning to design and analyse a two-question survey so that we can test whether two class responses appear to be related.

## Success criteria

Students can:

- write a big question that genuinely needs two pieces of information;
- turn it into two neutral, answerable survey questions;
- keep both answers linked in one anonymous form submission;
- classify each response pair into a two-way table;
- compare the relevant counts or percentages;
- state a finding supported by evidence and describe at least one limit.

## Teacher preparation

1. Open `Survey_Detectives_Presentation.html` in a modern browser.
2. Decide how students will access the nine team forms: a shared page, QR-code sheet, Teams post or similar class-only location.
3. Confirm that students can create a Microsoft Form, add Choice questions and view responses.
4. Have calculators or spreadsheet access available for percentages.
5. If a single session is short, use the natural pause after teams finish building and testing their forms.

## Synthetic teacher demonstration

The demonstration question is:

> Do students who enjoy sport tend to enjoy Maths, or not enjoy Maths?

The demonstration form contains:

1. **Do you enjoy playing or watching sport?** — Yes / No
2. **Do you enjoy Maths?** — Yes / No

These are synthetic results generated for this lesson. They are not information about the real class.

|                       | Maths: Yes | Maths: No | Total |
|-----------------------|-----------:|----------:|------:|
| Sport: Yes            | 8          | 4         | 12    |
| Sport: No             | 3          | 4         | 7     |
| **Total**             | **11**     | **8**     | **19** |

For the 12 students in the synthetic sample who enjoyed sport:

- 8 of 12, about 67%, also enjoyed Maths;
- 4 of 12, about 33%, did not enjoy Maths.

### Model conclusion

Our generated data does not support the idea that students who enjoy sport tend to dislike Maths. Of the 12 synthetic respondents who enjoyed sport, 8 (67%) also enjoyed Maths and 4 (33%) did not. In this sample, enjoying both was more common than enjoying sport but not Maths. This was a synthetic sample of only 19 responses, so it cannot describe all Year 5 students or prove that one preference causes the other.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. Compelling entry and prediction — 5 minutes

Use slides 1–2.

- Present the sport-and-Maths question before discussing Forms.
- Students choose one prediction: sport-enjoyers will be more likely to enjoy Maths, not enjoy Maths, or split about equally.
- Ask for reasons, but do not evaluate them yet.

**Teacher observes:** whether students distinguish a prediction from evidence.

### 2. Establish the evidence problem — 8 minutes

Use slides 3–5.

- Clarify the investigation arc: ask, collect, compare, conclude.
- Show why the sport question alone and the Maths question alone cannot reveal a relationship.
- Explicitly model one form with two questions, rather than two separate forms.
- Emphasise that anonymous answers are still linked inside the same response row.

**Likely misconception:** two separate summary charts can be compared to discover the relationship.

**Response:** show that separate totals do not identify which Maths answer belongs with each sport answer.

### 3. Model fair survey construction — 8 minutes

Use slides 6–7.

- Compare the biased and neutral question pairs.
- In Microsoft Forms, demonstrate creating one form, adding two Choice questions, making both required and sharing only with the class.
- Do not ask for names.

Useful teacher language:

> I am not collecting two piles of answers. I am collecting 19 pairs of answers.

### 4. Analyse the synthetic data — 15 minutes

Use slides 8–12.

- Remind students that the dataset is generated, not real class information.
- Reveal the 19 response rows.
- Students tally the four possible answer combinations before the on-screen counts are revealed.
- Read across the **Sport: Yes** row because that is the group named in the question.
- Calculate 8 ÷ 12 and 4 ÷ 12. Calculators are appropriate.

**Teacher observes:**

- whether students count pairs rather than separate columns;
- whether students choose 12, not 19, as the denominator for a sentence about sport-enjoyers;
- whether students compare the relevant groups.

### 5. Choose and construct the conclusion — 8 minutes

Use slides 13–14.

- Students select the strongest of three possible conclusions and defend it with a number.
- Reject causal language: the survey shows an association in a sample, not that sport causes a Maths preference.
- Model the four conclusion jobs: claim, evidence, interpretation and limit.

### 6. Student question design — 10 minutes

Use slides 15–17.

1. Students draft one relationship question independently.
2. Teams choose or improve one question.
3. If stuck, they select one of the ten questions in the on-screen bank.
4. Teams write the two exact survey questions and response options before opening Microsoft Forms.

### 7. Build and quality-check forms — 10 minutes

Use slide 18.

Teams must pass all six checks:

- the big question needs both answers;
- each survey question asks one clear thing;
- wording is neutral;
- both questions are required and options are clear;
- no names or private information are requested;
- the form has been tested once.

### 8. Collect the class sample — 10–15 minutes

Use slide 19.

- Number the nine team forms.
- Place all links or QR codes in one shared class-only location.
- Each student responds once to each form without discussing answers.
- Each team closes collection when it reaches 19 complete responses.

If time is limited, collect 10–12 responses and complete the full class sample next session. Students must state the smaller sample size in their conclusion.

### 9. Analyse and answer — 12 minutes

Use slides 20–21.

- Forms summary charts may help with single-question totals, but students need paired response rows for the relationship.
- Students open responses in Excel if available, or inspect/tally the individual response pairs manually.
- Teams make a four-group table, compare the relevant counts or percentages, and write a three-sentence conclusion:
  1. finding;
  2. numerical evidence;
  3. limitation.

### 10. Exit evidence — 4 minutes

Use slides 21–22.

Collect one conclusion per team. Students also identify what their team still needs:

- fairer wording;
- complete responses;
- a correct table;
- stronger evidence; or
- a clearer limitation.

## Ten fallback investigation questions

Each can be answered with two Yes/No questions. The exact survey wording is available interactively on slide 16.

1. Are strategy-game fans more likely to enjoy Maths?
2. Do team-sport fans tend to prefer group learning?
3. Are frequent readers more likely to enjoy writing?
4. Do creative-subject fans prefer open-ended tasks?
5. Are students who enjoy Science more likely to enjoy Maths?
6. Do students who choose active break games tend to enjoy HPE?
7. Are planners more likely to feel confident starting assignments?
8. Do students helped by worked examples prefer independent practice?
9. Do puzzle fans enjoy tasks that take more than one attempt?
10. Do fiction fans prefer imaginative writing?

## Safety and privacy boundary

Students must not ask for:

- names or information that makes classmates identifiable;
- family income, religion, health information or diagnoses;
- embarrassing experiences;
- passwords, account details or contact information;
- any topic a respondent could reasonably feel pressured to reveal.

If a topic feels unsafe or overly personal, the student chooses another topic without needing to explain why.

## Differentiation

### Support

- Start with one of the ten prepared question pairs.
- Keep both response options binary: Yes / No.
- Provide the four fixed table headings before students tally.
- Use the conclusion frame on slide 21.
- Allow oral rehearsal before writing.
- Calculate with counts first; treat percentages as an optional second representation.

### Extension

- Compare the within-group percentages for both rows, not just raw counts.
- Explain why unequal group sizes can make raw-count comparisons misleading.
- Identify a possible confounding factor without claiming it caused the result.
- Redesign one Yes/No question as a four-point scale, then explain how this makes analysis richer and more complex.

## Assessment evidence

The team’s final product should contain:

1. the big relationship question;
2. the exact two survey questions and response options;
3. the number of complete responses;
4. a labelled two-way table;
5. at least one correct comparison;
6. a conclusion containing a finding, evidence and limitation.

The most diagnostic error is a conclusion based only on two separate totals. That indicates the student has not yet understood paired response data.
