# Comprehension Web Question-Writing Guide

## Purpose of this guide

This is the authoring standard for question sets in the Literacy Comprehension Web App. Use it when creating, adapting, reviewing, or marking activities for:

- **Inferencing** — finding meaning that is implied rather than directly stated.
- **Reorganisation** — joining, grouping, sequencing, filtering, or calculating with information that is stated in different parts of a text.
- **Evaluation** — combining stated information, reorganisation, and inference to interpret significance, writer intent, nuance, or a text-based judgement.
- **Reaction** — making a personal judgement that is justified from the particular text. Reaction is treated as the most demanding form of evaluation.

The aim is not simply to ask questions about a passage. Each item must deliberately assess one comprehension process, contain enough textual evidence for a defensible answer, and work reliably in the app's open-response and one-mark format.

---

## 1. Start with the thinking, not the question wording

Before writing a prompt, identify four things:

1. **Target skill:** What mental operation must the student perform?
2. **Evidence:** Which exact words, sentences, or relationships in the text make the answer possible?
3. **Expected answer:** What is the shortest complete answer that demonstrates the skill?
4. **Acceptance boundary:** Which reasonable alternatives should receive the mark, and which tempting answers should not?

Use this planning sentence:

> The student must **[thinking operation]** using **[clue 1]** and **[clue 2]** to conclude **[answer]**.

Example:

> The student must **infer the time of day** using **breakfast** and **catch the bus** to conclude **morning/before school**.

If this sentence cannot be completed precisely, the item is not ready. The passage may need another clue, the question may be too broad, or the intended skill may be misclassified.

### The evidence sufficiency test

A good item passes all four checks:

- The answer is supported by the supplied text, not hidden background knowledge.
- The answer is not copied verbatim when the target is inferencing or evaluation.
- A reasonable reader can identify the intended evidence.
- The evidence supports one clear conclusion or a deliberately defined range of acceptable conclusions.

General knowledge may help a reader connect clues, but it must not become a trivia test. For example, “breakfast + school bus” reasonably supports “morning”; asking for an obscure historical date not supplied by the text does not assess comprehension.

## 2. Distinguish the comprehension skills

The same passage can support different question types. Classification depends on what the student must do, not on whether the prompt begins with *what*, *why*, or *how*.

| Skill | Where is the answer? | Required operation | Useful diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Literal recall | In one explicit statement | Locate or copy | Can the answer be lifted from one place? |
| Reorganisation | Explicitly stated across two or more elements | Join, group, sequence, eliminate, resolve a referent, or calculate | Must the student combine stated details? |
| Inferencing | Implied by textual clues | Connect clues with ordinary knowledge to make the implicit explicit | Is the conclusion unstated but strongly signalled? |
| Evaluation | Suggested by the text as a whole or by the writer's choices | Reorganise, infer, analyse significance, intent, plausibility, or nuance | Must the student go beyond a single inference and judge what the information means? |
| Reaction | In the student's judgement, constrained by the text | Form a position and justify it with text evidence | Could more than one position earn the mark if supported? |

Literal questions can occasionally support a sequence, but they should not dominate a set intended to assess a higher-order skill.

### One text, four different demands

Text:

> Mia zipped her raincoat, tucked the library book under it, and ran from the gate while thunder rolled overhead.

- **Literal:** What did Mia tuck under her raincoat?  
  The answer is stated in one place.
- **Reorganisation:** What two things did Mia do before she ran from the gate?  
  The student groups two stated actions.
- **Inference:** Why did Mia put the book under her raincoat?  
  The student connects the raincoat, thunder, and protected book to infer that she expected rain and wanted to keep it dry.
- **Evaluation:** What does Mia's treatment of the book suggest about her attitude towards borrowed property? Explain.  
  The student interprets the significance of her action and supports a judgement.

## 3. Writing inferencing questions

### What inferencing assesses

Inference is the translation of implicit information into a clear, literal conclusion. The student finds cue words or details, connects them, and uses reasonable everyday knowledge.

A well-designed inference has this shape:

> textual clue(s) + ordinary knowledge = implied conclusion

App example:

> “It was time to turn the lights on.”  
> **Question:** What time of the day was it likely to be?  
> **Reasoning:** Lights are needed as natural light fades, so it was evening, dusk, or getting dark.

### Suitable inference targets

Ask students to infer:

- setting or location;
- time or sequence;
- identity, role, or relationship;
- cause and effect;
- motive or intention;
- emotion or attitude;
- what happened before or between stated events;
- what is likely to happen next;
- the meaning of a word, phrase, idiom, simile, metaphor, or referent in context;
- the difference between appearance and reality;
- a character trait supported by behaviour.

### Strong app examples

**Location from action**

> “Jane pushed the button and waited for the lights to change.”  
> **Question:** Where was Jane?  
> **Evidence path:** button + changing lights → pedestrian crossing.

**Identity from procedure**

> “After motioning me to place my suitcase and hand luggage on the counter, he methodically examined the contents.”  
> **Question:** Who was the man?  
> **Evidence path:** luggage + inspection counter → customs or airport security officer.

**Emotion from behaviour**

> Wade wanted to show his grandfather the healthy piglet he had raised and looked as though he would burst into tears when the driver refused him entry.  
> **Question:** Was Wade proud of his piglet? How do you know?  
> **Complete answer:** Yes. He had carefully raised it and wanted to show it to his grandfather.

**Figurative meaning**

> A tennis champion struggles repeatedly but survives each match.  
> **Question:** Why is he described as having “as many lives as a cat”?  
> **Complete answer:** He repeatedly comes close to losing but recovers and stays in the competition.

### Inferencing design rules

- Include at least one identifiable clue; use two or more when the answer would otherwise depend on guessing.
- Do not state the answer in another sentence. If students can copy it, the item is literal.
- Avoid clues so broad that many unrelated answers become equally likely.
- Ask for the inference directly. Prefer “Why did…?”, “Where was…?”, “What does this suggest…?”, or “What had most likely happened?”
- Use *likely*, *probably*, or *most likely* when the text supports probability rather than certainty.
- When asking about an emotion or trait, require evidence from behaviour rather than accepting an unsupported label.
- Do not require students to share a cultural experience that is not explained or commonly accessible.

### Open and closed inferencing prompts

Prefer open questions:

> Why could Anne not eat any more?

Use a closed question only when yes/no is the clearest way to focus attention. Always add an evidence demand:

> Was Mandy in the bath for a long time? How do you know?

Do not write only “Was Mandy in the bath for a long time?” A student could guess *yes* without demonstrating comprehension.

## 4. Writing reorganisation questions

### What reorganisation assesses

Reorganisation reconstructs two or more explicit pieces of information into a complete answer. The pieces may be separated, out of sequence, expressed through pronouns, mixed with irrelevant details, or require a simple calculation.

The defining feature is that the required information is stated. The challenge is manipulating it.

### Reorganisation subtypes and codes

Some existing source questions carry codes such as `J`, `JR`, `G`, `GR`, and `GC`. These are authoring labels inherited from the source materials. They are useful for checking variety but are not student instructions.

| Code | Type | What the student does | Example target |
|---|---|---|---|
| `J` | Joined | Joins separated related details | Connect a person to a stated role or reason |
| `JR` | Joined with referent | Joins details and resolves a pronoun or substitute word | Decide what *it*, *they*, or *this* refers to |
| `G` | Grouped | Collects a list or sequence | List several actions, places, or reasons |
| `GR` | Grouped with referent | Collects details while resolving one or more referents | List what several pronouns refer to |
| `GE` | Grouped with elimination | Collects relevant details and excludes distractors | Select only the animals a character actually saw |
| `GC` | Grouped with calculation | Collects stated quantities and calculates | Determine elapsed time, number of people, cost, or distance |

Existing files do not use every code consistently. Classify the thinking accurately first; add a code only when the set's convention calls for it.

### Reorganisation targets

**Joined information**

> Todd thanked his grandparents for a gift. The gift was a football jersey.  
> **Question:** What gift did Todd get from his grandparents?  
> The student connects *gift* across the sentences.

**Grouped information**

> Claire was born in Malaysia, studied in Thailand and England, and taught in Ireland and Japan.  
> **Question:** In which countries has Claire lived?  
> The student gathers five separated places.

**Grouped with calculation**

> Brazil won every four-yearly World Cup from 1958 to 1970 except the 1966 tournament.  
> **Question:** In which years did Brazil win?  
> The student generates the sequence 1958, 1962, 1966, 1970 and eliminates 1966.

**Conversation and speaker tracking**

> “Where have you put my pen?” said Terry.  
> “I haven't used it,” Abby replied.  
> “Will you help me search for it?”  
> “Okay.”  
> **Question:** Who said “Okay”?  
> The student follows turn-taking to identify Abby.

**Referent resolution**

> “A sympathetic co-worker approached Fred. ‘Why don't you go and lie down?’”  
> **Question:** Who suggested that Fred lie down?  
> The student connects the unnamed speaker to *a sympathetic co-worker*.

### Reorganisation design rules

- Require at least two textual elements. Otherwise the question is literal recall.
- Separate related information naturally; do not create awkward prose solely to hide details.
- For grouped questions, define the scope: “Which countries…?”, “What three reasons…?”, or “List the animals Ashley asked to see.”
- For calculation, keep the arithmetic subordinate to reading. The assessment is comprehension, not advanced mathematics.
- Make units and time frames clear.
- For elimination, include plausible but distinguishable distractors in the passage, then word the scope precisely.
- For referents, ensure there is one defensible parent noun. Ambiguous pronouns create a writing flaw, not a valid challenge.
- For dialogue, format speaker changes clearly and ensure the student can follow the conversation without relying on voice or performance.

## 5. Writing evaluation questions

### What evaluation assesses

Evaluation sits above a single inference. The student reorganises relevant information, interprets its implications, and forms a text-supported judgement about significance, intent, effectiveness, plausibility, attitude, or nuance.

Evaluation does **not** mean asking for any opinion. A valid answer must be anchored in the supplied text.

### Suitable evaluation targets

Ask students to evaluate:

- why the writer chose a particular word, comparison, title, or detail;
- what a statement reveals about an attitude, value, or viewpoint;
- why an outcome is surprising, important, unfair, effective, or problematic;
- whether a claim or conclusion is supported by the passage;
- the likely appeal, effect, advantage, or limitation of an idea;
- competing explanations using several pieces of information;
- the significance of contrast, irony, tone, or omission;
- how a character's perspective changes;
- which interpretation is most convincing and why.

### Strong app examples

**Significance of a writer's word**

> The 1918 flu killed an estimated 40 million people across much of the globe, yet scientists waited forty years to investigate the virus.  
> **Question:** Why does the writer describe the delay as “surprising”?  
> **Evidence path:** enormous worldwide death toll + forty-year delay → such a serious event would be expected to prompt earlier investigation.

**Evaluating appeal and limitation**

> A self-cleaning house uses waterproof surfaces, jets of water, hot air, and stark concrete design.  
> **Question:** Why might the house not appeal to some homeowners?  
> **Complete answer:** Some may find its stark concrete surfaces unattractive or may not want the major lifestyle changes its design requires.

**Interpreting contrast**

> Jaz calls missed opportunities and an embarrassing fall proof of a terrible life, then sees Elyse writing easily about a recent car accident.  
> **Question:** Do you think Jaz has had a terrible life? Why do you say that?  
> **Complete answer:** Probably not. Most of her examples are temporary disappointments, especially when contrasted with Elyse's serious injuries.

**Technical evaluation**

> Brake fade is caused by heat; disc brakes have greater air ventilation than drum brakes.  
> **Question:** Why are disc brakes more effective in resisting brake fade?  
> **Complete answer:** Their ventilation allows heat to dissipate, reducing the overheating that causes brake fade.

### Evaluation design rules

- Build the question around two or more relevant details or a meaningful writer choice.
- Make the judgement criterion visible: surprising, effective, fair, convincing, appropriate, appealing, or significant.
- Require explanation. “Do you agree?” alone is insufficient.
- Ensure a strong answer can cite or paraphrase the text.
- Do not reward unsupported personal preference.
- If several positions are defensible, define what evidence makes each acceptable in the marking guide.
- Avoid pretending there is open debate when the text clearly supports only one position. In that case, ask for the supported interpretation directly.
- Do not let specialised subject knowledge replace evaluation of the text.

## 6. Writing reaction questions

Reaction combines reorganisation, inference, and evaluation with a personal position. It is a subtype of evaluation and should be used sparingly, generally after students have already processed the passage.

### Essential rule

The response must be about **this text and this situation**, not merely the student's prior preferences.

Weak:

> Do you like walking?

Strong:

> Would you use the easy walking trails described in the passage? Why do you say that?

Both *yes* and *no* may earn the mark:

- **Yes:** they are healthy, inexpensive, pleasurable, close to town, non-competitive, or allow people to set their own pace.
- **No:** the popularity might mean crowds, or the easy and non-competitive design may offer too little challenge.

The position is not marked as right or wrong. The mark is for a coherent position supported by a relevant feature of the text.

### Reaction design rules

- Name the exact proposal, decision, character action, or situation being judged.
- Follow the position prompt with “Why?” or “Use evidence from the text.”
- Write acceptance criteria for more than one defensible position.
- Reject answers based only on unrelated personal experience.
- Avoid sensitive disclosure prompts. Students should not need to reveal trauma, health, family circumstances, or beliefs to demonstrate comprehension.
- Place reaction after foundational questions so students first examine the text objectively.

## 7. Writing the texts students read

Questions can only be as valid as the text that supports them. In this app, a reading text is not a neutral container written first and questioned later. The passage and its questions form one designed assessment unit:

> **text content + evidence placement + question wording + marking threshold = the comprehension task**

A polished story can still make a poor comprehension activity if the intended answers are under-supported. A plain paragraph can make an excellent activity if it contains purposeful, natural evidence and requires the intended kind of thinking. Write for both readability and assessability.

### 7.1 Begin with a text brief

Complete a brief before drafting prose. This prevents the passage from becoming interesting but unusable, or becoming so mechanically built around answers that it no longer reads naturally.

```text
Skill: Inferencing / Reorganisation / Evaluation
Level: 1 / 2 / 3
Form: Narrative / information / explanation / dialogue / mixed
Handout position: Quick item / short passage / extended text
Topic and purpose:
Intended reader assumptions:
Central situation or main idea:
Target comprehension opportunities:
Required explicit facts:
Required implicit clues:
Referents, chronology, or contrasts to control:
Knowledge that must be explained in the text:
Potential ambiguity or sensitivity:
Approximate length and number of questions:
```

The **target comprehension opportunities** are the reasons this particular text belongs in the activity. For example:

- infer a setting from objects and actions;
- join a person's travel history across three paragraphs;
- resolve who is speaking in an interrupted conversation;
- evaluate why a writer calls an outcome surprising;
- judge whether a proposal meets a stated group's needs.

Aim for more usable opportunities than the final activity needs. During review, remove weak or repetitive questions rather than forcing every planned detail into the set.

### 7.2 Choose content that is readable, fair, and worth thinking about

Good comprehension texts give students a reason to construct meaning. They need a small problem, change, contrast, explanation, discovery, decision, or relationship.

Suitable foundations include:

- a character trying to solve a practical problem;
- an event whose cause is revealed indirectly;
- a conversation with a clear purpose and changing speaker turns;
- a process with steps, conditions, and consequences;
- an object, animal, place, invention, or phenomenon explained through connected facts;
- two options with different benefits and limitations;
- a claim followed by evidence and qualification;
- a description in which word choice conveys mood or attitude.

Avoid texts that are only a loose list of facts unless grouping or classification is the deliberate target. Avoid plots that depend on coincidence, missing information, or a character behaving irrationally only to create a question.

#### Topic and knowledge fairness

- The text must supply any specialist knowledge needed for the answer.
- Ordinary world knowledge may connect clues, but it should not determine success by itself.
- Explain technical terms in context, through definition, example, contrast, or cause and effect.
- Do not assume that all students share the same family structure, holidays, housing, transport, pets, sport, religion, or cultural practices.
- Use Australian settings and vocabulary naturally where appropriate, but explain any regional term whose meaning is needed.
- Avoid requiring personal disclosure about trauma, health, family finances, identity, or belief.
- Represent people and cultures respectfully; do not use stereotypes as inferential shortcuts.
- Check that names, roles, and pronouns allow characters to be tracked without signalling that one type of person always behaves in one way.

#### Originality and source use

Prefer original passages written for the activity. If adapting factual material, verify it against a reliable source and rewrite it in an age-appropriate, cohesive form. Record the source used for factual checking. Do not reproduce protected stories or articles merely because they are available online. If a supplied text must remain unchanged, design questions only from evidence genuinely present in that version.

### 7.3 Build an evidence architecture before polishing

An evidence architecture is a map showing what the passage states, what it implies, how details connect, and which question each detail can support.

#### Step A: Create a fact ledger

List every fact the passage must state explicitly.

Example brief: a student misses a bus because she returns home for an assignment.

```text
F1: Leila waits beside the route map.
F2: She notices the assignment is not in her bag.
F3: She runs back through her front gate.
F4: She returns to find the road empty.
F5: Her watch shows the bus is five minutes past its usual time.
```

These facts can support literal or reorganisation questions. They also act as clues for inferences.

#### Step B: Create an inference ledger

For every unstated conclusion, list the clues and ordinary knowledge required.

| Intended conclusion | Textual clues | Ordinary connecting knowledge | Strength check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leila was at a bus stop | route map, waiting, bus's usual time | buses stop at marked places with route information | Strong |
| She went home | ran back through her front gate | a person's front gate commonly marks their home | Strong if ownership is clear |
| She missed the bus | road empty, bus five minutes late | the bus has already passed | Strong |

If a conclusion depends on only a vague clue, strengthen the text. If the conclusion is actually stated, it is no longer an inference target.

#### Step C: Create a relationship map

Record details that must be joined:

- people and their roles;
- pronouns and parent nouns;
- causes and later effects;
- objects and their changing locations;
- time references and elapsed periods;
- members of groups and items to exclude;
- claims, supporting evidence, and qualifications;
- speaker turns and interruptions.

This is especially important for reorganisation and evaluation texts. It exposes accidental ambiguity before students encounter it.

#### Step D: Create an answerability table

| Proposed question | Required details | Where details appear | Intended operation | Possible competing answer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Why did Leila miss the bus? | F2, F3, F4 | sentences 2–4 | Join cause and outcome | She waited too long |

The competing answer in this example may also be plausible. The author must decide whether to accept it, revise the question, or clarify timing in the passage.

### 7.4 Control clue strength and redundancy

Students should have to think, but they should not have to guess. Control clue strength deliberately.

#### Anchor clues

An anchor clue points strongly towards the intended meaning: a route map and timetable for a bus stop; a boarding pass and departure screen for an airport; smoke entering a room for a nearby fire.

#### Supporting clues

Supporting clues confirm or refine the anchor: waiting at the kerb, checking a watch, or hearing an approaching engine.

#### Discriminating clues

A discriminating clue rules out a plausible alternative. If “counter + suitcase inspection” might mean hotel reception or airport security, mention that the traveller has just arrived from overseas to support *customs officer*.

#### Natural redundancy

Use enough clues to make the inference fair, but distribute them naturally. Repeating the same idea three times does not create deeper comprehension.

Weak over-signalling:

> Kai was freezing. He was very cold. His teeth chattered because the temperature was low.

Better:

> Kai tucked his hands into his sleeves. Each breath clouded in front of him, and frost silvered the grass beside the track.

The better version provides several concrete clues without naming the conclusion.

#### Deliberate distractors

Distractors are details that are present but irrelevant to a particular answer. They are useful for grouped-with-elimination and higher-level evaluation, but must be meaningful parts of the text. Do not add random names, numbers, or events solely to trick students.

### 7.5 Write cohesive prose, not a disguised answer sheet

Evidence should be carried by a coherent text. Use cohesion deliberately:

- repeat a key noun when a pronoun would be ambiguous;
- use pronouns when the parent noun remains recoverable;
- connect causes and consequences with appropriate conjunctions, while sometimes separating them for a valid reorganisation demand;
- keep paragraph boundaries meaningful;
- introduce people and objects before referring to them indirectly;
- maintain tense unless a purposeful time shift is clearly signalled;
- make changes of speaker visible through new paragraphs and dialogue punctuation;
- use temporal markers such as *earlier*, *meanwhile*, *two days later*, and *by then* according to level;
- ensure descriptive details contribute to setting, character, mood, or evidence.

Do not distort natural syntax simply to separate two facts. Reorganisation should arise from authentic text structure, not from deliberately confusing sentences.

### 7.6 Use length as one control, not the definition of level

The app does not enforce passage word counts. These planning bands are starting points for new material, not pass/fail rules:

| Handout component | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Quick sentence or paragraph | about 8–35 words | about 15–55 words | about 20–80 words |
| Short passage | about 50–120 words | about 80–180 words | about 110–240 words |
| Extended text | about 180–350 words | about 250–500 words | about 300–650 words |

A short text can be difficult because its meaning is compressed or figurative. A longer text can be easy because it is chronological, repetitive, and concrete. Judge load across all of these dimensions:

- word familiarity;
- sentence length and clause density;
- distance between related clues;
- number of characters, objects, places, and times to track;
- directness of causal links;
- number and ambiguity of referents;
- text structure and chronology;
- abstractness of the topic;
- figurative or technical language;
- number of plausible interpretations;
- amount of evidence students must select and combine.

### 7.7 General text design for Level 1

Level 1 introduces the target skill without making decoding, memory, or text navigation the main obstacle.

#### Language and sentence design

- Prefer familiar, concrete vocabulary.
- Use mostly simple and compound sentences, with occasional short complex sentences.
- Keep subjects and verbs easy to identify.
- Explain an unfamiliar word immediately through context.
- Use figurative language only when nearby clues make its meaning accessible.
- Keep negatives straightforward; avoid stacked forms such as “not unlikely”.

#### Structure and cohesion

- Use chronological order for narratives.
- Keep the main idea or problem visible.
- Limit the number of active characters, usually to two or three in a short passage.
- Keep most referents close to their parent nouns.
- Place evidence within the same sentence or adjacent sentences for early items.
- Use clear paragraph breaks and explicit speaker labels when dialogue begins.

#### Evidence and answer design

- Build one main reasoning link per quick item.
- Use concrete behavioural clues for feelings and motives.
- Ensure one answer is clearly most likely.
- For reorganisation, join two nearby facts or track a simple exchange.
- For evaluation, use an obvious contrast or consequence and ask for a bounded judgement.
- Ask questions in passage order.

#### Level 1 mini-example

> Ben set two bowls beside the back door. He filled one with water and shook dry food into the other. A lead hung from the hook above them. When he called, something scratched excitedly at the laundry door.

Designed opportunities:

- **Inference:** What animal was Ben most likely caring for?  
  *Clues:* two bowls + dry food + lead + scratching → dog.
- **Reorganisation:** What two things did Ben put in the bowls?  
  *Details:* water + dry food.
- **Evaluation:** Which detail gives the strongest evidence that the animal was eager to see Ben? Why?  
  *Evidence:* excited scratching after he called.

The passage never names the dog, but it supplies converging, familiar clues. It contains no exotic breed knowledge or ambiguous second animal.

#### Avoid at Level 1

- unexplained irony or sarcasm;
- several unnamed speakers;
- long flashbacks;
- subtle shifts in viewpoint;
- multiple equally plausible motives;
- calculation mixed with complex language;
- large lists that overload memory;
- questions requiring evidence from opposite ends of a long text.

### 7.8 General text design for Level 2

Level 2 increases the amount of integration. Students should handle separated clues, less direct wording, and more than one relationship while the text remains fair and cohesive.

#### Language and sentence design

- Combine simple, compound, and complex sentences.
- Introduce some lower-frequency or technical vocabulary with contextual support.
- Use a manageable idiom, simile, or descriptive phrase whose meaning can be inferred.
- Use varied sentence openings and embedded detail without hiding the main action.
- Allow a negative or qualification to affect meaning, provided it is not syntactically confusing.

#### Structure and cohesion

- Separate related evidence across a paragraph or across nearby paragraphs.
- Use three or four trackable characters where the task benefits from it.
- Include a brief interruption, change of plan, or shift in time.
- Use several pronouns, with context sufficient to resolve them.
- For information texts, connect definition, example, cause, effect, and comparison.
- Include relevant distractors for grouping or evaluation.

#### Evidence and answer design

- Require two or more clues for significant inferences.
- Allow an initial interpretation that later evidence corrects or refines.
- Use grouped-with-elimination or a simple calculation after details have been collected.
- Support evaluation through a clear benefit–limitation, claim–evidence, or expectation–outcome pattern.
- Permit limited answer variation but define the core idea.

#### Level 2 mini-example

> The community garden's new water tank filled quickly during the spring storms. By January, however, only a muddy puddle remained at its base. The gardeners had planted extra tomatoes, and a loose tap had dripped unnoticed behind the shed for several weeks. Council restrictions meant they could not refill the tank from the mains supply.

Designed opportunities:

- **Reorganisation:** What two things caused the stored water to run out?  
  *Details:* extra plants used more water + the tap leaked.
- **Inference:** Why would the gardeners need to change their plans in January?  
  *Clues:* empty tank + no mains refill → insufficient water for the existing planting plan.
- **Evaluation:** Which problem should the gardeners address first, and why?  
  *Defensible answer:* repair the loose tap because it wastes limited water; other answers require equally direct textual justification.

The text contains a seasonal shift, two causes, and one external constraint. It is more demanding than Level 1 without relying on obscure knowledge.

#### Avoid at Level 2

- increasing every difficulty dimension at once;
- specialist knowledge not defined in context;
- referents that remain grammatically ambiguous;
- a twist that invalidates earlier questions;
- opinion questions without textual criteria;
- long passages containing only one usable evidence path.

### 7.9 General text design for Level 3

Level 3 requires sustained integration, selection, and interpretation. Complexity should come from meaningful relationships, not poor editing.

#### Language and sentence design

- Use a natural range of sentence lengths and clause structures.
- Include abstract, technical, or figurative language supported by the passage.
- Use qualification, contrast, understatement, irony, or shifts in tone where they are central and recoverable.
- Allow a phrase to gain fuller meaning from information that appears later.
- Maintain clarity at sentence level even when the overall interpretation is demanding.

#### Structure and cohesion

- Distribute evidence across several paragraphs.
- Use a non-linear element such as a memory, report, comparison, or later re-interpretation, with clear time markers.
- Track several relationships, claims, or perspectives.
- Use paragraphing to signal changes in focus without explicitly explaining every connection.
- For information texts, include qualifications, exceptions, competing effects, or evidence of different strength.
- For narratives, allow what a character says, thinks, and does to differ in revealing ways.

#### Evidence and answer design

- Require students to select relevant evidence from a larger field.
- Allow a plausible surface interpretation, then provide discriminating evidence for a stronger interpretation.
- Build questions about significance, reliability, writer attitude, trade-offs, or change in perspective.
- Accept more than one conclusion only when each can be supported from the text.
- Make the one-mark threshold concise even when the thinking is complex: identify the essential synthesis, not every possible observation.

#### Level 3 mini-example

> The council described the trial bus as “lightly used”, noting that an average of only twelve passengers boarded each morning. The report did not mention that the service left the outer suburb at 10:15, after most workers and secondary students needed to arrive in town. Nor did it compare the route with the earlier service it had replaced, which had often travelled full. Cancelling the trial, the report concluded, would demonstrate responsible use of public money.

Designed opportunities:

- **Reorganisation:** What two timetable facts are needed to compare the trial with the former service?  
  *Details:* the trial left at 10:15; the former, earlier service was often full.
- **Inference:** Why was the trial probably lightly used?  
  *Clues:* departure after work and school arrival times → inconvenient timing.
- **Evaluation:** How does the omitted information affect the council's conclusion?  
  *Synthesis:* it weakens the conclusion because low use may reflect the changed timetable rather than lack of demand.
- **Writer's craft:** Why might the writer put quotation marks around “lightly used”?  
  *Interpretation:* to distance the writer from, or question, the council's description.

The passage supports critical evaluation through stated omissions and comparison. Students do not need external knowledge of local government or transport planning.

#### Avoid at Level 3

- ambiguity with no textual way to resolve it;
- factual density unrelated to the questions;
- adult subject matter presented as a substitute for cognitive complexity;
- obscure idioms whose meanings must already be known;
- a marking guide that recognises only the author's preferred wording;
- questions so broad that almost any thematic statement could pass.

### 7.10 Writing texts for inferencing

An inferencing text deliberately leaves an important meaning unstated while providing enough clues to recover it. Begin by writing the intended literal translation, then remove that statement and replace it with observable evidence.

Example design transformation:

```text
Intended conclusion: Priya was nervous before speaking.
Too explicit: Priya was nervous before speaking.
Under-supported: Priya stood up.
Well supported: Priya smoothed the same note card twice, swallowed, and waited for her name to be called.
```

#### What to embed

- **Setting clues:** objects, sounds, routines, signs, clothing, or procedures.
- **Time clues:** meals, light, schedules, elapsed time, seasonal conditions, or sequence markers.
- **Identity and relationship clues:** forms of address, shared relatives, duties, uniforms, possessions, or dialogue.
- **Emotion clues:** body language, speech patterns, choices, avoidance, attention, or physical reactions.
- **Motive clues:** a goal, obstacle, valued object, prior promise, or anticipated consequence.
- **Cause clues:** a changed condition, preceding action, trace, result, or response.
- **Prediction clues:** an unresolved plan plus conditions that make one outcome likely.
- **Vocabulary clues:** definition, synonym, antonym, example, restatement, or effect.
- **Figurative clues:** literal context that allows a metaphor or idiom to be translated.

#### Inferencing by level

**Level 1**

- Target one concrete inference at a time.
- Keep clues close together and familiar.
- Prefer observable behaviour over abstract commentary.
- Make one conclusion clearly dominant.

Pattern:

> empty bowl + crumbs on face → the child ate the food

**Level 2**

- Require two or three clues across a paragraph.
- Infer motive, relationship, or cause rather than only location.
- Use contextualised figurative language or a corrected first impression.
- Include a plausible distractor that a later clue rules out.

Pattern:

> character denies needing help + repeatedly checks instructions + unfinished task → pride or reluctance to admit difficulty

**Level 3**

- Distribute clues and require selection from non-essential detail.
- Use contrast among speech, action, and narration.
- Infer attitude, reliability, significance, or a chain of causes.
- Allow nuance while providing discriminating evidence.

Pattern:

> official positive claim + omitted comparison + inconvenient conditions → the claim is technically true but misleading

#### Inferencing text audit

For every intended inference, ask:

1. Is the conclusion genuinely unstated?
2. Can the exact clues be underlined?
3. Is the connecting knowledge reasonable for the intended readers?
4. Is another conclusion equally well supported?
5. If so, will it be accepted, or does the text need a discriminating clue?
6. Does another sentence accidentally give the answer away?

### 7.11 Writing texts for reorganisation

A reorganisation text contains all required information explicitly but places related elements where a reader must reconstruct them. Plan the complete answer first, divide it into elements, and place those elements naturally in the text.

Example design transformation:

```text
Complete idea: Rosa feared the move because she might lose her friends and have no garden.
Element 1: Her closest friends lived in the old street.
Element 2: The new flat had no outdoor area.
Placement: Put element 1 in Rosa's conversation and element 2 in the later description of the flat.
```

#### Writing for each subtype

**Joined (`J`)**

- Split a complete relationship across two statements.
- Repeat a linking noun or idea naturally.
- Ensure both elements are needed for the answer.

**Joined with referent (`JR`)**

- Introduce a clear parent noun before using *he*, *she*, *it*, *they*, *this*, or *that*.
- Keep other possible parent nouns grammatically or semantically distinct.
- Test the referent by asking another reader what it replaces.

**Grouped (`G`)**

- Distribute list members through meaningful prose.
- Decide whether order matters.
- State a category that includes all intended items and excludes others.

**Grouped with referent (`GR`)**

- Use pronouns or substitute phrases across the list.
- Make the identity chain recoverable without guesswork.

**Grouped with elimination (`GE`)**

- Include relevant and irrelevant examples within a clear scope.
- Make exclusion depend on reading conditions such as *actually visited*, *bought*, or *completed*, not arbitrary author preference.

**Grouped with calculation (`GC`)**

- State every number, unit, interval, or starting condition needed.
- Keep calculation simple enough that reading remains the primary demand.
- Check inclusivity: whether both start and end points count.
- Avoid requiring unstated conversion knowledge unless it is appropriate and clearly intended.

**Conversation/direct speech**

- Give each speaker a consistent goal, voice, or knowledge state.
- Begin a new paragraph for each turn.
- Make omitted speaker tags recoverable from turn-taking or content.
- Use interruptions and embedded quotations only at a suitable level.

#### Reorganisation by level

**Level 1**

- Join two close, concrete details.
- Use a single clear referent.
- Group a short list of two or three items.
- Keep dialogue to two speakers with regular turns.

**Level 2**

- Separate details across a paragraph or two.
- Use several referents with clear chains.
- Add relevant distractors or simple elapsed-time calculations.
- Use three speakers only when names, roles, or content keep them trackable.

**Level 3**

- Combine grouping, referent resolution, elimination, or calculation.
- Place information in different text forms, such as dialogue plus narration or claim plus table-like facts.
- Use a time shift or change of plan that requires reconstruction.
- Require students to distinguish intended events from imagined, planned, or cancelled events.

#### Reorganisation text audit

Draw a line between every answer element and its location. Then check:

- no element is merely assumed;
- the category and scope are unambiguous;
- pronouns have defensible parent nouns;
- numbers and units are complete;
- the intended sequence can be reconstructed;
- distractors are textually relevant but clearly excludable;
- the question requires reconstruction rather than one-line retrieval.

### 7.12 Writing texts for evaluation

An evaluation text gives readers enough information to interpret importance, intent, effectiveness, plausibility, or limitation. It needs more than a hidden fact: it needs a meaningful relationship that can be judged.

Useful structures include:

- expectation versus outcome;
- claim versus evidence;
- benefit versus limitation;
- appearance versus reality;
- action versus stated value;
- cause versus proposed solution;
- general rule versus exception;
- two accounts of the same event;
- emotionally loaded wording versus neutral facts;
- important information included, omitted, minimised, or emphasised.

#### Constructing an evaluative centre

The **evaluative centre** is the tension or issue worth judging.

Example:

```text
Topic: A self-cleaning house.
Benefits: saves labour; assists people who find housework difficult.
Costs: stark waterproof surfaces; major changes to routines and furnishings.
Evaluative centre: practical assistance versus personal comfort and preference.
```

Without the costs, “Is it a good idea?” has only an obvious answer. Without the benefits, rejection is equally automatic. A balanced evaluative centre makes the student weigh information.

#### Evaluation by level

**Level 1**

- Use a visible cause, consequence, or contrast.
- Keep the criterion concrete: safe, useful, fair, sensible, or surprising.
- Supply one or two strong reasons.
- Ask for a bounded judgement rather than a broad critique.

**Level 2**

- Include several benefits and one meaningful limitation, or the reverse.
- Use a word or phrase whose significance depends on surrounding information.
- Allow a judgement about motive, appeal, effectiveness, or likely impact.
- Require information from more than one paragraph.

**Level 3**

- Present qualified evidence, omissions, competing perspectives, or a gap between wording and reality.
- Use tone, irony, comparison, or selection of detail as evidence.
- Allow students to assess how strongly a conclusion follows.
- Ensure the passage still establishes boundaries for defensible interpretations.

#### Information texts for evaluation

- State the central claim clearly enough to identify it.
- Provide evidence of varying relevance or strength.
- Include sources or attribution when source reliability is being evaluated.
- Distinguish fact, prediction, explanation, and opinion through wording.
- Explain necessary technical concepts inside the passage.
- Use numbers meaningfully; give comparison or scale so students can interpret them.
- Avoid outdated factual claims when the activity is meant to be reusable.

#### Narrative texts for evaluation

- Give characters goals and constraints that make decisions understandable.
- Show traits through patterns of action, not a single convenient gesture.
- Provide consequences or reactions that allow decisions to be judged.
- Use contrast between characters or between a character's belief and later evidence.
- Do not require students to diagnose a character or speculate beyond the text.

#### Evaluation text audit

Ask:

1. What exactly is worth judging?
2. Which criterion will the question use?
3. What evidence supports the expected judgement?
4. What qualification, limitation, or alternative must be considered?
5. Is the judgement possible from the passage alone?
6. Are multiple positions genuinely defensible or merely imaginable?
7. Does the marking guidance reflect that range?

### 7.13 Writing texts for reaction

Reaction questions use evaluation texts but invite the student to adopt a position. Design the passage so that a response can be personal without becoming detached from reading.

#### Supply decision-relevant features

If students will decide whether they would use a walking trail, the passage might state:

- location and accessibility;
- cost;
- difficulty;
- time commitment;
- social or solitary nature;
- safety features;
- environmental experience;
- rules or restrictions.

These are the features a student can use. “I like walking” alone should not be enough.

#### Reaction by level

**Level 1**

- Ask about a concrete choice.
- Provide two or three plainly stated features.
- Require one relevant reason from the text.

**Level 2**

- Include a benefit and a trade-off.
- Ask students to match the proposal to a stated purpose or preference.
- Accept different positions when each uses a relevant detail.

**Level 3**

- Present competing priorities or stakeholders.
- Ask for the most responsible, effective, or justified option under stated constraints.
- Require students to weigh evidence, not merely list it.

#### Balanced does not mean artificially equal

A text can lean towards one conclusion while leaving another defensible. Do not insert implausible disadvantages merely to manufacture balance. Instead, make the reaction criteria specific enough that different readers may reasonably prioritise different real features.

#### Reaction text audit

- Can a student answer using the passage rather than autobiography?
- Are there enough relevant features to justify a position?
- Is at least one alternative position genuinely supportable when the task is open?
- Does the topic avoid pressuring students to disclose private experiences?
- Can the one-mark threshold be explained consistently?

### 7.14 Design different text forms deliberately

#### Narrative

Use narrative when assessing motive, cause, emotion, relationship, prediction, viewpoint, or evaluation of a decision.

Plan:

```text
Normal situation → goal or expectation → obstacle/change → response → consequence or revealing detail
```

Every event should either advance the situation, develop a relationship, or provide evidence. Remove decorative incidents that overload tracking without supporting meaning.

#### Information report

Use a clear organising principle: description, classification, cause and effect, comparison, or problem and solution. Headings can reduce navigation load, but avoid a heading that directly gives away an intended inference.

Plan:

```text
Main idea → explanation/definition → evidence or examples → qualification/contrast → implication
```

#### Explanation or process

Make stages, inputs, conditions, and outcomes clear. Use causal connectors accurately. For reorganisation, distribute a consequence and its cause; for evaluation, include conditions under which the process succeeds or fails.

#### Dialogue

Give each speaker a purpose and ensure turns sound natural. Dialogue is particularly useful for relationships, referents, conflicting viewpoints, and unstated feelings. Do not use dialogue merely to remove speaker labels and create artificial confusion.

#### Mixed text

A notice, message, timetable, caption, or quoted report inside prose can create authentic reorganisation and evaluation. At higher levels, students may combine information across forms. Make layout relationships explicit in the built page and ensure the exported passage retains the information needed for marking.

### 7.15 Control readability without flattening the text

Readability formulas and word counts can flag possible difficulty, but they cannot judge clue quality, cohesion, irony, or conceptual load. Use them as warnings, not automatic level selectors.

During editing:

- read the passage aloud for rhythm and missing words;
- underline each sentence's subject and main action if syntax may be dense;
- circle every pronoun and trace its parent noun;
- mark every time expression on a timeline;
- box technical terms and identify the contextual explanation;
- highlight each question's evidence in a different colour;
- identify sentences that support no main idea or question and decide whether they earn their place;
- check that paragraph breaks are preserved in the generated page;
- ask a fresh reader to paraphrase the passage before showing the questions.

### 7.16 Editorial and accessibility standards for passages

- Use Australian English spelling and punctuation consistently.
- Preserve correct paragraphing, especially for dialogue.
- Use names that are visually and phonetically distinguishable within one text.
- Avoid giving several characters names beginning with the same letter.
- Define who is present before using group pronouns such as *they*.
- Check quotation marks, apostrophes, dashes, degree signs, and special symbols after generation.
- Avoid long blocks of italic, uppercase, or decorative punctuation.
- Do not rely on colour, image position, or font styling as the only carrier of meaning unless the activity explicitly supplies an accessible alternative.
- Spell out uncommon abbreviations on first use.
- Give units with quantities and keep unit formatting consistent.
- Use numerals or words consistently within the same type of information.
- Check factual accuracy, internal dates, travel times, ages, totals, and names.
- Remove transcription artefacts, duplicated fragments, and accidental answer text.
- Keep instructions outside the passage unless a document within the story genuinely contains them.

### 7.17 Passage-writing workflow

#### Pass 1: Define the assessment purpose

Write the skill, level, text form, and intended thinking operations. Do not draft the story until this is clear.

#### Pass 2: Write the answer propositions

Write each intended answer as a plain statement. These propositions become the targets the passage must explicitly state, distribute, or imply.

#### Pass 3: Map the evidence

For every proposition, decide whether its evidence will be:

- explicit and separated for reorganisation;
- implicit but convergent for inferencing;
- contrasted, qualified, or weighed for evaluation; or
- balanced around a decision for reaction.

#### Pass 4: Draft for natural meaning

Write a coherent passage that would make sense without questions. Establish purpose, sequence, and relationships. Do not insert labels such as “this is a clue”.

#### Pass 5: Engineer clue placement

Move, strengthen, weaken, or separate details according to the target level. Add discriminating clues where alternatives remain equally likely. Remove any sentence that directly states an intended inference.

#### Pass 6: Draft and classify questions

Write more questions than needed. Classify each by the actual thinking required. Reject literal questions accidentally labelled as inference, and reject reorganisation questions that use only one fact.

#### Pass 7: Write marking thresholds

Define the core answer, accepted variants, evidence requirement, and rejection boundary. Revise the passage immediately if the threshold cannot be stated fairly.

#### Pass 8: Conduct an adversarial read

Try to produce:

- a different answer using the same clues;
- the intended answer without reading the passage;
- a literal copied answer to a higher-order prompt;
- an answer based on specialist knowledge;
- a response that exploits an ambiguous pronoun or timeline.

Repair every unintended route.

#### Pass 9: Conduct a cold trial

Give the text and questions to a reader who has not seen the design notes. Ask them to answer, underline evidence, identify uncertainty, and explain each reasoning step. Do not dismiss a plausible unexpected answer; either accept it explicitly or revise the item.

#### Pass 10: Verify the built version

Read the generated activity, not only the authoring copy. Layout, encoding, omitted paragraph breaks, or a mismatched response box can change the task students actually receive.

### 7.18 Passage quality checklist

#### Purpose and coherence

- [ ] The text has a clear situation, topic, or communicative purpose.
- [ ] It makes sense without the questions.
- [ ] Every paragraph contributes to meaning, evidence, or necessary context.
- [ ] The title supports orientation without revealing an answer.
- [ ] Narrative actions and informational claims follow logically.

#### Level calibration

- [ ] Difficulty is controlled across language, structure, evidence distance, referents, and answer openness.
- [ ] Complexity comes from comprehension rather than errors or obscurity.
- [ ] The number of characters, times, places, and ideas is manageable for the level.
- [ ] Figurative and technical language has enough context.
- [ ] Passage length is proportionate to its number and depth of questions.

#### Evidence architecture

- [ ] Every answer can be traced to specific passage evidence.
- [ ] Intended inferences are unstated but sufficiently supported.
- [ ] Reorganisation elements are all explicit and naturally distributed.
- [ ] Evaluation has a genuine criterion, contrast, consequence, or tension.
- [ ] Reaction has decision-relevant textual features.
- [ ] Discriminating clues rule out unintended alternatives where necessary.
- [ ] No later sentence accidentally gives away an earlier higher-order answer.

#### Fairness and accessibility

- [ ] Required external knowledge is ordinary and age-appropriate.
- [ ] Specialist concepts are explained in the text.
- [ ] Cultural or personal experience is not used as a hidden prerequisite.
- [ ] The content is respectful and does not demand sensitive disclosure.
- [ ] Pronouns, dialogue, chronology, units, and comparisons are recoverable.

#### Editorial and app readiness

- [ ] Facts, calculations, names, and chronology have been checked.
- [ ] Australian spelling and punctuation are consistent.
- [ ] Paragraphing and dialogue formatting are correct.
- [ ] Encoding artefacts are absent.
- [ ] The generated page preserves every meaning-bearing element.
- [ ] A cold reader has completed the activity and identified the intended evidence.

## 8. Structure and progression of an app handout

Most handouts use a three-part progression:

1. **Quick items** — short sentences or paragraphs, usually one question each.
2. **Short passages** — a compact passage with one or more linked questions.
3. **Extended text** — a longer narrative or information text with a set of questions.

This sequence moves from a single, visible evidence path to sustained comprehension across a complete text. A typical existing handout contains roughly 8–12 one-mark responses, but question count should follow the quality and coverage of the text rather than an arbitrary quota.

### Quick items

Use quick items to isolate one operation. Keep context sufficient but compact. Each item should have its own evidence and should not depend on the previous item.

### Short passages

Use two or three questions that examine different relationships in the same passage. Avoid asking the same inference twice in different words.

### Extended text

Build a purposeful sequence, for example:

1. establish a key relationship or situation;
2. connect separated events;
3. infer a motive, cause, or attitude;
4. interpret a later development or writer choice;
5. evaluate or react to the text as a whole.

Questions should normally follow the order in which evidence appears, especially at Level 1. This reduces memory load while preserving the target comprehension demand.

### Suggested level progression

The app has Levels 1–3 within each skill. Level is determined by the total cognitive and reading load, not passage length alone.

| Feature | Level 1 | Level 2 | Level 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evidence | Obvious, close together, usually concrete | More dispersed or partly indirect | Widely dispersed, subtle, or competing |
| Number of links | One main link | Two or more links | Multiple linked ideas across the text |
| Language | Familiar vocabulary and syntax | Some figurative, technical, or abstract language with context | Nuance, irony, denser syntax, or sustained figurative meaning |
| Referents | Clear and nearby | Several possible nouns but context resolves them | Multiple speakers, perspectives, or chains of reference |
| Answer scope | Narrow and predictable | Some acceptable variation | Requires synthesis and carefully bounded judgement |
| Text structure | Chronological or plainly organised | Some shifts, interruptions, or non-adjacent information | Complex chronology, viewpoint, comparison, or argument |
| Support in prompt | Direct question | Less scaffolding | Independent selection and weighing of evidence |

Increase one or two dimensions at a time. Do not make an item “harder” merely through obscure vocabulary, misleading wording, or missing information.

## 9. Prompt-writing standards

### Use clear, economical wording

- Match vocabulary and syntax to the intended level.
- Ask one assessable thing at a time, unless the second clause explicitly requests evidence.
- Name the relevant character or object when a pronoun in the question could be ambiguous.
- Use the same term as the passage unless paraphrasing is part of the intended challenge.
- Put the question mark at the end and remove author notes from student-facing wording.
- Use Australian spelling throughout: *reorganisation*, *favourite*, *centre*, *realised*.

### Prefer prompts that reveal reasoning

| Avoid | Prefer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Was Wade proud? | Was Wade proud of the piglet? How do you know? | Requires evidence, not guessing |
| What happened? | What most likely happened to the bottle? | Defines the inference target |
| Explain the story. | Why did Black Bob decide to leave the pirates? | Sets a bounded response |
| What do you think? | What does the description suggest about the writer's attitude? Use evidence. | Anchors judgement in text |
| List the details. | What three reasons did Una have for choosing the gift? | Defines scope and completeness |

### Closed questions

Whenever a prompt permits *yes* or *no*, add one of:

- “How do you know?”
- “Why do you say that?”
- “Which details support your answer?”
- “Use evidence from the text.”

For the app's single response box, keep the position and justification in the same prompt and mark them together as one response.

### Avoid accidental ambiguity

Check:

- pronouns with more than one possible referent;
- questions such as “Why did he do it?” when several actions are nearby;
- *when* questions that could mean time of day, date, or position in a sequence;
- *where* questions that could reasonably accept a broad or narrow location;
- *why* questions with several causes when the mark expects only one;
- quantities that depend on whether repeated people, places, or objects are counted twice;
- vocabulary with multiple relevant meanings.

If multiple answers are intended, say so: “Give one reason…”, “Give two details…”, or “Which answer is most likely? Explain.”

## 10. Writing answers and marking guidance

The student interface uses free-text response boxes. Current activities normally allocate **one mark per response**, so every question needs a crisp threshold for a complete answer.

For each question, prepare:

1. **Core answer** — the minimum idea needed for the mark.
2. **Evidence path** — the clue or combination of clues that proves comprehension.
3. **Acceptable variants** — synonyms, concise forms, and defensible alternatives.
4. **Rejection boundary** — common vague, literal-only, contradictory, or off-target responses.

Example:

> **Prompt:** What time of day was it likely to be?  
> **Passage clue:** It was time to turn the lights on.  
> **Core answer:** It was evening or getting dark.  
> **Accept:** evening, dusk, nightfall, late afternoon, sunset, getting dark.  
> **Reject:** morning, midday, “light time,” or an answer that does not address time.

### Marking closed questions

If the prompt asks for a position and evidence, the expected response contains both.

> **Prompt:** Did the officer believe their story at first? How do you know?  
> **Complete:** No. He viewed them with suspicion and barely listened to their explanation.  
> **Incomplete:** No.  
> **Incomplete:** He had a gun.  

Do not make correctness depend on a ceremonial word if the response unambiguously communicates the position and supporting evidence. For example, “He was suspicious and did not listen to them” clearly conveys *no* even if the first word is omitted.

### Marking grouped answers

State how many elements are required for the single mark. If all three details are essential, say so. If any two demonstrate the skill, define that threshold. Do not decide after seeing student responses.

### Marking evaluation and reaction

Mark the quality of the text-based justification, not whether the marker shares the student's opinion. A different position should receive the mark when it is coherent and uses relevant evidence.

### Generated marking guides

The build process creates a marking-guide JSON file containing the prompt, passage, question ID, section ID, order, maximum mark, and a generic guide. Generic wording is only a starting point. For dependable assessment, refine the generated `markingGuide` into question-specific acceptance and rejection criteria, as demonstrated in the existing Inferencing Level 1 guides.

If the source activity is rebuilt later, verify that customised marking guidance has not been replaced by generic generated text.

## 12. End-to-end authoring workflow

### Step 1: Select the skill and level

Decide the intended comprehension process and use the progression table to choose a level. Review at least two existing handouts at that skill and level for calibration.

### Step 2: Analyse the text

Annotate:

- explicit facts;
- separated related facts;
- pronouns and their parent nouns;
- clues that imply unstated information;
- cause-and-effect links;
- shifts in time, speaker, perspective, or attitude;
- figurative language and significant word choices;
- details that could support more than one judgement.

### Step 3: Build an evidence map

For each proposed question, record:

| Question | Skill/subtype | Required evidence | Expected answer | Accept/reject boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Why did Mia cover the book? | Inference: motive | raincoat + thunder + book | To keep it dry from expected rain | Accept protection-from-rain equivalents; reject “she was cold” |

Delete or revise questions with duplicated evidence paths.

### Step 4: Draft in progression

Begin with direct evidence paths and progress towards synthesis. Across a handout, vary the target: setting, identity, cause, motive, referent, sequence, attitude, significance, or judgement as appropriate to the chosen skill.

### Step 5: Write the answer criteria immediately

Do not postpone the answer key. Difficulty writing a fair marking rule usually reveals an ambiguous question.

### Step 6: Conduct a cold read

Ask a reviewer who has not seen the intended answers to complete the set and underline the evidence used. Investigate every unexpected but defensible response.

### Step 7: Integrate with the current build

Place the approved content in the handout data used by the build, or update the pipeline so it reads the maintained source file. Do not run `build_all.py` on the assumption that it consumes an edited file under `content/`; it currently writes those files.

Run the project's build process only after the content has been integrated at the true input point. Inspect the generated student activity, Markdown copy, and marking guide. Because the current build compiles the activity collection and can replace customised guidance, also check that unrelated handouts and marking guides were not unintentionally changed.

### Step 8: Test the student experience

In the browser, verify:

- title, skill, level, and handout number;
- passage paragraphing and dialogue;
- question order and section navigation;
- each response box maps to the correct prompt;
- completion totals equal the visible number of questions;
- apostrophes, quotation marks, dashes, degrees, and other symbols render correctly;
- exported responses contain the correct activity, section, question, prompt, and passage.

### Step 9: Finalise marking guidance

Replace generic guidance with question-specific criteria. Confirm the total marks, question IDs, and section IDs match the built activity.

## 13. Quality-assurance checklist

### Skill validity

- [ ] Every question is classified by the thinking it requires.
- [ ] Inferencing answers are implied, not directly copied.
- [ ] Reorganisation questions require at least two explicit elements.
- [ ] Evaluation questions require synthesis and a text-supported judgement.
- [ ] Reaction questions allow justified positions and remain anchored to the text.
- [ ] Literal questions are limited and purposeful.

### Evidence and fairness

- [ ] Each intended answer has sufficient textual evidence.
- [ ] Required background knowledge is ordinary, age-appropriate, and not the real test.
- [ ] No item depends on guessing the author's private intention.
- [ ] Alternative defensible answers are identified.
- [ ] Distractors arise naturally and do not make the text misleading.

### Prompt quality

- [ ] Wording is concise, grammatical, age-appropriate, and unambiguous.
- [ ] Yes/no questions also ask for evidence.
- [ ] Pronouns in the prompt have clear referents.
- [ ] The required number of reasons, details, or examples is stated.
- [ ] Questions do not repeat the same demand in different words.
- [ ] Question order supports comprehension without giving away later answers.

### Marking quality

- [ ] Every question has a core answer and evidence path.
- [ ] Acceptance criteria include reasonable wording variants.
- [ ] Rejection criteria identify likely vague or off-target answers.
- [ ] The threshold is realistic for a one-mark response.
- [ ] Position-plus-evidence prompts are marked for both components.
- [ ] Evaluation and reaction are marked by evidence, not marker preference.

## 14. Common failure modes and repairs

### The “inference” is explicitly stated

**Problem:** The student only has to copy a sentence.  
**Repair:** Ask about an unstated cause, setting, relationship, motive, or implication supported by the details.

### The question is answerable without reading

**Problem:** “Are parks good?” invites general opinion.  
**Repair:** “Would the particular walking trails described meet the needs of someone seeking a competitive challenge? Why?”

### The passage does not determine the answer

**Problem:** A character smiles, and the only accepted emotion is *happy*, even though the context could support relief or politeness.  
**Repair:** Add discriminating clues or accept all supported interpretations.

### The wording supplies the answer

**Problem:** “Why did the frightened dog hide?” already labels the emotion.  
**Repair:** “What was the dog probably feeling? Which action suggests this?”

### A yes/no answer earns a mark without comprehension

**Problem:** “Was the trip difficult?”  
**Repair:** “Did the family regard the trip as too difficult? How do you know?”

### Reorganisation becomes a memory dump

**Problem:** “Tell everything that happened.”  
**Repair:** Define the relationship and scope: “What three actions did the family take before reaching the lions?”

### Evaluation becomes unsupported opinion

**Problem:** “Did you like the character?”  
**Repair:** “Was the character's decision responsible in this situation? Use two details from the text.”

### Difficulty comes from poor editing

**Problem:** Obscure words, broken punctuation, missing context, and ambiguous pronouns make the item hard for the wrong reason.  
**Repair:** Fix the prose and preserve only the intended comprehension challenge.

### The marking guide repeats the prompt

**Problem:** “Accept answers showing comprehension of the question.”  
**Repair:** State the core concept, accepted variants, required evidence, and rejection boundary.

## 16. Digital Reading Layout Standards

To optimize student comprehension and focus during digital assessment, the web application employs four content-aware, screen-split layouts. Authors should specify the appropriate layout based on the text structure and length:

### 1. Sentence Task List (`sentence-task-list`)
* **Use case:** Short, item-scoped sentence clues (e.g., Quick Inferences).
* **Layout:** A vertical stack of independent task cards. Each card displays the single sentence on the left and the open-ended text input on the right (50/50 side-by-side split on desktop). On mobile, it collapses to a stacked single column (reading on top, input on bottom).

### 2. Paired Passage List (`paired-passage-list`)
* **Use case:** Medium-length paragraphs where each paragraph has its own small set of associated questions (typically 1–2 questions).
* **Layout:** A vertical list of task cards. Each card displays the paragraph on the left and the associated questions on the right (50/50 split on desktop).

### 3. Focus Passage Layout (`focus-passage-list`)
* **Use case:** Single, focused passages associated with multiple questions.
* **Layout:** A carousel-style navigation. Only one question is displayed at a time alongside the passage (side-by-side). A sticky toolbar at the bottom provides "Prev", "Task X of Y", and "Next" buttons to allow step-by-step progress, minimizing visual noise.

### 4. Shared Passage Workspace (`shared-passage`)
* **Use case:** Long texts or stories associated with a vertical list of multiple comprehension questions.
* **Layout:** A split-screen layout. The left column contains the full scrollable passage, and the right column contains the scrollable list of all questions.
* **Reading Focus Mode:** A "Reading focus" toggle allows students to collapse the questions column and expand the reading panel to 100% width for undisturbed reading.

### Core Student Features
* **Auto-Save:** All responses are automatically saved locally on keyup, displaying a subtle green "Saved" badge next to each question.
* **Focus View:** A global "Focus view" mode collapses the sidebar and page headers to maximize screen real estate, letting the student focus entirely on reading and answering.
* **Print Optimization:** The print CSS forces a light theme, removes sidebars, navigation panels, and scrollbars, and replaces the interactive textarea fields with print-friendly dotted lines representing handwriting boundaries.
