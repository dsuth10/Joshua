KEY into inference LEVEL ONE SET S1

SENTENCES when

With these questions you are looking for KEY words that tell you -

when something happened, how often things happened.

 

MODEL

SENTENCE:

It was still quite dark when Uncle Harry woke Paula to go fishing.

 

QUESTION:

When did Uncle Harry wake Paula?

 

ANSWER:

In the early morning.

 

KEY WORDS:

quite dark - tells you it is not completely dark; it is starting to become light

still - tells you that it had been dark for some time before

woke - tells you that Paula had been sleeping

 

PRACTISE

ITEM 7

JUMBO

Language is constantly changing. Words are invented, meanings are changed and some words are lost.

 

Mumbo-jumbo is thought to be an African term for a witch doctor who carried out seemingly strange ceremonies. In English, mumbo-jumbo took on the meaning for something that is either mysterious or meaningless.

 

In 1884 a circus owner named P.T. Barnum bought an enormous male elephant from London Zoo. He named the elephant Jumbo and shipped it to America to take it on tour. It was the largest known elephant in captivity. Barnum\'s advertising posters showed a huge elephant with the name Jumbo written in large letters.

 

Soon the word jumbo was being added to anything that was especially large. There were jumbo meals, jumbo fruits - the new word could be added to any product. In more modern times we got the \'jumbo jet\'. This was the nickname given to the Boeing 747 aircraft - a plane with a large, distinctive hump. This plane could carry more passengers than any other.

 

For about a year Jumbo was exhibited across America. Then, one night as he was being led across some railway tracks to board the train which would take him to his next destination, another train arrived unexpectedly and ran straight into him. Neither he nor the train survived the collision, but the word jumbo continues to be part of the English language .

c\. How did the word jumbo come to be used for anything that was especially large?

d\. Why is the Boeing 7 47 called a Jumbo jet\'?

e\. What happened to the train that hit Jumbo?

VAMPIRE BATS

ITEM 10

GLOBAL WARMING

Earth has a very fragile environment. The atmosphere which surrounds our planet holds enough of the sun\'s heat to allow life to survive. The sun heats up the oceans and the land and some of this heat then escapes back to space.

 

The atmosphere prevents the escape of too much of the precious heat. Without this protection the average surface temperature of Earth would be lowered by 35 degrees to minus 20 degrees celsius.

 

For about two centuries, increasing amounts of gases have been released into the air in alarming amounts. The gases most responsible for this are called chloroflourocarbons (CFC\'s) that are used in refrigeration and aerosol spray cans, and carbon dioxide (C02) which comes from burning fossil fuels such as coal and oil.

 

These gases have built up in the atmosphere causing more heat to be trapped; this is called global warming.

 

If Earth\'s temperature continues to increase the ice caps at the North and South Poles will melt. Major population centres and large areas of farmland in lower lying regions of the world would disappear.

e\. Why would some of the major population centres disappear if global warming continues?

ICE SKATING

For centuries, in the colder countries of Europe, Asia and North America, ice skating has been a winter sport. At first it relied on natural ice, but the development of refrigeration changed the sport. Once people were able to skate indoors on artificial ice, it meant that they could skate all year round.

 

Skaters compete for World and Olympic Championship honours in two types of ice skating. Speed skating is held on an oval circuit and is a race over set distances. The aim is to get to the finish line first.

 

Figure skating has a different approach. In figure skating, there are two divisions: compulsory skating and free skating. In the compulsory section, solo skaters must make a number of set patterns on the ice. Free skating is a four or five minute individual performance. Skaters are allowed to make up their own routines for this competition.

 

Dance skating is similar to figure skating, with the main difference being that both feet are not allowed to leave the ice at the same time.

 

Ice skating became a highly popular spectator sport in the late twentieth century. Pairs of skaters, in dazzling costumes, performed spectacular and highly developed dance routines to television audiences around the world.

AYERS ROCK - ULURU

 

Almost at the very centre of Australia, rising abruptly from the flat arid plains of the Northern Territory, is Ayers Rock, or Uluru in the ancient language of the Anangu Aborigines. Wind blown sands have sculpted this huge landmark into a dome shape that has many crevices, caves and cavities weathered into strange shapes. Uluru is a national symbol, recognised internationally.

 

For the Anangu people it is a place of reverence around which their lives have centred for thousands of generations and is part of their code of living. Every part of Uluru is known to them and has some significance. Some caves are decorated with Aboriginal paintings in ochre, ash and charcoal.

 

Although it is known as a rock, it is not, in fact, just a rock; it is a sandstone hill that was pushed up by earth movements about 500 million years ago. About 70 million years ago it was an island in a large lake. Most of Uluru is submerged in the surrounding sand; only its flattened tip is exposed.

 

Rainfall is rare in the centre of Australia, but when it occurs, it can be extremely heavy. After a downpour, water cascades down the flanks of the hill, leaving them streaked with black. While most of the rain runs off onto the plain below, sustaining the plant life that exists there, water-holes build up in rock crevices. Some of the water-holes last for weeks or even months, but most evaporate rapidly in the heat. One large pool, however, has water in it in all but the very driest periods.

 

There are water snakes living in these pools. In Anangu mythology they are regarded as being the pools\' guardians. Frogs, lizards, marsupial moles and hopping mice living among the sand dunes are easy prey for the highly venomous brown snakes that live in the region.

GEOTHERMAL

Underground water heated to extreme temperatures occurs in nature in few places in the world. It is heat from the ground that makes this possible. The Ancient Greek word for earth is geo, and the word for heat is therme. Geothermal conditions only occur in places where there is volcanic activity. Geothermal energy is used to generate electricity in places around the rim of the Pacific Ocean such as Japan, New Zealand and California.

 

Throughout.1he world, electricity is generally produced by turbines and machinery being rotated and driven by the force of steam or water.· Where there are strongly flowing rivers, such as in the South lsland of New Zealand, the water is used to do just this in a process known as hydroelectricity.

 

More commonly, the turbines are driven by steam that is produced by using nuclear power or by the burning of fossil fuels, such as oil or coal. But where geothermal conditions allow, it is more desirable not to generate electricity in these ways. Nuclear power stations carry the risk of radiation leaks and the burning of fossil fuels is a major source of atmospheric pollution.

 

In New Zealand the production of geothermal power is restricted to Wairakei, in the volcanically active central North Island, where bores are drilled down to the reservoirs of scalding water. Once on the surface, the steam from this water is used, under pressure, to turn the turbines to create electricity.

ITEM 12

BACTERIA

To many people, bacteria and germs are synonymous. While it is true that there are harmful bacteria which cause disease, it is also true that there are bacteria which are useful to us.

 

A good example of useful bacteria is when they act as decomposers. This happens on a compost heap or when a living organism dies. Where there is dead matter, there will also be small creatures such as ants, beetles and earthworms. They digest and excrete the material that they feed on, but they don\'t really change it. On any decaying matter, there are microscopic bacteria which break it down completely.

 

Most of this work is done by aerobic bacteria; a type of bacteria that needs air to exist. They not only eat the dead matter left by the worms and ants, they change it into simple chemicals that become nutrition for plants. If the decaying matter compacts into a more liquid mass, the aerobic bacteria can no longer survive there and the anaerobic bacteria take over.

 

Bacteria reproduce by dividing themselves. Each bacterium takes about twenty minutes, in the right conditions, to split into two. Each of these will divide again in another twenty minutes. This goes on endlessly. Sometimes bacteria will form colonies with millions of members. When this happens, the colony is visible as a small feathery shape.

 

The human body has a natural ability to resist the effects of harmful bacteria. However, some of the more virulent varieties, such as those that cause tetanus and tuberculosis, have been harder to resist. Human intervention with antiseptics and drugs has effectively reduced the number of serious illnesses and deaths.
