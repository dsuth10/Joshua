  ROW 0: Source 1: The Dayak peoples 41497100Sadalmelik, Borneo locator topography http://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Borneo_Locator_Topography.png CC BY-SA 3.0 creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/deed.en 00Sadalmelik, Borneo locator topography http://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Borneo_Locator_Topography.png CC BY-SA 3.0 creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/deed.en People and place The Dayak peoples are indigenous groups who live on the island of Borneo (South-East Asia). Borneo includes areas of Indonesia, Malaysia and Brunei. There are many ethnic groups amongst the Dayak peoples, with about 140 different languages and dialects. For thousands of years, Dayak peoples have relied on the rainforest for their resources. They adapted to living in hot, wet, equatorial climates. The temperatures are high all year round and they get heavy rain. Food The Dayak life centres on the rice paddy planting every year, and they move their farms each year to allow the forest to regenerate. Other than rice, they grow many vegetables, They catch fish and hunt animals using spears. 3024048212268Tropenmuseum , part of the National Museum of World Cultures, Netherlands,  A Dayak longhouse, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:COLLECTIE_TROPENMUSEUM_Een_Dajak_langhuis_TMnr_60031796.jpg CC BY-SA 3.0 creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/00Tropenmuseum , part of the National Museum of World Cultures, Netherlands,  A Dayak longhouse, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:COLLECTIE_TROPENMUSEUM_Een_Dajak_langhuis_TMnr_60031796.jpg CC BY-SA 3.0 creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/Culture Traditionally, Dayak peoples lived along the banks of the larger rivers in longhouse communities. The whole village live in one longhouse. Dance and ceremony is an important part of religious ceremonies. The men traditionally had elaborate tattoos running up their shoulders. Women wore many brass rings around their arms or waist, and as earrings.   ROW 0: Source 2: The Pitjantjatjara people People and place The Pitjantjatjara (pronounced as pit-jan-jah-jarra) are an Aboriginal people of the Central Australian desert. They speak a dialect of the Pama-Nyuangan language. These people consider Uluru and Kata Tjuta important sacred sites. The landscape is mostly flat, sandy country covered by low scrub and sand dunes.
  ROW 1:  [IMG: media/image15.png] | Uluru, http://pixabay.com/en/uluru-ayers-rock-australia-83961/Uluru, http://pixabay.com/en/uluru-ayers-rock-australia-83961/ Food The Pitjantjatjara people hunt bush animals such as the red kangaroo, bush turkey, emu and the sand goanna. They also gather insects like the honey ant, and bush fruits and vegetables such as the bush plum and bush tomato. The people use fire skilfully to manage the landscape for plant growth and attracting animals for food. Water is very scarce in these desert lands, so the people carefully maintain wells and rock holes to trap rainwater. [IMG: media/image16.jpeg, media/image17.jpeg]
  ROW 2: Culture The Pitjantjatjara people pass on their knowledge and beliefs by teaching their children about their heritage through their art, stories, songs, dances and ceremonies. Through their crafts and other artworks, the Elders teach the young people of their community about important cultural traditions and stories. The land is an important part of their identity, and every part of it is rich with stories and meaning.
