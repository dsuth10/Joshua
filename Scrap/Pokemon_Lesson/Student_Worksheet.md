## 📖 Reading Passage
### From Game Boy to Nintendo Switch: The Language of Pocket Monsters

In 1996, a Japanese video game designer named Satoshi Tajiri created a franchise that would change the world: **Pocket Monsters**. When it was brought to Australia and the West in 1998, it was rebranded as **Pokémon**. Today, it is the highest-grossing media franchise in human history, spanning video games, trading cards, television series, films, and toys. 

However, the Pokémon we play today is vastly different from the original version. The first games, *Pokémon Red* and *Pokémon Blue*, were played on the original Nintendo Game Boy. This handheld console was extremely simple compared to today's devices. Its screen was **monochromatic** (meaning it could only display shades of grey-green, with no true colours) and its sound was limited to simple electronic beeps. 

More importantly, the game cartridges had severe storage limits. A single game cartridge could only hold **512 kilobytes** of data. To put this in perspective, a single photo taken on a smartphone today is usually around 3,000 kilobytes! Because memory space was incredibly scarce, the game developers had to make compromises. 

This technological limit directly shaped the language of the game. The descriptions of the creatures, known as **Pokédex entries**, had to be extremely brief. Writers could not write long, flowing paragraphs. Instead, they had to choose vocabulary with immense precision, using concise, descriptive words and short sentence structures to convey details about a Pokémon's behaviour, habitats, and powers.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------+
|                       1996 POKÉDEX ENTRY                        |
|                                                                 |
| "A timid, fire-breathing reptile. It raises its temperature    |
|  by burning its tail. It flees if it is startled."             |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Another challenge was **localisation**. Since the game was designed in Japan, all the text, storyline dialogues, and creature names were originally written in Japanese. The Japanese name for Pikachu's pre-evolution, Pichu, stayed the same, but others had to change. For example, the turtle-like Pokémon *Zenigame* was renamed *Squirtle* (a blend of "squirt" and "turtle") to make sense to English-speaking children. Localisation is the process of translating and adapting a text so that it fits the language, culture, and expectations of a specific local audience.

Today, modern Nintendo Switch consoles can hold over **32 gigabytes** of data on a single game card—more than 60,000 times the capacity of an early Game Boy cartridge! With this massive technical freedom, Pokémon has become a fully **multimodal** experience. Players do not just read brief text blocks; they observe 3D characters moving dynamically through open worlds, listen to cinematic orchestral scores, and interact with complex touchscreen menus. The language of modern Pokémon has expanded, yet it still relies on the same core linguistic device invented in the 1990s: creating vivid imagery using highly precise, imaginative descriptions.

## 🧪 Part 3: The Grammar Lab (Expanded Noun Groups)
An **expanded noun group** is a group of words built around a central noun (the "head noun"). It provides details about the noun by placing **adjectives** before it, and/or **qualifying prepositional phrases** after it.

```
       [Adjectives]         [Head Noun]      [Qualifying Prepositional Phrase]
  "A fierce, fire-breathing    dragon      with razor-sharp claws on its wings."
```
