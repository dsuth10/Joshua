What Causes Earthquakes?

Earth is not as solid as it feels under our feet. The crust (the hard, rocky outer layer of our planet) is actually broken into huge pieces. These pieces are called tectonic plates (giant puzzle pieces that make up Earth's outer shell). Tectonic plates float very slowly. They ride on top of a softer layer of hot rock deep inside the earth.

As they float, these giant plates bump into each other. They also pull apart or slide past one another. The edges of the plates can be rough and jagged. Sometimes, the plates get stuck together. They stick because of friction, but they do not stop moving. The rest of the plate keeps pushing. This build-up of force creates tension (growing pressure that is stored in the rocks).

When the pressure becomes too great, the rocks suddenly break or slip. This sudden movement usually happens along a fault (a crack in the Earth's crust where rocks can move). When the rocks slip, they release a massive amount of stored energy. 

This energy travels outward in all directions. It moves as seismic waves (powerful ripples of energy that travel through the ground). These waves make the ground shake. This shaking is what we feel as an earthquake. 

The point deep underground where the rocks first broke is called the focus. Directly above this point is the epicentre (the point on the surface directly above where the earthquake started). The shaking is always strongest near the epicentre.

Scientists measure earthquakes using a seismograph (a special machine that measures the strength of ground movements). By studying earthquakes, we learn how tectonic plates move. We also learn how to keep our buildings safe.
